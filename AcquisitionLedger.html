01 Why Buy Instead of Build

Core idea. We're taught that the road to wealth is starting something from nothing — a startup, an app, a brand. But look at the richest people on earth and a strange pattern appears: not one of them got there through founding alone. Amazon has made over 110 acquisitions. Google has made roughly 200. Scan the Forbes 100 list and you won't find a single person who hasn't bought at least one company at some point in their climb. That's not a coincidence; it's a strategy. Acquisition entrepreneurship means buying an already-operating, already-profitable business instead of building one from zero. You inherit customers, systems, cash flow, and a track record on day one — the exact things a startup spends its first five years (and most of its luck) trying to create. The numbers back this up hard: about 70% of U.S. millionaires own a business, and 88% of people worth $30 million or more have done an acquisition. Meanwhile, roughly 90% of startups fail, but about 80% of acquisitions survive their first year, and acquisitions financed through SBA loans or seller financing have a failure rate around just 13%. That gap in survival odds is the entire argument. This doesn't mean founding is worthless — it means that if your primary goal is wealth with a high probability of actually keeping it, buying an existing, proven cash-flow machine is the statistically smarter play, and almost nobody teaches you how to actually do it.

Step-by-Step: The Mental Shift

  1. Stop treating "start a business" and "get a business" as the same idea — they have wildly different risk profiles.
  2. Accept that proven cash flow beats a promising idea, every time, for a first deal.
  3. Recognize that ordinary-looking, boring businesses are where this game is actually won.
  4. Realize capital isn't the real bottleneck — recognizing opportunity is (more on this in Module 6).
Typical

Amazon's acquisition machine

Amazon didn't build Whole Foods, Zappos, Ring, or MGM from scratch — it bought proven operators in categories it wanted to dominate, instantly acquiring their customers and infrastructure instead of the years it would take to build a competitor from nothing.

Counter-intuitive

The boring sign shop beats the hot startup

A 12-year-old sign manufacturing business making steady profit is a safer wealth vehicle than a trendy AI startup, purely because it already has a track record — while the startup carries the same 90% failure odds as any other new venture, no matter how exciting it looks.

Edge Case

Not every acquisition is "safe"

Buying an unprofitable business on the hope you can "turn it around," or financing 100% through a bank with no seller involvement, reintroduces startup-level risk. The safety comes specifically from buying profitable businesses, often with the seller sharing in the financing — remove either piece and the odds get worse.

02 The Seven D's — Why Sellers Sell

Core idea. The average small business owner in the U.S. is 67 years old. Many have run their business for a decade or more, and running a business for that long tends to wear a person down in one of a handful of predictable ways. Finance people who study seller motivation boil it down to seven triggers, all conveniently starting with "D": Departure (they want to move, often to retire somewhere warmer), Divorce (assets need to be split and the business becomes part of the settlement), Disease (a health scare forces them to prioritize family or treatment over the business), Disagreement (a partnership has soured and someone needs to exit), Distress (the business is struggling financially and can be picked up cheaply), Death (the owner has passed and the business is sitting in probate, often disappearing entirely if nobody buys it fast), and Dullness (nothing is wrong at all — the owner is simply bored and craving a new challenge). Understanding these seven triggers matters enormously because it shifts your search from "which businesses are for sale" to "which owners are quietly ready to be done," which is a much larger and far less competitive pool. It also reframes the ethics of the whole exercise: buying from a motivated seller isn't exploitation, it's giving a neglected or overlooked asset a new life — a line borrowed from billionaire investor Sam Zell, who built much of his fortune finding value other people had stopped seeing.

Step-by-Step: Spotting a "D" in the Wild

  1. Notice life-stage signals: age, health mentions, talk of relocating or retiring.
  2. Ask directly (politely) — most owners will tell you if you ask a real question, not a sales pitch.
  3. Match the "D" to urgency: Death and Disease need speed; Dullness and Departure can move at a normal pace.
  4. Remember: the "D" tells you their real priority (speed, trust, legacy) — which matters more than price.

03 Seller Financing — Becoming the Bank

Core idea. Normally, to buy anything expensive, you either pay cash or borrow from a bank, which charges interest and takes months to underwrite. Seller financing simply removes the bank from the picture: the seller takes on the role of lender, letting the buyer pay some money down and the rest over time out of the business's future profits. The surprising part is why a seller would ever agree to this — and it comes down to three concrete benefits. First, more total cash: a bank might only appraise a business at, say, $750,000 against a $1 million asking price, forcing a lower sale price. A buyer offering seller financing can instead offer $1.15 million, paid over time with interest, netting the seller far more money in total than a lower cash lump sum ever would. Second, lower taxes: a lump-sum sale triggers a big tax bill immediately; spreading payments over 5–10 years often drops the seller into a lower tax bracket for those years, saving them real money annually. Third, speed: an SBA or bank loan can take three to nine months to close, during which deals frequently fall apart — "time kills all deals." A seller-financed deal can often close in one to three weeks. Put together, seller financing isn't a favor sellers do reluctantly — it's frequently the financially smarter choice for them, which is exactly why buyers with limited cash can still win these deals if they present themselves as trustworthy and fast-moving.

Step-by-Step: Pitching Seller Financing

  1. Find out what the seller actually wants for the business, and what a bank would realistically appraise it at.
  2. Offer a higher total price than the bank route, paid over time with interest.
  3. Show how spreading payments reduces their tax bill versus one lump sum.
  4. Emphasize speed — weeks instead of months — and demonstrate you're a serious, trustworthy buyer.

04 The Deal Box & Seller Avatar

Core idea. If you go looking for "any business, anywhere, for some amount of money," you'll never buy anything — this is called the spray-and-pray method, and it fails because you can't recognize a good deal if you haven't defined what "good" means. The fix is building a deal box: a specific, written set of parameters covering price range, required income, how much financing you'll need, location, industry, and whether you want to operate the business yourself or just invest in it. Alongside the deal box sits the idea of a seller financing "avatar" — a checklist of traits that make a seller more or less likely to finance you. A good avatar looks like: an older owner, tired, running a simple/boring/brick-and-mortar business worth under $1 million in profit, with low competition, messy or unbankable financials, no successor lined up, and no crowd of buyers already bidding. A bad avatar looks like the opposite: young, fast-growing, "sexy," heavily competed-for, business with clean books and dozens of offers already on the table. A real example: Heather and Chris built a deal box requiring $120,000/year in replaceable income, no traditional bank loan, a recession-resistant business in San Diego, no license requirements, and a small team. They found a 12-year-old sign manufacturing business doing $170,000 in seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) on $278,000 revenue, priced reasonably at $275,000 with seller financing offered — it hit nearly every box, so they moved forward with confidence instead of guessing.

Step-by-Step: Building Your Deal Box

  1. Set your numbers: minimum income needed, operator salary (if any), max financing, target debt service.
  2. Set your must-haves and can't-stands (location, remote vs. in-person, industry exclusions).
  3. Score each real business you find against your seller-avatar checklist below.
  4. Only pursue businesses that clear both the deal box and the avatar bar.

05 On-Market Sourcing

Core idea. On-market deals are businesses already listed for sale — the owner knows they want out, the listing is usually broker-represented, and (in theory) some basic financials are available, much like browsing Zillow for a house that's staged and ready to show. This is the fastest way for a beginner to get real "reps": you can filter by price, location, and industry, see estimated cash flow and a sales multiple, and message a broker directly. The tradeoff is competition — an average business listing receives over 100 outreach messages, so brokers screen hard for buyers who look serious. The winning approach is "be good, be brief, be gone": a short, professional message that states your criteria, shows financial capability (proof of funds, a verified identity, a completed buyer profile), and signals you can move fast. It's also critical to understand who a broker actually works for: not you. They represent the seller and get paid on the seller's side, which is exactly why hiring a buy-side broker for a small deal is usually a bad idea — most brokers "go up-market" and either charge five-figure monthly retainers or a percentage of the deal, pricing out first-time buyers. Instead, buyers use marketplaces built specifically for small business acquisition (comparable to Zillow, but for companies) — filtering listings against their saved deal box, setting alerts so they see new matches instantly, and reaching out themselves. Speed matters enormously here: the buyer who responds first and looks most credible usually wins the deal, not the buyer with the biggest offer.

Step-by-Step: On-Market Outreach

  1. Lock your deal box, then filter listings against it (price, location, industry, revenue).
  2. Set daily alerts — the fastest responder usually wins the deal.
  3. Send a short, professional message stating your criteria and typical closing timeline.
  4. Verify your identity and show proof of funds to boost response rates.
  5. Move through the funnel: NDA → financials → letter of intent → due diligence → close.

Pros: Visible financials, faster initial process, transparent asking price, easier comparison shopping.

Cons: More competition (100+ inquiries per listing), harder to negotiate creative terms like seller financing, prices reflect full market value.

Typical

Filtered marketplace search

Searching "laundromats, Austin, under $2M" on a business marketplace returns a filtered shortlist you can message directly — the small-business equivalent of Zillow.

Counter-intuitive

Paying more can be smarter

Sometimes an on-market deal costs slightly more than an off-market one — but because it closes faster and with more certainty, it can be the better choice. "Time kills all deals."

Edge Case

The empty listing

A listing with no financials attached (just a name and price) can't be evaluated yet — the right move isn't to judge it, but to send a low-friction message purely to request financials before making any decision.

❌ What People Get Wrong: Assuming a business broker will "find you a business" the way a real estate agent finds you a home. Brokers work for sellers, not buyers — and buy-side representation is typically expensive and not built for small first-time buyers.
Why This Matters: On-market listings are the ideal training ground — low-stakes practice reading numbers and writing outreach — before tackling the much larger, less competitive off-market world.

06 Off-Market Sourcing & The Deal Funnel

Core idea. There are roughly 11 million small businesses publicly listed for sale in the U.S. at any given time — but an estimated 20 to 40 million more are theoretically sellable and simply haven't realized it yet. This is the "Quiet Seller Methodology": instead of waiting for deals to appear, you generate your own proprietary pipeline through direct outreach. Five approaches work well: strategic targeting (a sniper-rifle focus on one specific business type and area), direct outreach (talking to owners at chambers of commerce or industry events), third-party deal sourcers, technology-driven aggregation (scraping off-market business data), and personal curation — reviewing your own Venmo/PayPal history or credit card statements to find small businesses you already spend money with and could buy or invest in. A related channel is the "9-to-5 strategy": looking at your own employer or the vendors you work with for an aging owner without a succession plan. Whichever channel you use, the endgame is the same: track every conversation in a funnel (listings reviewed → NDAs signed → letters of intent → deep due diligence → close), because deal-making rewards volume and consistency far more than talent. A buyer typically has to review 50–100 businesses to close just one deal — which is precisely why accountability (a partner, weekly check-ins, and small celebrated wins) is what actually separates people who close deals from people who talk about closing deals.

Step-by-Step: The Quiet Seller Method

  1. Pull your Venmo/PayPal/credit card history and list every small business you already pay.
  2. Ask the owner directly how much revenue they do, and whether they'd ever consider selling or taking on a partner.
  3. Estimate value using the industry's typical margin and the "Keep It Stupid Simple" rule (2–3x SDE or revenue for small businesses).
  4. Track every conversation in a funnel; move consistently through outreach → NDA → LOI → due diligence → close.
  5. Report progress weekly to an accountability partner to stay consistent.

09 FAQ

Do I need to be rich to start buying businesses?

No. The entire point of seller financing and SBA loans is that the business's own future cash flow (and sometimes the seller directly) funds most of the purchase. What you need is a clear deal box, credibility, and consistent outreach — not a pile of cash.

Isn't it exploitative to buy from a struggling or elderly owner?

The lesson frames it the opposite way: many sellers are relieved to find a trustworthy buyer who will take care of employees and customers and finally let them retire, resolve a divorce, or step away from a business they're burned out on. A fair, transparent, seller-financed deal typically leaves the seller better off (more total money, lower taxes, faster close) than doing nothing.

What's the difference between on-market and off-market deals in one sentence?

On-market businesses know they're for sale and are listed publicly; off-market businesses haven't realized (or admitted) they're sellable yet, so you find them through direct outreach instead of a search filter.

Why avoid brokers when buying?

Brokers are typically paid by and loyal to the seller, and buy-side representation for small deals is often prohibitively expensive (five-figure retainers plus a percentage of the deal) — money better spent on the acquisition itself.

What size business should a first-time buyer target?

Generally under $10 million in revenue, cash-flowing today (not "hopeful"), with at least a 15% profit margin, in a simple, unglamorous, fragmented industry — think blue-collar services, not biotech.
