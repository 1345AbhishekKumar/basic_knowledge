Module 1 · Define win: explain it in one line

What Copywriting Actually Is

Copywriting is transferring information from one brain to another in the simplest way possible. That is the whole game: persuasive writing that makes someone understand, believe, and act. Newcomers confuse it with the little © copyright symbol next to logos, but the transcript corrects that fast — this is not legal paperwork, it is everyday persuasion. A blog post is copywriting. A YouTube script is copywriting. A short-form script, an email, an ad — all copywriting. The speaker learned it from Sam Parr, who sold a media company for tens of millions, and reasoned that a skill behind that kind of outcome was worth stealing. Zoom out and the economics get stark. Code is the back end of the internet, but copy is the front end — the layer every customer actually touches. In an age where AI can build almost anything, building is cheap and attention is scarce, so the remaining edge is distribution: the ability to shift supply and demand in your favor until you are oversubscribed. And the skill leaks far beyond marketing. An investor must convince LPs to wire money. A founder must convince investors, then convince a team to join. An employee must convince someone to hire them. A parent must convince kids to go to bed on time. Strip away the job titles and every one of those is the same act — thinking clearly, then communicating persuasively. That is why the transcript calls copywriting a life skill rather than a job skill, and why getting better at it quietly upgrades everything else you do.

Step-by-step: use the definition

  1. Name the transfer: whose brain, and what must land there?
  2. Delete the © confusion: is this persuasion or paperwork?
  3. Point at the surface: blog, script, email, or ad — same skill.
  4. Attach the stakes: who must act, and what changes if they do?
ObviousA landing-page headline that gets the click — persuasion doing its job.
Counter-intuitiveA parent getting kids to bed on time — no sale, still copywriting.
Edge caseAn investor memo to LPs — formal dress, same brain-to-brain transfer.

Analogy card

Code is the kitchen; copy is the menu. Guests never see the kitchen, but the menu decides what they order.

Plain version: building matters less than explaining, because explaining is what customers respond to.

What people get wrong

That copywriting means the © symbol, or that it is only ads. The transcript is explicit: it is all persuasive writing, everywhere, and a life skill.

Why this matters

If AI makes building free, the person who can create demand owns the game. One clear sentence can be worth more than one more feature.

Module 2 · Think win: run the Six Ps

Think Before You Write: The Six Ps

The transcript’s central claim is blunt: copywriting was never about the writing, it was about the thinking. Templates feel like value — identity hooks that call out “business owners,” outcome hooks like “if you’re ambitious but inconsistent,” promise hooks like “we build your funnel free or you pay nothing” — but repeating them without understanding why they work leaves you stuck. The fix is a pre-writing ritual called the Six Ps, used with every client before any copy exists: People, Positioning, Promise, Proof, Priority, Process. People comes first because you cannot answer “why choose us?” until you know who “they” are. The marathon-program demo proves it five times over: a beginner fears not finishing, so the promise is “finish your first”; a busy professional fears the schedule, so the promise bends around a 60-hour week; the over-40 runner fears injury; the Boston chaser fears missing by 90 seconds; the returner fears re-injury. Same product, five different messages — the avatar decides. Research runs through an empathy map with four quadrants: current daily reality (see, hear, smell, feel), future fears or hell, current frustrations (traffic, dress codes, useless meetings), and future goals or heaven — governed by the platinum rule of treating people the way they want to be treated, plus a personal bar of not writing until material feels overabundant. The deepest research trick is stealing customers’ own words: transcribe sales calls, find why they bought, and mirror it — freedom-flexibility-control becomes “disgustingly financially independent,” the 9-to-5 escape becomes “the normal path will never make you a millionaire,” the mom’s schedule becomes money around the kids, 30K months become “learn one skill,” the overworked Chicagoan becomes “if hard work was the answer, the donkey would own the farm.” Describe the pain better than they can and they assume you hold the cure: the pain is the pitch. Positioning is why you over anyone else — how the product sits in the buyer’s mind, raising recognition, easing sales, and getting customers to spread the idea, per Seth Godin. The Schlitz beer story carries it: dead last on “pure beer” while every rival claimed purity, until Claude Hopkins toured the plant, found 4,000-foot wells, expensive filters, and a thousand-year-old mother yeast cell, and made the ordinary process the campaign — first to state the facts wins, which is also why AI cannot replace the digging. Promise is the outcome, never the deliverable: Jobs sold “a thousand songs in your pocket,” not gigabytes, and the rug seller climbs the so-what ladder from 5×5 silk to anchored room to compliments to feeling like someone with taste — confuse the buyer and you lose them. Proof answers “why trust you,” since trust is belief plus evidence; three kinds stack from weakest to strongest, authority by association, your own results, and clients’ stories like Sabrina’s $50K-a-year-teacher to $50K-a-month-closer — matched to the avatar, because a middle-aged Asian woman believes another middle-aged Asian woman, not a kid from Alabama. The deaf-and-mute test hardens it: point at logos, numbers, “400 clients, 11 years,” “ads for a $106M launch in three days,” never adjectives. Priority answers “why now” through pain over pleasure — people jog toward pleasure but sprint from pain — via scarcity, urgency, and above all the cost of inaction, since the gap between now and heaven breeds deprivation, motivation’s cousin, like a drowning swimmer clawing for air. Process answers “how does it work” and must explain why alternatives fail, feel believable, feel different, and stay simple — built from “everyone thinks X, wrong because Y, solution is Z,” whether high-ticket sales or the value acceleration method. Both client demos close the loop: Hormozi’s six Ps roll into the “swamp” ad for $1–3M owners, Shelby’s roll into the barista-to-millionaire masterclass invite — each ad nearly writing itself once the thinking is done. The game is won before the pen is used.

Step-by-step: run the Six Ps

  1. People: name the avatar and map current, fears, frustrations, goals.
  2. Positioning: find the true, first-said fact rivals ignore.
  3. Promise: climb so-what until you hit identity, not features.
  4. Proof: stack authority, self, then matched client stories; pass the deaf-mute test.
  5. Priority: add scarcity, urgency, and the cost of standing still.
  6. Process: name the X-wrong-because-Y, then the Z mechanism.
ObviousMarathon promises rewritten per avatar — same race, five messages.
Counter-intuitiveSchlitz won by describing what every brewer did — first-said beats best-done.
Edge caseShelby’s unsophisticated audience: the vehicle itself is the process.
ObviousRug so-what ladder: silk square to “home worth showing off.”
Counter-intuitive“Donkey would own the farm” — insults hustle, converts the exhausted.
Edge case$106M-launch pointer beats any adjective about skill.

Module 3 · Write win: pass the five tests

Write for the Internet: The Five Cs

School writing and internet writing are opposite sports. School pays a teacher to read you, rewards the 500-word count, bans “I” and “you,” and mistakes big words for smartness. The internet owes you nothing: every reader is busy, distracted, and reading below a fifth-grade level, and the only smartness that counts is how smart the reader feels. That inversion produces the Five Cs of real-world writing. Clear comes first because nobody can buy what they cannot understand — and clarity is achieved by subtraction, working from the end backward and deleting everything unclear. The firefighter test makes it physical: mumbling “there may or may not be a situation downstairs” while the house burns is criminal, while “fire downstairs — window, jump” saves lives; the Hemingway app mechanizes the same instinct by grading level and flagging fat. Concise follows because every word competes with infinity — pretend each costs ten dollars and cut whatever survives without loss of meaning, training the muscle where brevity is forced: ten-plus tweets a day for six months inside 280 characters, which doubles as an idea playground where winners get promoted into hooks, video points, and scripts. Concrete means visualizable — readers must be able to picture it — so “we help businesses grow” becomes “a roofing company from 14 to 70 leads a month,” and the abstract ache of love zooms into a hand reaching across an empty bed and sniffing a pillow that still smells like her; not every line must be a movie, but every meaningful claim deserves its close-up. Conversational means matching the reader’s in-group, because corporate-school diction on social reads robotic the moment you say it aloud — twenty-year-olds get “text me if you need anything,” fifty-year-old lawyers get the register they live in, and the rule only works if Module 2 already told you who “they” are. Cadence turns sentences into music: unvarying lengths drone like a stuck record, so Gary Provost’s famous passage shortens, lengthens, then unleashes the long crescendo sentence on the rested reader — diagnosed by reading aloud until no stumble remains, and by squinting at the paragraph’s silhouette to check it zigzags instead of flatlining. The capstone image binds all five: great copy is a mall window, invisible glass between the shopper and the goods. Nobody admires the window. They see what it reveals — the offer.

Step-by-step: the five passes

  1. Clear: run the firefighter test, then Hemingway; delete fog.
  2. Concise: price words at $10; compress on tweets first.
  3. Concrete: replace abstractions with numbers and close-ups.
  4. Conversational: read aloud; rewrite into the avatar’s register.
  5. Cadence: vary lengths; confirm by ear and by squint.
Obvious“Fire downstairs — window, jump” vs. the mumbling version.
Counter-intuitiveSchool rewards length; the market punishes it at $10 a word.
Edge caseA love scene carried by a pillow’s smell, not the word “love.”

Analogy card

Good copy is mall glass: noticed only when dirty.

Plain version: the writing should never draw attention to itself — only to the offer.

What people get wrong

Writing to sound smart instead of making the reader feel smart — long sentences, big words, corporate tone pasted into social feeds.

Why this matters

Attention is rented by the second. Each C removes one reason to leave.

Module 4 · Structure win: ship a full post

Hooks, Bodies, and Calls to Action

Ogilvy’s old arithmetic still prices the work: people read five times as many headlines as bodies, so eighty cents of the dollar goes into the hook — and the transcript pushes it further, crediting the hook with roughly ninety percent of a post’s performance. The personal hook formula turns that pressure into steps: virality equals benefit times relevance times credibility, divided by perceived effort. Benefit is the payoff for caring; relevance is how many people it fits; credibility is why your mouth deserves the claim; perceived effort is the cost of reading plus the cost of doing. The Noah Kagan thread is the worked proof — “employee #30 at Facebook, fired, bitter, then a $100M company, 10 lessons from Zuckerberg”: credibility from the inside seat, benefit in ten transferable lessons, relevance because anyone can use them, effort collapsed from “know Zuckerberg” to “read a thread.” Bodies get two universal frames, chosen by intent. Teaching runs what-why-how: the point, why it matters, then the exact move — “drink more water” becomes clearer thinking, glowing skin, steadier energy, executed as one eight-ounce glass before each meal for twenty-four ounces a day, a skeleton the speaker even uses for book chapters. Stories run but-therefore: linear “and then” narration has no tension, while “woke, went to the store, but forgot the keys, therefore went home, but someone was in the kitchen” flips expectation at every joint and builds its own arc. Closes get the who-what-when-how CTA, because the goal is business, not influence: name who it fits, what to do, the deadline, and the mechanism — “copywriters from zero wanting a first high-paying client in 90 days, click below, download the free workbook, finish it in 20 minutes.” The guardrail on the whole assembly is one CTA to one avatar to one offer; two asks split the reader and kill both.

Step-by-step: assemble a post

  1. Hook: maximize benefit, relevance, credibility; minimize effort.
  2. Body: teaching? what-why-how. Story? but-therefore.
  3. CTA: who, what, by when, how — one avatar, one offer.
ObviousKagan thread: insider seat plus ten lessons anyone can use.
Counter-intuitiveNinety percent rides on the hook — the body is the smaller bet.
Edge caseWater advice: trivial what, heavy why, tiny how — still converts.

Analogy card

The hook is the storefront, the body the fitting room, the CTA the register. No register, no revenue.

What people get wrong

Posting to be an influencer — content with no next step — or stacking two CTAs and splitting the reader’s resolve.

Why this matters

Distribution without direction is entertainment. The CTA converts attention into business.

Module 5 · Money win: price your skill

From Practice to Paychecks

The transcript refuses to sell shortcuts: the secret to better copy is reading great writing and writing more, full stop. The speaker’s own immersion shows what “more” means — every copywriting book in reach, old ads daily, podcasts and videos in the cafeteria line, a swipe file of everything that grabbed attention with notes on why, copywork by hand-copying masters to absorb rhythm, ten-plus tweets a day for six months, and three agency jobs to practice on someone else’s dime while studying the operation from inside. It is slow and physical on purpose; cramping hands are the tuition, because the prize is the skill rather than any single payout — lottery winners go broke for the same reason in reverse, money without the person who can re-earn it, while the goal here is financial confidence, the knowledge that total loss could be rebuilt on skill alone. Money itself runs on a three-part machine: traffic, conversion, offer — people in, something sold, something to sell — and copy serves all three. Traffic is shorts, reels, YouTube scripts, paid ads; conversion is the email list, webinar, VSL and landing page; the offer is the thing the six-Ps mind now shapes better than the average owner can. Entry means picking one slice — ghostwriting, ad copy, email, VSL/webinar specialization, or growth operator building an influencer’s backend — then aiming high-ticket, since richer clients pay better, behave better, and grasp the value faster. Two lanes get the explicit nod: tech startups flush with raise money but clueless about spending it, pitched on launch videos or ad management for a cut; and info brands, pitched as a free upfront email sequence for ten to fifteen percent of the revenue it prints. Pricing power itself reduces to value creation along three vectors — save time, make money, cut risk — packaged until refusal feels silly, like a full sequence in fourteen days promising $100K or free labor. And when the offer still fails, the transcript hands a three-question autopsy: am I hard to say yes to, am I unclear on the money, or am I short on proof — then tune speed, risk, and ease until the answer flips.

Step-by-step: monetize

  1. Practice: read masters, swipe, copywork, daily reps, agency years.
  2. Position: one slice of the funnel, high-ticket buyers only.
  3. Offer: promise time, money, or risk relief — or free until proven.
  4. Debug: hard to say yes? money unclear? proof thin? Fix that vector.
ObviousEmail specialist taking 10–15% of sequence revenue.
Counter-intuitiveFree work upfront as the fastest route to high prices.
Edge caseGrowth operator: influencer keeps traffic, you own everything after.

Analogy card

Skill is the root system; offers are the fruit stand. No roots, no restock.

What people get wrong

Skipping the reps to chase money — then wondering why no proof exists when buyers ask for it.

Why this matters

Confidence compounds: every rep raises both the work and what you can charge for it.

Module 6 · Clients win: send one TAG pitch

Sign Clients with TAG

Beginners get a four-rung ladder with no skipped steps: study the craft through courses, videos, podcasts and copywork; practice in public by documenting the climb daily on Twitter and LinkedIn, which widens the surface area for luck; join an agency to get paid repetitions without hunting clients; then work free for a first case study before charging anything. Underneath sits the operating ethic for the broke and unknown — no money means no shame — proven at seventeen with no degree and no record: instead of hiding his age, the speaker did the work before the call, arriving with a Google Slides deck of specific ideas so tailored that refusal felt irrational. Every opportunity, the transcript argues, demands exactly two demonstrations, hunger for it and ability to do it, and upfront work is the only artifact that proves both at once — compressed into the lifelong question, how do I make this a no-brainer, how do I cut the friction of yes? The named version is TAG, read aloud from the book Work with the Best, and anchored to landing Noah Kagan’s AppSumo business at seventeen for what became a $150K role. Target meant picking one person whose trajectory matched his own — Kagan, needing newsletter growth before a book launch, already followed and studied. Audit meant fifty hours of disappearance into the subject: second- and third-page Google results, decade-old videos, every podcast, AppSumo’s backstory, allies, self-descriptions — then the newsletter itself interrogated end to end, list size, platform, landing page, first impression, signup path, welcome email, traffic sources, promoted versus ignored channels, consistency gaps, competitor habits, SimilarWeb splits, ending on the found gap of dormant Twitter and LinkedIn. Gift meant converting the pile into a nineteen-slide, deliberately scrappy, skimmable deck plus nine ready-to-publish posts the busy founder could test instantly — proof of taste and competence in one phone-scroll, which is why the pitch-deck format works: hunger and ability, pre-demonstrated, friction near zero. The transcript closes the whole course where it must: start messy and imperfect, accept the long game the twenty-year-old still claims to be early in, and remember that persuasive skill never makes its owner poorer — and on discouraged days, ask only whether quitting raises the odds of getting what you want, and which story you would rather one day tell, the one where it got hard and you quit, or the one where it got hard and you kept going. You cannot lose if you do not quit.

Step-by-step: run TAG this week

  1. Target: one buyer whose problem you already understand.
  2. Audit: fifty tabs deep — history, funnel, gaps, competitors, numbers.
  3. Gift: a skimmable deck plus usable work they can ship today.
  4. Ask: one CTA, one avatar, one offer — then follow up once.
ObviousThe 19-slide Kagan deck that won head of content at 17.
Counter-intuitiveScrappy beats polished — the deck matched Kagan’s anti-corporate taste.
Edge caseSame playbook reused for acquisition.com and Beehive.

Analogy card

A TAG pitch is a free sample at the bakery: taste first, wallet second — nobody buys blind.

What people get wrong

Pitching credentials you lack instead of work you already did. Buyers fund proof, not promises.

Why this matters

From zero, upfront work is the only currency. TAG converts hunger into evidence.

Loops back to Module 1: every deck, deck follow-up, and onboarding email is copywriting again.

FAQ

Is copywriting just ads?

No. The transcript counts blogs, scripts, shorts, emails, and ads — plus investor memos and job pitches. Any persuasion in writing qualifies.

Templates or thinking first?

Thinking. Templates without the Six Ps break on the first unfamiliar buyer; the thinking rebuilds them anywhere.

How long until I am good?

Years, not weekends — the speaker started at 15 and still claims beginner’s distance at 20. Daily reps plus agency years are the stated price.

Which niche should a beginner pick?

One slice, high-ticket: tech launch videos or ads, or info-brand email for a revenue cut. Depth first, breadth later.

What if I have zero proof?

Work free for one case study, document it publicly, then charge. Proof is built, not waited for.
