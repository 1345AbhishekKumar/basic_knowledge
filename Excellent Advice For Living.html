Excellent Advice For Living

On his 68th birthday Kevin Kelly wrote 68 bits of advice for his children, kept going on later birthdays, and compressed about 450 lessons into compact seeds — each one expandable into an essay. This lesson turns the highlighted 79 maxims from the transcript into seven cards you can practice: relate, work, repeat, think, see others, own it, and play long. Your win: pick three seeds today, expand each into one action, and share one with someone younger.

What You Will Learn

  • Listen people open with questions that reach the truth.
  • Ship creative work using deadlines and separate drafting.
  • Install habits that remove daily self-negotiation.
  • Think for yourself and judge choices by your future self.
  • Resolve conflict by seeing through the other side.
  • Play the long, compounding, one-of-one game.

Card 1 — Relate Deeply

Core explanation. Almost half of Kelly’s seeds are about other people, and the host keeps noting they work at home and at work alike. Start with energy: being enthusiastic is worth 25 IQ points. People lend attention, help, and second chances to the enthusiastic before they check credentials. Next comes the superpower, listening well. While listening to someone you love, keep asking “is there more?” until there is no more. The rule of three sharpens this: ask someone to go deeper than what they just said, then again, then once more — the third answer sits closest to the truth. Interest compounds socially: the more interested you are in others, the more interesting they find you, so to be interested, be interested. Encouragement is the highest-leverage speech. Kelly says you can change a life with a few words, and history agrees — a young Henry Ford heard Thomas Edison bang the table and shout “keep at it,” and Ford carried those words through years of struggle. Praise should arrive alive: don’t reserve your kindest praise for a eulogy; write it in a letter they can keep. Compliment people behind their backs and it returns to you. And in friction, subtract yourself: outlaw the word “you” in domestic arguments, don’t attend every argument you’re invited to, and remember you can’t reason someone out of a notion they didn’t reason themselves into. When someone is nasty, treat it as affliction or illness — empathy softens what argument hardens. Definitions to keep: enthusiasm means visible eagerness that raises a room; listening means questions that pull, not nods that wait; encouragement means true words, spoken early, in writing when they matter.

Card 2 — Work With Force

Core explanation. Kelly respects force applied kindly: deadlines, ambition, teaching, redoing, and protecting young ideas. Always demand a deadline, because it weeds out the extraneous and ordinary — without one, people justify more time and money in the name of better, while a real date forces different, and different is better. Ed Catmull of Pixar insists there must be a cut-by date; Christopher Nolan calls deadlines creative accelerators that ramp effort exponentially once the date is real. Aim ridiculously high next: a huge goal sets the bar so high that even falling short can beat ordinary success. Then learn by teaching — the best way to learn anything is to try to teach what you know, because explaining exposes the gap and forces the return trip. Then redo: to make something good, just do it; to make something great, redo it and redo it — fine things live in remaking. Guard the fragile middle. Separate creating from improving: you can’t write and edit, sculpt and polish, make and analyze at once, or the editor strangles the creator — while inventing don’t select, while sketching don’t inspect, while drafting don’t reflect. Charles Kettering says ideas grow like tender shoots needing care; Catmull says originality starts ugly and needs time; Henry Ford ran a “maternity ward” locking new programs away until they could walk; David Ogilvy calls ideas magical fragile seeds needing friends and defense. Finally, move through trouble: if you’re not falling occasionally you’re coasting, keep showing up since 99% of success is showing up (Jobs: half of entrepreneurship is pure perseverance), and when crises strike don’t waste them — no problems, no progress — with Michael Dell’s sharper form: if you lack a crisis, make one to drive change. Lucky breaks, the admired add, happen on detours, so embrace them.

Card 3 — Habits Build You

Core explanation. Kelly returns to habit more than any other lever: habit is far more dependable than inspiration, so make progress by making habits. Rockefeller’s rope image carries it — every day you spin a rope, until it is too thick to break, leading either to the peak or the trough, since bad habits form easily and sever hard while good ones form hard and hold easily. The purpose of a habit is to remove the act from self-negotiation: you no longer spend energy deciding, you just do. Kobe Bryant wrote summer training down precisely so that when difficulty whispered “skip this,” the paper answered “no negotiating.” Become the person who never misses rather than chasing the shape. Then aim the machine: focus on the important, because the urgent is a tyrant while the important should be king; spend on biggest opportunities, not biggest problems; and remember you don’t need more time — you already hold all you’ll ever get — you need more focus. Consistency beats quantity: small things daily outweigh occasional bursts, so limit your hours since worthy work is endless and only time is manageable. Rest is equipment, not weakness: taking a break signals strength, goofing off is underrated, efficiency overrated, and a weekly Sabbath of no work — rest, recharge, cultivate — becomes counterintuitively the most productive act. Gratitude oils everything: it opens every other virtue, is trainable like daily prayer (Rick Rubin), even one written line a day is the cheapest therapy. Character outlasts achievement: attend funerals young and listen — nobody recites the departed’s achievements, only what kind of person they were — which John Mackey echoes: at his age you think of relationships, not the 5,000th store. And you are what you do — not what you say, believe, or vote, but what your calendar funds (Aristotle via Jobs: excellence is habit). Ask: repeated 365 times, would today put me where I want to be?

Card 4 — Think For Yourself

Core explanation. Don’t measure your life with someone else’s ruler. Kelly’s line lands on people playing infinite games their own way — Michael Dell and James Dyson never auditioned for another’s yardstick; they chose the game and kept playing. When stuck between paths, pick the one producing change. Choose not to be outraged today. Over the long term the future is decided by optimists — not people ignoring problems, but people trusting our problem-solving ability compounds. Fear runs on thin imagination, so its antidote isn’t bravery but richer imagining of what could work. Anything real begins with the fiction of what could be, making imagination the most potent force — improvable precisely by ignoring what everyone knows. Thinking for yourself has a signature: your conclusions stop being predictable. The host’s three-hour talk with Shopify’s Toby Lutke felt exactly so — question after question, answers no one could have guessed, because Lutke reasons from scratch. Decide with your future self in the room: evaluate choices as if looking back 25 years — what would that self think? Dose even good things (for each love, ask its proper dose), and starve worry, since 99% of anxieties never happen. You can even rewrite the past’s meaning: you can’t change events, but you can change your story about them — what matters is what you did about what happened. And if misfortune piles up, remember the pile test: thrown together with everyone’s troubles, you’d grab your own back. Terms to fix: ruler means borrowed metrics (titles, likes, rivals’ scores); infinite game means play to continue, not to win once; future-self test means judging today from age plus 25.

Card 5 — See Through Others

Core explanation. Kelly’s social engine is perspective: your golden ticket is seeing through others’ eyes — it opens empathy, persuasion, and design, opening doors nothing else opens. Negotiation turns on one question: what does “yes” mean for them? Learn that before you sit down. Want a partner instead of a critic? Ask for advice, not feedback — feedback summons judges, advice summons teammates. Prefer kind over right with no exceptions, without confusing kindness for weakness. Collect, don’t hoard: collecting benefits you only when displayed prominently and shared in joy — Buffett modeling it by synthesizing decades of reading into shareholder letters — while hoarding is the same instinct curdled. Give to get, the universe’s counterintuitive math: the more you give, the more you get, the beginning of wisdom. Friends beat money since almost anything money does, friends do better; replace transactions with relationships and life improves. Trust compounds slowly and collapses fast — earned in drops, lost in buckets — sealed by unwavering honesty, since an honest friend wants nothing from you, and by keeping no secrets: deliver bad news yourself, because secrets are rarely unknown and corrode their holders. Write emails you’d speak aloud (anything emailed eventually arrives), craft subject lines like headlines — Ogilvy: five times more readers see the headline, 80 cents of the dollar spent there — and elevate endings, giving bad news first since people remember how things end. Choose customers over competitors: both obsessions work, but customer obsession travels further (Bezos: it compounds into a moat, invents proactively, aims at durable needs). Constantly hunt overlapping agreement and dwell there; disagreements shrink to edge cases.

Card 6 — Own It Fully

Core explanation. Nothing elevates you like owning mistakes: mess up, fess up. Munger’s bluntness sets the bar — admit plainly, even harshly (“complete stupid…”), never deny, externalize, or wallow; use errors as fuel for better decisions. Apologize in Kelly’s triple form: quickly, specifically, sincerely — and never dilute it with an excuse. Forgiveness is the inward twin: when you forgive, they may not notice but you will heal, since forgiveness is a gift to yourself; hatred poisons only the hater, so release grudges like poison, and accept the apology you will never get. Maturity’s foundation is wider than fault: just because it isn’t your fault doesn’t mean it isn’t your responsibility. Pair strictness correctly — strict with yourself, forgiving of others, since the reverse is hell for everyone — and answer insults with “you’re probably right,” which is often true and always disarming. Stay calm to lend calm, because calm is contagious. Stop auditioning: fear makes people stupid, and if you can avoid seeking approval, your power is limitless — ignore what others think of you, since they aren’t thinking of you. If you meet one jerk, ignore them; if you meet jerks everywhere daily, look inward. Be strict about becoming, not acquiring: work to become, not to acquire; contemplate your own weaknesses (easy to spot others’, profitable to face yours); and let the guiding question be “who should I become?” before “what should I do now?” Respect then follows without chasing: don’t aim to be liked, aim to be respected.

Card 7 — Play Long, Stay You

Core explanation. Don’t be the best — be the only. Competition has a finish line; uniqueness has none. Your childhood weirdness is raw material: what made you weird as a kid can make you great as an adult if you keep it (for the host, endless childhood reading became a career). Master something: through one thing deeply learned you gain a viewpoint for finding bliss — take one simple thing, almost anything, and take it extremely seriously, as if the only thing in the world, and you’ll light up the sky (Munger’s twin: find a simple idea and take it seriously). Then let compounding work: all great prizes in wealth, relationships, and knowledge come from amplifying small steady gains; bad things strike fast, good things grow slow; a long game compounds small gains until they outweigh big mistakes — miraculous things take about ten years, so cultivate an allergy to average and don’t optimize greatness on short horizons. Play infinite games, played to continue rather than win, since they yield unlimited rewards; to succeed get paid, to become wealthy help others succeed. Stay high-agency: everyone is shy, all waiting for your hello, email, and invitation — go ahead; always ask for what you want in life, business, and love; choose luck by treating setbacks as temporary; and believe the universe conspires behind your back to help. Travel light: possessions need repair, what you own ends up owning you — Sam Zell kept little despite billions — so choose selectively, rent the rest. Work the craft details Kelly sprinkles throughout: buy cheapest tools first, then the very best of what you use daily; spend headline-level care on email subject lines (Ogilvy’s 80 cents of the dollar); let words pass three gates (true? necessary? kind?); delete the manuscript’s first page and start in action; elevate good behavior over punishing bad; get incentives right (Munger: top-5% lifetime student, still underestimated yearly); hire aptitude plus attitude and train skills, since breakthroughs come from first-timers; research to seven levels deep; act like you belong (Bushnell to young Jobs); think with feet on walks and hands in notebooks; read books to be remarkable; guard your peak-energy hours; filter future invites with “would I do this tomorrow?” (Buffett’s empty calendar); chase opportunities over problems; promote what you love as an evangelist, simplify then exaggerate the message, keep the people around whom you feel best, read to your children, and trust lessons arrive in the order needed.

FAQ

79 maxims is a lot — where do I start?

Pick three seeds that sting slightly. Expand each into one action this week, per Kelly: seeds must be grown in your situation, not admired.

What if deadlines kill my creativity?

The transcript says the opposite with Catmull and Nolan: real dates accelerate by forcing decisions. Try one cut-by date before judging.

How do habits and rest fit together?

Daily-small execution plus weekly Sabbath. Rope-spinning six days, deliberate rest the seventh — rest is counted as training.

Is “be the only” realistic for beginners?

Yes via mastery: one simple thing taken extremely seriously, plus kept childhood weirdness, compounds into uncopiable edge over years.
