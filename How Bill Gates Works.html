How Bill Gates Works

Gates calls his edge fanaticism — all capabilities, day and night, aimed at writing good software, no weekends, no vacations. Rival Larry Ellison agrees with a twist: plenty of people are smarter than Bill; almost nobody matches his focus and endurance. One observer put it sharply: Gates wants you to think Edison, but he operates like Rockefeller — relentless, and after everything. This lesson follows that operating system down a timeline from Lakeside childhood to a 20,000-person Microsoft: fit, leanness, brutal work, small teams, ownership of waves, and endless competition. Your win: run one project for a month the way Gates ran software — narrow focus, lean costs, fast feedback, owned result.

What You Will Learn

  • Aim fanatical focus at one fitting field.
  • Turn tight constraints into leaner engineering.
  • Convert raw interest into skill with fast loops.
  • Keep teams tiny, bright, and fiercely driven.
  • Own the wave and its source code.
  • Compete daily without ever feeling finished.

Node 1 · The engine

Module 1 — Fanatical Focus And Endurance

Core explanation. Everything else in this story runs on one fuel. Gates names it himself: being fanatical meant taking every capability, day and night, and pointing it at writing good software — and he loved it, then reveled in it. Weekends and vacations simply didn’t exist in his calendar, and early Microsoft felt frantic and demanding to anyone who didn’t share the temperature. Ellison, watching from across a fierce rivalry, says brilliance wasn’t the separator — plenty of scientists outranked Bill on raw intellect — while almost nobody matched his focus and endurance across decades. Barry Diller’s compliment lands the same way: young, mean, and not tired. Gates lived in binary states his whole life: totally apathetic toward anything outside his obsession, completely consumed inside it. As a boy he poured intensity into board games, puzzles, and whatever caught him, and nothing into the rest. As a CEO he sealed himself like a submarine hatch — no girlfriend, no hobbies — asking why anyone wouldn’t work 80-hour weeks with such an opportunity open, exhausted yet exhilarated, prowling for people with the same excess energy. The therapist’s verdict on the teenage Gates held for the whole career: loosen your grip, he’ll win, you’ll break first. Endurance here has a precise meaning: not sprinting once, but returning to the same desk, the same problem, for twenty-plus years while rivals tire, marry, fame, or drift. Fix two terms now. Fanaticism means sustained, joyful over-allocation to one craft. Endurance means still being at that desk when the wave finally pays.

Node 2 · Lakeside childhood

Module 2 — Fit, Reading, And Virgin Territory

Core explanation. Focus needs somewhere true to land, and Gates found it absurdly early. His school, Lakeside, owned something almost no teenagers on earth had: access to a real computer over a phone line to a mainframe. The moment he touched it, he and his friends gave it every free hour. Programming fit his nature exactly — he could set his own measures of success, the field felt limitless, and the logic, stamina, and retreat-into-thought it demanded came naturally. He would picture code in his mind for hours. Two older traits fused with the new machine. First, reading: by elementary school he read alone for hours, learned how to learn by himself, and found school slow by comparison — then read the whole World Book Encyclopedia A to Z, one of those shelf-devourers (Edison, Churchill, Musk share the pattern) who must consume everything on a beloved subject. Second, will: confident in his intellect early (“I can do anything I put my mind to” at 11, first million by 25 stated as proof, not boast), argumentative, forceful — the family therapist told his parents to ease up because the boy would win and they would break. Harvard later disappointed him: he came to learn from smarter people and left hungry. His mature advice compresses the lesson: look for virgin territory, fields with no track, no instruction — he and Allen literally knew more than their teachers and pieced together first programs alone. Fit here means the work rewards your native pleasures. Territory means an unmapped field where effort, not credentials, decides.

Node 3 · Constraints into creed

Module 3 — Lean Engineering, Demon Costs

Core explanation. Small machines taught Gates a creed he never dropped: constraints are your friend, and lean engineering is good engineering. Early computers held almost no memory, so programs had to be short — it is easier to sprawl sloppy code for pages than to compress the same work onto one page, just as a short letter costs more time than a long one. Gates shared this with Dyson and Musk, but he also lived it as disgust: a visceral hatred of waste and inefficiency that followed him from code into company. He became what Buffett demands of managers — a demon on costs — writing a seven-page business plan whose first line refused to get ahead and drown in costs, setting twin goals of growing reputation and making money, and later scolding memos about $14,000 spent since he left and royalties uncollected sending them “down the tube.” He tracked everything: travel expenses, oversight, follow-ups, negotiations, even the missing company credit card. The creed scaled: Microsoft printed cash from year one, needed no venture capital, ran no extravagant overhead, and kept that one-year buffer described later. Two definitions anchor this. Lean means achieving the function with the least code, people, and spend — elegance measured by subtraction. Demon-on-costs means reviewing small outflows personally, because small leaks predict large floods. The payoff was strategic, not aesthetic: every dollar unspent was a month of independence from MITS, Pertec, IBM, or any payer who might vanish.

Node 4 · Nights at the terminal

Module 4 — Reps That Turn Interest Into Skill

Core explanation. Loving computers wasn’t enough; Gates converted love through a specific machine. His favorite line in his own book describes it: after falling for a field, put in hard focused work until raw interest becomes real skill — for him about four months of unconstrained, zoned-in nights that set a decades-long style. The loop was addictive and exact: computers punished sloppiness instantly (one comma breaks everything), demanded logical consistency, and answered immediately — try, run, wrong, retry. Getting better felt like a rush, and proving himself against older programmers sharpened it; he resolved to let nobody get anything on him. Hunger supplied what money couldn’t: sneaking out bedroom windows to catch night buses to the computer center, and with Allen diving into dumpsters for discarded printouts of engineers’ code — the transcript’s refrain asks, how bad do you actually want it, because someone like Gates or Dell wants it that badly. Critique became fuel rather than insult. When an older programmer tore his code apart — not just bugs but whole structure — Gates’s reflex to snap “you’re wrong” gave way to awe: the corrections lifted his work to levels he hadn’t known existed, opening a new floor. At Harvard the same style returned: 36 hours awake programming and studying, then crashing 12 hours fully clothed, shoes on, for months. Then the Altair sprint proved the machine under business pressure: having bluffed MITS that BASIC was nearly done with nothing written, Gates and Allen worked to collapse and back up again until they had the first working version and won the deal. Define the parts: zone means cost-blind, time-blind total focus; loop means instant, unforgiving feedback per attempt; teardown means welcoming a stronger critic who rebuilds your structure.

Node 5 · Albuquerque to Seattle

Module 5 — Tiny Crews Of Relentless People

Core explanation. Gates scaled by refusing to scale. Four years in, Microsoft held about eleven or twelve people — the famous moving photo shows eleven — and even at $7 million in sales facing IBM’s near-$30 billion, fewer than forty. At thirty employees it was Gates, one secretary, and twenty-eight programmers; he personally wrote checks, answered mail, and took calls. The crew, the Microkids, were high-IQ insomniacs driving themselves to ability’s edge, and hiring doctrine matched: grab programmers young and enthusiastic before other workplaces ruin them, freshly minted degrees, brightest lights first — a great programmer outproduces an average one ten to one, a genius fifty to one. Around them Gates wanted only high-energy, intense people, recognizing his own excess instantly; Ballmer had it beyond anyone he’d known and became the 24-hour partner Allen couldn’t be — someone to argue big calls, track which customers might pay, and model the bank balance nightly. Standards cut both ways: glass 7/8ths empty, no laurels, make it great then do it again, and rigorous weeding of underperformers. The culture was famously abrasive. Gates prowled parking lots on weekends, memorized license plates, and asked a man after an 81-hour four-day marathon what he was doing tomorrow — “taking the day off?” — “why would you do that?” Conflict was method: intense one-on-ones, sarcastic prods about lost contracts, the legendary “stupidest thing I’ve ever heard” for unprepared positions — yet he never pulled rank, wanted pushback, drilled down to the best answer, and respected whoever survived his skepticism. Terms: Microkids means tiny elite bound by crusade, not headcount; pushback means earning respect by defeating his doubts with preparation.

Visual analogy. A speedboat circling a tanker. It can’t outpush — it outturns.
Mnemonic: YBR — Young, Bright, Relentless
  • Obvious: Eleven people, $7M sales, versus IBM.
  • Counter-intuitive: Cruel-sounding reviews that promoted whoever fought back well.
  • Edge-case: Memorizing staff license plates to audit weekends.
What people get wrong

Abrasion looks like dysfunction. Here conflict was quality control with rules: prepare, push back, best answer wins regardless of rank.

Why this matters. Small brilliant crews kept the burn low and the bar high enough to seize the wave next.

Node 6 · Altair to IBM

Module 6 — Own The Wave And Its Code

Core explanation. Gates repeatedly bet on a technological wave before fares were posted — then fought to own what the wave carried. The first wave: scarce computing giving way to plenty, millions of machines coming. He and Allen bluffed MITS that Altair BASIC was nearly finished with nothing written, then out-worked every claimant to deliver the first running version and take the deal — RVs camping outside MITS proved the hunger was real and markets for novel products run larger than forecasts. The deeper conviction sounded insane at the time: a pure software company, when culture said software should be free, copied, shared, even stolen. Their late-night conclusion held that cheaper machines spreading everywhere meant nearly unlimited demand for high-quality software — a paragraph the host rereads in disbelief, now that annual software sales measure in hundreds of billions. Then came the hard schooling. The MITS contract gave MITS exclusive sublicense rights, forcing every new customer through MITS with revenues split — a bad deal that nearly killed Microsoft in its cradle. Gates diversified frantically, fought, and caught luck when Pertec bought MITS for $6 million and froze everything: no royalties, no sublicenses, blocked deals. Ed Roberts had warned the buyers they’d have their hands full — “Roosevelt telling Churchill he could deal with Stalin.” Gates’s close reading of his own contract won the arbitration: Microsoft had licensed, never transferred, the code — ownership clarified, exclusivity terminated, five or six waiting clients paying within weeks. He replayed the ownership logic on the IBM deal: forego per-copy royalties to license DOS freely to clone makers, because the compatibles, not the IBM version, were the bonanza. Pricing sharpened the same way Herb Kelleher reframed rivals — Gates sold against in-house engineering budgets at less than half their cost, with a stronger product. Underneath sat the buffer from Module 3: enough cash to survive a year with zero income, software’s light capital needs giving wide margins for error, and a yearly top-10 mistakes memo (mostly markets entered too late, constrained by hiring and delivery promises) keeping them edgy.

Node 7 · 1990 → 1997 and beyond

Module 7 — Compete Daily, Never Feel Finished

Core explanation. By 1990 Microsoft became the first software company to sell over a billion dollars in a year — and Gates’s fire only whitened. Neither marriage, fame, nor fortune dented it. His doctrine stayed simple: killing competition is the game, because fewer rivals raise your odds, so study every rival’s CEO, revenues, projects, and product flaws, and treat a lost $50,000 contract as a $100,000 swing since the enemy gains what you lose. Competitors felt it personally. Philippe Kahn said Gates treated everything — ideas, share, contracts — as rightfully his, understanding only leverage; Gates answered in interviews by itemizing the fallen: Lotus late to Macintosh and Windows, Borland distracted by a bad merger, Kahn better at saxophone and sailing than money, WordPerfect fading. The Borland war councils, “Delete Philippe” shirts, a rival’s photo slammed on tables (“how do I get rid of this guy?”), even Gates alone in a conference corner studying Kahn’s photograph — the transcript calls it hardcore, driven by hunger to win and dread of failure, aimed not just to beat but to destroy. Yet the same man told Stanford interviewers in 1997, with 20,000 employees and about $20 billion, that it was just the beginning: maybe $55 billion of opportunity ahead (last year’s revenue would near $280 billion), software only growing more valuable. The operating rules of that endless game: keep the main thing the main thing — sell software, not stock; commit past the point where founders fade, since most quit caring what the next stage demands; reject the “entrepreneur” label — he was a software creator who gathered a team and did whatever the work needed; enjoy each day’s work for its own excellence; and define aggression as shipping good product. Terms: leverage means position that compels outcomes; main-thing means the product before the paper.

FAQ

Was Gates just the smartest person in the room?

Ellison’s verdict says no — many outranked him on intellect. The separator was focus plus endurance sustained for decades.

Didn’t Microsoft just get lucky with IBM?

The transcript shows preparation meeting luck: wave thesis, bluff-plus-sprint, contract literacy, and the non-exclusive clone strategy turned one deal into an industry.

How do I copy the 80-hour weeks safely?

Don’t copy hours; copy the seal — one fitting field, lean costs, fast loops, tiny crew. The transcript’s extremes describe history, not medical advice.

Where should I read next?

Source Code for childhood, Hard Drive for the build, Paul Allen’s Idea Man for the partnership’s other side, and the 1997 In the Company of Giants interview for mature doctrine.
