Field Notes — Entry 01

The Keystone

Why a literal, verifiable scripture became the church's survival strategy — and the moment it almost lost.

Growing up, Johnny Harris was taught that the Book of Mormon was “the most correct book of any book on earth” and “the keystone of our religion.” The second phrase isn't a metaphor the church chose casually. A keystone is the stone at the top of an arch that locks every other stone in place: pull it out, and the whole arch collapses. That is exactly how the church thought about its founding scripture.

Joseph Smith claimed he had translated the book from ancient records — a history of a lost American civilization, with specific names, battles, languages, and technologies. That specificity made the book verifiable: it is either a true record or it isn't. And by the 1930s, the verdict from archaeology, geology, linguistics, and anthropology was increasingly clear — and devastating. Even researchers at the church's own university, BYU, sorrowfully concluded that the evidence pointed to Joseph Smith having written it himself.

Here was the existential fork. If the Book of Mormon fell, so did everything attached to it: Smith seeing God, the “one true church,” the priesthood authority, the preparation for the second coming. A symbolic, modernized Christianity would survive — but, as Johnny notes, every Christian church has that. It's not special. It wouldn't drive people across oceans and plains to sacrifice everything. So in the 1930s, church leaders chose literalism. J. Reuben Clark told BYU's professors they were to be “watchmen upon the towers of Zion,” teaching the scriptures and the leaders' words — not their own private interpretations. One leader put the stakes plainly: if reinterpretation became dominant, “we may as well close up shop.”

The twist is that the early church wasn't always rigid. In its first hundred years it was full of debate: leaders speculated about star systems where God lived, a heavenly mother, and many gods. That flexibility worked for a small band of renegades in the Utah desert. It could not survive the modern world. So the church standardized its theology — and the keystone, literal and unchanging, is what the whole later machine was built to protect.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. 1830 — a book that invites checking. Smith publishes a translation of ancient American records, full of literal names, dates, and places.
  2. Verifiability cuts both ways. As science advances, the book's claims look less like history and more like invention.
  3. The doubt goes internal. BYU's own PhDs and official church historians conclude the evidence favors Smith as author.
  4. Two possible futures. Modernize into a symbolic church (safe, but ordinary) or double down on literal truth (bold, but risky).
  5. 1930s — literalism wins. J. Reuben Clark's BYU address demands obedience, certainty, and one authorized interpretation.

Field Notes — Entry 02

The Rebrand

How a persecuted polygamist movement overcompensated its way into America's most family-friendly faith.

The church's 19th century ended badly: driven out of the United States, sheltering in the high desert, practicing polygamy, fighting the federal government — and almost being destroyed by it. Under intense pressure the church gave up polygamy; members who refused split off into fundamentalist offshoots. Then came what scholars call hyper-Americanization: not just assimilating, but overcompensating — becoming more American than most Americans.

The church leaned hard into the nuclear family (the opposite of polygamy), backed Prohibition, sent tens of thousands of members to fight in World War I, and flipped its relationship with national holidays. In the 1880s members commemorated the Fourth of July with a funeral for American democracy; by the early 1900s they were celebrating the founding and praising America in general conference. Patriotism became a proof of worthiness.

The look changed too. After decades of frontier beards — an “expression of non-conformity” — the church went clean-shaven. Prophet David O. McKay was the first to shave entirely, preaching about white shirts: “We are not so poor that we can't afford clean white shirts.” BYU codified it all in its honor code: no beards, no tank tops. For girls the standards were stricter — a whole modesty code: skirts below the knee, no strapless or backless dresses, clothes that don't “call attention to the body.” One leader put the rationale bluntly in 2008: “Our outward appearance and behavior give a message.”

By the 1970s the church hired a consulting firm to improve its image. The advice: downplay the doctrine, highlight the family. Out came wholesome TV commercials, Reader's Digest inserts, and later the “I'm a Mormon” campaign. Post-WWII, that clean-cut look read as American values — even anti-communist values — and helped the church grow fast in Central America, Europe, the Pacific, and Australia. The operating creed, repeated to Johnny as a kid: we are in the world but not of it — play the system, do it better than everyone else, adopt none of its beliefs.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Crackdown & capitulation. The U.S. government nearly destroys the church; polygamy is renounced; fundamentalist offshoots split off.
  2. Overcorrect toward Americanness. Nuclear family, temperance, World War I service, patriotic holidays.
  3. Change the look. Beards fall; white shirts rise; leaders dress like mid-century businessmen.
  4. Codify at BYU. The honor code bans beards and tank tops; a dress and grooming manual governs members' appearance, with stricter modesty rules for girls.
  5. Hire the professionals. 1970s consulting → family commercials, Reader's Digest inserts, the “I'm a Mormon” campaign.

Field Notes — Entry 03

Correlation

The 1960s technocratic revolution that made a global church teach one message, from one office, on the same day.

After World War II the missionary effort exploded — and with it, a problem. Converts poured in from Samoa, France, Japan, and Latin America, each bringing local traditions. Doctrine started to fracture; a lesson in Peru might drift from a lesson in Provo. The leaders in Salt Lake City realized they had lost control over what was being taught in every branch on earth. So they did what any corporation would do: they centralized.

In the 1960s a committee spent years rewriting every manual, policy, and procedure into super-clear language. Then they kept going — reorganizing the entire church into a hierarchy where the prophets and apostles at the top influenced and controlled everything below. Every bishop on earth received a handbook laying out official policy in black-and-white. Standardized verbiage dictated how to teach the Book of Mormon and the Bible — “what to say and what not to say.” Scholars call it a “technocratic revolution”: the end of open theological debate in the church. Leaders agreed not to publicly criticize one another. The result was “one voice.”

Even the church's most successful institution was absorbed. The Relief Society — a historically autonomous women's organization known for social outreach and food drives — was brought under priesthood control. Its curriculum shifted toward teaching women to be wives and mothers. Its many magazines were shut down and replaced by three official ones: adults, youth, kids. Johnny grew up with those magazines on a coffee table that never saw a cup of coffee.

The physical church became uniform too: identical chairs, floors, layouts, woodwork, even a gym in every building. The same lessons were taught worldwide on the same day. Johnny describes sitting in a chapel in the mountains of Guatemala and hearing the same songs — and feeling, powerfully, part of a global community. That belonging was a designed output, not an accident: standardization creates both control and a profound sense of membership.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Growth fractures the message. Global converts blend local traditions into doctrine; Salt Lake can't supervise every branch.
  2. The committee rewrites everything. Manuals, doctrine, policies, and procedures — all rewritten in clear, unambiguous language through the 1960s.
  3. The hierarchy consolidates. All teaching comes under the priesthood; a bishop's handbook governs every congregation on earth.
  4. Autonomy is absorbed. The Relief Society loses its independence; magazines collapse into three; leaders stop publicly disagreeing.
  5. The uniform output. Identical buildings, songs, and lessons worldwide — belonging and control, produced at scale.

Field Notes — Entry 04

The Information Ecosystem

Movies, music, posters, blessings, and conference — the media apparatus that turned a correlated message into lived identity.

Correlation standardized the message; the information ecosystem made it inescapable. Church bookstores stocked approved manuals, stickers, highlighters, and book bags. The church made high-quality films — “The Testaments,” an ambitious dramatization of the Book of Mormon story that Johnny estimates he watched 500 times in Spanish on his mission — plus youth videos modeling correct behavior, original music, musicals, and comedy movies about missions. There was jewelry engraved with CTR (“choose the right”), and pithy posters for bedroom walls: clean your sin, dress modestly, prepare for the temple, believe in the gold plates, go on a mission.

Two rituals personalized the message. First, the patriarchal blessing: a seasoned priesthood holder lays hands on your head and “the spirit speaks” your future — transcribed, printed, laminated, and carried with you for years. Johnny's honest description: “It feels awesome… like I have access to a guy with the priesthood who is going to see my future.” Second, general conference: twice a year, ten hours of talks from prophets and leaders, delivered to 21,000 believers in Salt Lake and broadcast worldwide, wrapped around the famous choir. Sitting in that room, Johnny says, you feel lucky to be part of something enormous.

Then there's the defensive wall. Anything that contradicted the official line was “anti-Mormon literature” — a phrase, Johnny notes, that was “embedded in my psychology.” A Reddit post about Joseph Smith's wives? Anti-Mormon. A New York Times article debunking Smith's translations? Anti-Mormon. Bishops warned against it: “theological pornography.” Historians and leaders who published contradicting history were sidelined or excommunicated — Kate Kelly was excommunicated for advocating women's priesthood. The ecosystem had a one-way valve: information in, criticism out.

Even conversion techniques were engineered. Johnny's mission used a chemistry demo — food dye “sin” in a glass of water, then bleach making it clear — to dramatize baptism on the spot. The goal of the whole apparatus: repetition so constant that the message becomes identity.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Canonize. Approved manuals and official verbiage define exactly what may be taught.
  2. Distribute. Bookstores, films, music, musicals, jewelry, and posters carry the message into homes and bedrooms.
  3. Personalize. Patriarchal blessings and conference talks make the message feel individually addressed.
  4. Amplify. General conference's scale and the choir's emotional power make belief feel collective and chosen.
  5. Gate the exits. “Anti-Mormon literature” is demonized; dissenting historians and activists are sidelined or excommunicated.

Three Examples

  • Obvious —General conference: twice a year, 10 hours, 21,000 in the room, broadcast to the world.
  • Counter-intuitive —The “yodo y cloro” baptism demo: missionaries literally purified dyed water with bleach in front of a stranger — conversion engineered as a chemistry trick.
  • Edge case —Self-policing: a faithful historian who publishes what church research actually found isn't expelled by critics — he's excommunicated by the institution itself.

Field Notes — Entry 05

Tithing & the Temple Recommend

How a 10% income rule and a 15-question interview turn belief into a revenue engine.

From childhood, members are taught that the first thing you do with any money is pay 10% — tithing — to the Lord. Johnny paid it from his first paycheck at Dairy Queen. But tithing isn't a casual contribution; it's administered. At the end of every year comes tithing settlement: you sit in the bishop's office, he shows you the record of what you declared, and you look him in the eye and confirm, yes, I paid a full tithe.

The real enforcement mechanism, though, is the temple recommend — a small card with a barcode that is the only ticket into the temple. And the temple is where the most important rituals happen: baptisms for the dead, eternal marriage (sealing), the ordinances needed for the highest tier of heaven. To get the card, you sit alone with your bishop and answer fifteen pointed questions. Do you have a testimony of the restoration — that Joseph Smith restored the one true church? Do you sustain the church president as prophet, seer, and revelator, “the only person on the earth authorized to exercise all priesthood keys”? Do you follow church teachings in private and public behavior? Do you support any teaching contrary to the church? Do you keep the Sabbath? Are you honest? Are you a full tithe payer? Do you keep temple covenants — including wearing the garments? Are there serious sins to resolve? And finally: are you worthy?

Answer “correctly” and you get the recommend. Don't — and the temple is closed to you, even for your own child's wedding. You enter a public repentance process: no bread and water on Sunday, with your whole congregation watching. Johnny had his recommend taken away once, six weeks before his temple wedding: “It was horrific… I felt shame.”

Notice the design. The recommend bundles money, doctrine, and behavior into one loyalty check. Tithing isn't just collected — it's verified annually, and non-payment carries social and spiritual consequences. Belief, behavior, and revenue are enforced by the same gate.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Teach 10% early. First money → first tithing; it becomes automatic long before there's real income.
  2. Verify annually. Tithing settlement: declare a full tithe face-to-face with your bishop.
  3. Interview. Fifteen questions, in a private room, from an authority figure — covering belief, obedience, and tithing.
  4. Issue the card. A barcoded temple recommend, valid for entry to the most sacred space.
  5. Enforce the stakes. No recommend = no temple = no sealing = no highest heaven — plus public shame and a repentance process.

Field Notes — Entry 06

The Money Machine

From Eldon Tanner's financial discipline to $200 billion in liquid assets — the compounding of a century of control.

In the 1970s, church leadership began drawing from the corporate world — high-powered CEOs and lawyers drafted in to manage the institution. The pivotal figure was N. Eldon Tanner, a Canadian oil tycoon and business powerhouse. Tanner's project was simple: turn the church from an organization that spends tithing into one that makes money. He cut costs, imposed financial discipline, and began investing tithing in stocks, bonds, and real estate. The church hired Wall Street professionals to run a trust fund, and adopted a rule: spend only a fixed percentage each year, so a permanent rainy-day fund keeps growing forever.

Then compounding did the rest. Through the Reagan-era 1980s and the neoliberal 1990s, the investments exploded. The church now owns enormous holdings of land — ranches, farms, commercial, hospitality, industrial, and retail property across the country. And it invests in people: BYU subsidized 70% of Johnny's tuition, and the business school is the Marriott School — yes, that Marriott. Graduates who pass through the whole pipeline become loyal, lifelong tithe payers. (Johnny's honest aside: “I was not a good investment for the Church.”)

The scale is staggering. In the last decade alone, wealth is estimated to have grown by $100 billion. More than $200 billion sits in stocks, bonds, and cash — more liquid money than Walmart holds. It's the richest religious organization in the world (the video's title puts total estimated wealth around $293 billion).

How is it spent? Some on humanitarian aid; more on aid to members in need; about $1 billion a year on education; a large chunk on operating the church. But the largest slice — roughly 65% of expenditures — goes straight back into investments. A few years ago, a believing employee of the church's investment firm went public, alleging $100 billion earmarked for charity sat unspent. The SEC investigation found the church had used shell companies across the country to conceal its wealth; it paid a fine, and since 2019 has filed its taxes properly. Since then, humanitarian spending has risen sharply — almost certainly a reaction to the scrutiny.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. 1970s — change the operating model. Eldon Tanner: invest tithing instead of spending it; cut costs; impose financial discipline.
  2. Professionalize. Wall Street managers run a centralized trust fund; a spending cap protects the principal.
  3. Compound. 1980s and 1990s markets multiply the portfolio; land, real estate, and the BYU pipeline all grow it.
  4. Reach scale. $200B+ in liquid assets; richest religious organization in the world; ~65% of spending reinvested.
  5. Face scrutiny. Whistleblower → SEC investigation → shell companies exposed → fine → tax compliance since 2019 → a surge in humanitarian spending.

Field Notes — Entry 07

The Pipeline & the Thesis

How a child becomes a lifelong tithe payer — and what Johnny concluded after ten years out of the church.

Bring the modules together and you see the pipeline. It starts in childhood: songs and lessons about where you came from and where you're going, seminary at 5:30 a.m. before school, baptism at eight, the priesthood at twelve, youth camps, a strict Sunday culture. Then the mission: two unpaid years — Johnny served in Tijuana under a strict dress code with no free time, no music, no vacation — where missionaries are also administrative foot soldiers, filling forms, reporting lessons taught and baptisms performed, auditing member records (Johnny did exactly that in Mexicali, binder in hand). Then BYU, “the Lord's campus,” with subsidized tuition and an honor code. Then the adult loop: temple marriage, career, volunteer leadership, and lifetime tithing.

It works. The post-WWII missionary surge grew the church across Central America, Europe, the Pacific, and Australia — in part because its clean-cut Americanness read as trustworthy, even anti-communist. Today the same pipeline is growing the church in South America and Africa while Western membership declines. The newest leader has softened some of the culture Johnny grew up with — more local variation, more transparent history. But the keystone, the doctrine, hasn't changed: it remains a high-commitment, high-control religion.

Johnny left about ten years ago — and says he could only begin questioning once he was physically out of the pipeline, out of BYU, away from the repetition. Even then it was brutal: he has spent the decade rewriting his understanding of worthiness, unlearning the need to be an ambassador for a misunderstood organization, and holding the impossible contradiction that the church is in him — his work ethic, curiosity, Spanish, and family focus are its gifts, while its control did real harm to his mind. His conclusion, stated plainly: “All of this was made possible because of one thing, and that thing is control. Control of the story of my life by someone else.”

Not coercion — willing submission to earnest men who believe they're called by God. That's what makes the machine powerful: it didn't need to force anyone. It built a world in which compliance felt like love, and control felt like belonging.

09Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Mormon Church really the richest religious organization in the world?
According to the video's reporting, yes: total estimated wealth around $293 billion, with more than $200 billion in stocks, bonds, and cash — more liquid money than Walmart holds. It's unusually centralized compared to, say, the Catholic Church, which is a federation of many entities rather than one balance sheet.
Where does all the money actually come from?
The base is tithing — 10% of members' income, verified annually. Since the 1970s the church has invested that stream in stocks, bonds, and real estate, and ~65% of current expenditures are reinvested. The portfolio's growth through the 1980s and 1990s markets did the heavy lifting.
Why don't they spend more on charity?
The church's stated mission is proselytizing — preparing the world for the second coming — not humanitarian work. It believes it needs a giant rainy-day fund for that mission. Humanitarian and member-aid spending is real but a small slice; it increased markedly after the SEC scrutiny and whistleblower attention.
Did the Church break the law?
The SEC investigation found the church had used shell companies across the country to conceal the size of its holdings. It paid a fine and has filed its taxes properly since 2019. The whistleblower's broader allegation — that ~$100 billion intended for charity sat unspent — didn't produce legal fallout of its own.
Is everyone leaving the Church?
Membership is declining in the West (Johnny is among the leavers), while the same missionary pipeline is growing the church rapidly in South America and Africa. The leadership has softened some cultural rules and published more transparent history, but the core doctrine — the keystone — remains unchanged.
