How to Make Ads That Sell

Claude Hopkins spent more hours writing advertising than anyone in history — and wrote down the handful of principles he kept repeating. These field notes distill them, from a Founders podcast reading of My Life in Advertising and Scientific Advertising.

What you'll learn
  • Test any ad with one question: would this help a salesman face to face?
  • Turn "buy my brand" appeals into service people actually want.
  • Use the fixed levers of human nature: rewards, curiosity, price, belonging, specifics.
  • Write headlines that pick your buyers out of a crowd — then test them.

01 / CRAFT
Where the insight came from

Exceptional hours, not exceptional talent.

Before any technique, Hopkins makes one claim about himself: he out-worked everyone, and liked it. That is the engine behind every principle in these notes.

Claude Hopkins ran the most successful advertising career of his era — and refused to call it talent. His claim: exceptional hours, not exceptional ability. From a railroad foreman he learned the attitude that made those hours possible: work is a game if you decide it is. The helpers counted minutes to quitting time; the foreman built a porch. Hopkins copied the foreman for fifty years and mined every failure for a principle, "never making a mistake twice." The lesson is not to glorify overwork — it's that advertising insight comes from volume of contact with the work, and volume comes from actually liking the game.

  1. Work when others stop — the hours are the moat.
  2. Decide the work is the game; let rivalry be the fuel.
  3. Take volume of attempts: more swings, more misses, more principles.
  4. Never make the same mistake twice.
  5. Stay close to the money — sell; don't be an expense.
Example · What

A railroad foreman builds his house every evening after 10-hour days, while his helpers whittle on soapboxes.

So what: same job, same hours — he treats it as a game and ends up with a home.

Example · What

Hopkins begs off dinners and dances to spend evenings at his typewriter.

So what: love of work can be cultivated like love of golf — and it shows up as output.

Example · What

"I made no mistake twice" — decades of small errors, each mined for a durable principle.

So what: volume of attempts, not genius, is what produces the principles.

AnalogySkill compounds like interest: double the deposits — the hours — and you double the account. Nothing mystical about it.
Same hours, two attitudes
The foreman and the helpers spent identical evenings — only one of them built something.

02 / DEFINITION
What an ad actually is

Advertising is salesmanship in print.

Hopkins' definition never changes, and it comes with a test you can run on every ad decision you will ever make.

Advertising is salesmanship in print — multiplied salesmanship, reaching thousands where a salesman reaches one. So every ad question gets the salesman test: would this help a salesman sell face to face? A good salesman doesn't beg. He offers service — wanted information, a demonstration, something free that costs the prospect only a moment of curiosity. The best ads ask no one to buy, because people can be coaxed but not driven: whatever they do, they do to please themselves. Selfish appeals — "buy my brand" — meet resistance. Service appeals meet a buyer who convinces himself.

  1. Run the salesman test on every ad decision.
  2. Lead with service: information, advantages, a demonstration.
  3. Let the product prove itself — samples beat arguments.
  4. Make them take a small step before the free thing arrives.
  5. Ask no one to buy; picture the customer's side until buying is natural.
Example · What

Young Hopkins selling his mother's silver polish door-to-door: 1 in 10 buy on talk, nearly all buy on demonstration.

So what: a good product is its own best salesman — get it into their hands.

Example · What

A brush maker's 2,000 door men say: "I was sent here to give you a brush — take your choice."

So what: the gift flatters self-interest; she picks one, sees several she wants, and reciprocates with an order.

Example · What

John Powers' bankruptcy ad: "We are bankrupt. We owe $125,000 more than we can pay… if you come and buy tomorrow, we shall have the money to meet them."

So what: truth told fascinatingly is the rarest appeal — thousands flocked, and the store was saved.

AnalogyA salesman begging versus a salesman helping: you buy from the one who solved something, not the one who pressed hardest.

03 / AIM
Who the ad talks to

One person, never the mass.

The recurring mistake in advertising is dealing with people in the mass. Hopkins' correction is to get down to units — and to tell the pains you took.

A woman once wrote in asking about a sewing machine. The company would have sent the general catalog — "treating all inquiries alike. Big mistake." Hopkins sent the sewing-machine catalog and the names of nearby owners: treat every inquirer like a prospect who walked into the store. Write every ad to one person standing in front of you, centred on her desires, never your own pride. And tell the pains you take: Schlitz sat fifth in beer, but it was first to tell its purity story — and "others have never told this story" gave a common process exclusive, lasting prestige. When you can, put a person behind the brand — personalities appeal, corporations do not; make a man famous and his creation becomes famous.

  1. Pick one typical person who likely wants what you sell.
  2. Write to that person — not to "everybody."
  3. Centre on her expressed desires, never your pride points.
  4. Visit the factory; collect the pains taken to excel.
  5. Tell those pains first, even if they are common to the trade.
Example · What

Sewling-machine inquirer: instead of the general catalog, she gets the machine catalog plus owners' names in her town, invited to see the machines in person.

So what: treat the inquiry as a person in your store, and the reply writes itself.

Example · What

The Schlitz tour: plate-glass rooms of filtered air, white-wood-pulp filters, pumps and pipes cleaned twice daily, 4,000-foot artesian wells, six-month aging, a mother yeast cell from 1,200 experiments.

So what: every brewer did these things — Schlitz told the story first and jumped to neck-and-neck with first place.

Example · What

Billboards in San Francisco, from some of the world's most valuable companies, that only say: buy my product.

So what: nobody cares about your company; ads that speak to the reader's gain are the ones that stand out.

AnalogyA form letter is a megaphone in a crowd; a letter to one person is a seat across the table.
The mass and the one
A megaphone aimed at everyone answers no one's question.

04 / PSYCHOLOGY
What never changes

Sell the reward, not the prevention.

Human nature, Hopkins argues, is perpetual — the same today as in Caesar's time. Learn its levers once and you never need to unlearn them.

Psychology is fixed: what you learn about human nature never needs unlearning. The core finding — people do much to cure a trouble and almost nothing to prevent one. They want rewards: more success, more happiness, more beauty, more cheer. So Pepsodent was sold as a creator of beauty, never once pictured decay, and made its advocate a million dollars. Around that core sit reliable levers: curiosity, the strongest of the incentives; price, which people use to judge value; belonging, the pull of offers limited to a class; and specific facts — actual figures, unlike platitudes, are not discounted.

  1. Sell the reward; never lead with prevention.
  2. Arouse curiosity wherever you can.
  3. Use price as a signal — people judge things by it.
  4. Limit offers to a class; belonging is an advantage people defend.
  5. Be specific: real numbers carry full weight.
Example · What

A painting is ignored until its $750,000 price is announced — then a crowd surrounds it.

So what: people judge value by price; the number itself changed behaviour.

Example · What

An identical offer limited to veterans or lodge members or executives beats the same offer open to everyone.

So what: belonging is an advantage people will go a long way not to lose.

Example · What

Pepsodent's illustrations showed attractive people with beautiful teeth — never the afflicted.

So what: rewards pull; prevention barely moves anyone.

AnalogyHuman nature is an old operating system: fashions update, but the kernel — curiosity, price, belonging — never patches.

05 / LEVERAGE
The sentence that does the work

The headline picks its readers.

This is the chapter that made the episode's host reread the book. One sentence decides whether the rest of your work is ever seen.

A headline exists to pick out the people you could interest — "Hey there, Bill Jones," in a crowd. Nobody reads everything; headlines select, the way newspaper headlines do. That is why Hopkins spent more time on headlines than on body copy, discarding scores before choosing one: the identical ad with a different headline can multiply returns five to ten times. Put your single best promise in it. Then submit everything to the court of public opinion — you cannot judge humanity by yourself. And once an ad works, leave it alone: you advertise to a moving parade, not a standing army.

  1. Write the headline first; spend hours on it.
  2. Aim it at the people you seek — them only.
  3. Carry the single best promise.
  4. Test: same ad, different headlines — watch the returns.
  5. When one works, don't change it.
Example · What

Scores of headlines discarded before one is chosen — and the same ad under different headlines varies five to ten times in returns.

So what: the headline is the ad's return; spend the hours there.

Example · What

"1,000 songs in your pocket" sells the whole iPod in six words.

So what: the best promise belongs in the headline — it earns the hearing.

Example · What

Weeks reading dry engineering documents, until one line: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."

So what: one buried fact can be the keystone — genius is the art of taking pains.

AnalogyA headline is a shop window on a busy street: it can't sell to everyone — it just has to stop the right person.
The headline as a door
It doesn't address the crowd — it selects the few who care, then the copy does the selling.
