AN INTERACTIVE LESSON · FROM THE FAMOUS MIT LECTURE

How to Speak

Patrick Winston’s craft of communication, rebuilt as a lesson: why talent barely matters, how to open without a joke, how to keep a fogged-out room with you — and how to make sure your ideas get the credit they deserve.

SOURCE · “HOW TO SPEAK,” PATRICK WINSTON, MIT 6 MODULES ≈ 35 MINUTES BUILT 30 JUL 2026

What you’ll learn

In this lecture — which he gave for decades at MIT — Patrick Winston argues that your success in life is determined largely by your ability to speak, then to write, then by the quality of your ideas, in that order. And communication, he insists, is not a gift; it is a formula you can work and a repertoire of techniques you can steal. This lesson rebuilds his argument as six modules, each with diagrams, three worked examples, an analogy, and something for you to do. His promise becomes ours: at least one technique in here will be the one that gets you the job.

  • Why communication quality is K × P × T — knowledge times practice times talent — and why the T is very small.
  • How to open with an empowerment promise instead of a joke, and why “no laptops” is a favor to the whole room.
  • Four heuristics that keep a drifting audience aboard: cycling, the fence, verbal punctuation, and the seven-second question.
  • How to choose the time and place — and why boards and props beat slides when the purpose is teaching.
  • The canonical slide crimes and their fixes, including the condiments rule and the hapax legomenon.
  • Special occasions: inspiring people, the five-minute job talk, Winston’s Star for getting remembered, and how to stop without saying “thank you.”
MODULE 1 · THE FOUNDATION

The Formula: Q = K × P × T

“I was a better skier because I had the K, and I had the P, and all she had was the T.”

The core idea

Winston opens with a provocation: the Uniform Code of Military Justice specifies court-martial for any officer who sends a soldier into battle without a weapon — and students deserve the same protection, because they are being sent out into life, where success will be determined largely by the ability to speak, then to write, then by the quality of ideas, in that order. The encouraging part is his formula for how good you will be at communicating: quality equals knowledge times practice times talent — Q = K × P × T — and the T, he notes, is very small.

He learned this on a ski slope at Sun Valley. Mary Lou Retton was there — the Olympic gymnast famous for perfect 10s on vault — and she was a novice skier. Winston spotted her on the beginner hill because when she lost her balance, her arms went up in a gymnast’s reflex. Then it struck him: he, an ordinary academic, was a better skier than an outstanding Olympic athlete. Why? He had the K — knowledge of how skiing works — and the P — years of practice. All she had was the T. Talent is real, but it is domain-specific and small; knowledge and practice are multiplicative and largely under your control.

That is the deal this lesson makes with you. Communication is a craft, not a gift. Winston promises that within one hour of lecture, at least one technique will “get you the job” — a non-linear payoff you cannot predict in advance. Your work is to build K and P: watch speakers you admire, ask why they are effective, and add what you find to your own armamentarium — his word for a personal toolkit of speaking techniques.

But why is the formula multiplicative, not additive?

Because the factors amplify each other rather than compensate for each other. Knowledge of what to say, multiplied by zero practice, is a talk no one hears properly — the product collapses. An additive model would let genius offset never rehearsing; the multiplicative one doesn’t, which is exactly why the brilliant professor who mumbles through unrehearsed slides is a familiar sight. The good news runs the same direction: small gains in K and P compound.

Step by step

  1. Accept the ordering. Speaking first, then writing, then ideas. The best idea, badly packaged, loses to a good idea well presented.
  2. Bank the K. Watch speakers you admire and interrogate them: why did that land? Collect the heuristics you notice.
  3. Spend the P. Practice out loud, in the real room, in front of people who don’t know your material.
  4. Discount the T. You can’t grow it much and you don’t need to. Nobody multiplies their way to a good talk with talent alone.
  5. Collect one technique at a time. The payoff is non-linear — you never know which heuristic will be the one that gets you the job.

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

The rehearsed colleague. You know this person: not the flashiest mind in the lab, but they have given the same conference talk ten times, to ten different practice audiences. At the meeting they field questions calmly while the “natural” beside them mumbles through slides they have never once seen projected. K and P, compounding quietly for years.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

The Olympic skier. Mary Lou Retton had more raw athletic talent than almost anyone on that mountain — yet a middle-aged professor out-skied her. Talent does not transfer across domains. A gymnast’s perfect 10 buys nothing on a novice slope, and a brilliant researcher’s IQ buys nothing at a podium they have never practiced at.

THE EDGE CASE

The brilliant zero. Because the formula multiplies, it is unforgiving: a researcher with encyclopedic K and near-zero P gives a talk nobody can follow — Q collapses toward nothing. This is the curse of the brilliant mumbling professor. The mirror case, all P and no K, is the smooth speaker who says nothing at all. Both are fixed the same way: multiply by the missing factor.

Visual analogy

THE CONCEPT
THE ANALOGY — BUILDING A BOOKSHELF
THE BRIDGE Talent is the thin spacer in the depth of the shelf: it is part of the volume, but you cannot stretch it much, and nobody builds a shelf around it. Knowledge and practice are the long planks — the dimensions you actually cut to size, and the ones that decide how much the shelf holds.
What people get wrong

THE MYTH“Great speakers are born, not made — and if you aren’t one, no technique will help.”

THE TRUTHThe formula makes talent the smallest factor. Winston — a famously compelling lecturer — frames his entire talk as transferable knowledge: techniques you can add to your armamentarium. People who “can’t speak” almost always have K or P at zero, not T.

WHY THE MYTH STICKSWe mistake the end state for the starting point. We see a fluent speaker and assume a gift, because the thousands of hours of practice happened offstage — and the nervous beginner comparing themselves to that finished product is comparing their K×P to someone else’s decade of it.

If talent is a rounding error, then every talk you give is winnable by preparation — and nobody is naturally out of your league.

MODULE 2 · THE FIRST FIVE MINUTES

The Opening: A Promise, Not a Joke

“At the end of the next 60 minutes, you will know things about speaking you don’t know now.”

The core idea

Most speakers believe they must break the ice with a joke. Winston disagrees, and his reasoning is mechanical rather than stylistic: in the first minutes of a talk, the audience is still putting laptops away, finding the exits, and — above all — adjusting to your vocal parameters: your pitch, pace, accent, rhythm. They are literally not tuned to receive you yet, so a joke lands on a room that isn’t listening, and it falls flat. Instead, open with what he calls an empowerment promise: tell people what they will know at the end of the hour that they didn’t know at the beginning. His own is a model of the form — by the end of this talk, he tells the room, you will have been exposed to ideas you will incorporate into your own repertoire, and they will ensure your ideas get the maximum chance of being valued and accepted. Notice what the promise does: it gives the audience a destination and a reason to pay the cost of attention.

Then come the rules of engagement: no laptops, no phones. The justification is the single language processor — humans have one, and it can be reading or listening, but not both. If it is engaged on email, the owner is distracted; worse, studies show the open screen distracts everyone around; worse still, it rattles the speaker, who then gives a worse performance for everyone who is paying attention. The rule is a favor to the whole room, not a power trip.

Step by step

  1. Write the promise first — before the slides, before the outline: “At the end of this talk you will know / be able to ___.”
  2. Make it a delta — something they don’t know now, framed as useful: “one of these may be the one that gets you the job.”
  3. Deliver it in the first sixty seconds, in plain words, before anything else.
  4. State the rules of engagement — warmly but explicitly — and give the reason: one language processor.
  5. Save the jokes for the end, when the room has finished calibrating to your voice (see Module 6).

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

The lecture’s own opening. Winston tells the room, almost verbatim: by the end of this hour you will know things about speaking you don’t know now, and one of them may make the difference in your life. That single sentence does all the work of the first five minutes — it is the reason the room keeps listening.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

The comedian’s secret. Even stand-up professionals don’t open with their best material — they open with a quick calibration bit, because the room needs a full minute to tune to the performer’s rhythm. If people whose living depends on timing skip the big laugh at minute one, your conference talk should too.

THE EDGE CASE

The post-lunch, hostile room. After lunch, in a dim hall, a vague promise (“I’ll talk about my research”) loses instantly to a concrete one (“you’ll leave with three fixes you can apply tomorrow”). The promise must name a benefit the audience can feel — because the foggiest 20 percent of the room are deciding, in the first minute, whether to stay.

Visual analogy

THE CONCEPT
THE ANALOGY — THE PILOT’S ANNOUNCEMENT
THE BRIDGE A pilot doesn’t open with a joke about airline food; they announce altitude, route, and arrival time — and two hundred people settle into their seats at once. A stated destination is what lets a room full of language processors commit to listening. Your empowerment promise is your pre-flight announcement.
What people get wrong

THE MYTH“A strong opening joke wins the room.”

THE TRUTHIn minute one the audience is calibrating, not listening; jokes fall flat. The winning move is a promise that gives them a destination. Jokes work beautifully at the end, once the room is tuned to you — the asymmetry is the whole point.

BONUS MYTH“Laptops only hurt the person using them.” Studies say otherwise: open screens distract the neighbors and degrade the speaker’s performance for everyone. The no-laptop rule protects the room.

The first minute decides whether a hundred language processors point at you — or at their inboxes.

MODULE 3 · KEEPING THE ROOM

Four Heuristics for a Fogged-Out Room

“Tell him what you want to tell him. Tell him again. And then tell him a third time.”

The core idea

Here is the operating assumption behind everything in this module: at any given moment, roughly 20 percent of your audience is fogged out — and it doesn’t matter how brilliant the lecture is. Attention is not a switch the audience flips once; it is a bus people keep falling off. So the craft lies in getting them back on, and Winston offers four heuristics for exactly that.

First, cycle on the subject: go around the idea three times — tell them what you’re going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them. It sounds like an insult to the audience’s intelligence; it is actually statistical insurance. If one pass reaches about 80 percent, three passes reach nearly everyone.

Second, build a fence around your idea so it cannot be confused with somebody else’s. Teaching a Martian what an arch is, you would point at an arch, then at the near-misses: this is not an arch, and neither is that. In technical work, the fence is what separates you from Jones: “his algorithm seems similar, except his is exponential and mine is linear.”

Third, verbal punctuation: announce landmarks and seams — “that’s the second of four ideas; now the third” — so a returning listener can locate themselves in the talk. And fourth, ask a question, then stop talking: the standard wait is seven seconds, which feels like an eternity and is exactly right. The question must be tuned — too obvious and people are embarrassed to answer, too hard and nobody has anything to say.

But why does seven seconds feel so long?

Because you, the speaker, are inside the silence with nothing to do — while the audience is busy formulating answers. Seven seconds of dead air feels eternal on stage and reads as a genuine, confident invitation in the seats. Cut it to two seconds and the room reads the question as rhetorical; stretch it past fifteen and the silence curdles into awkwardness. Seven is the standard precisely because it is uncomfortable — the discomfort is the invitation working.

Step by step

  1. Plan three passes. Write your core claim into the outline three times, in three different framings — on purpose.
  2. Fence every borrowed-looking idea. Name its nearest neighbor and state the difference in one sentence.
  3. Number your landmarks. “First… second… now the third” — and restate the outline at every seam.
  4. Ask one tuned question per section — then count seven silent seconds. Not too easy, not impossible.

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

The lecture demonstrating itself. Mid-talk, Winston says: “among these four samples, I’ve talked about the first idea — that’s cycling; the second idea, building a fence… and now, the third idea is verbal punctuation.” He is enumerating the seams out loud, live, so anyone who drifted thirty seconds ago can find their place again.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

Repetition is not for dumb audiences — it is for human ones. Even a room full of geniloses loses a fifth of its members to daydreams at any moment. Saying it three times isn’t condescension; it’s coverage. The people who complain that speakers “repeat themselves” have never been the ones who fogged out.

THE EDGE CASE

The seven-second eternity. Winston timed himself: seven seconds of dead air after a question felt endless — and it is the standard. Shorter, and the room reads the question as decoration; longer, and the silence spoils. And the question itself is a tuning problem: too obvious invites embarrassment, too hard invites silence.

Visual analogy

THE CONCEPT
THE ANALOGY — THE TOUR GUIDE AND THE STATUE
THE BRIDGE A good guide never describes the statue once and moves on — they walk the group around it, so the people stuck behind the pillar get the front view, then the profile, then the back. Cycling is not saying the same thing three times; it is giving the same idea three sightlines.
What people get wrong

THE MYTH“If I repeat myself, people will think I’m talking down to them.”

THE TRUTHAt any moment about 20% of any audience is fogged out, regardless of IQ. Repetition is probabilistic coverage, not condescension — and the sharpest listeners barely notice the extra passes.

BONUS MYTH“A pause means I’ve lost them.” The opposite: a seven-second pause after a tuned question is the standard invitation. It is what turns a rhetorical flourish into a real question.

One pass reaches about eighty percent of the room; three passes, plus landmarks and one good question, reach nearly everyone.

MODULE 4 · THE PHYSICS OF THE ROOM

Time, Place, and the Tools of the Trade

“It’s extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids.”

The core idea

Before a single word is spoken, the talk is already being shaped by the clock, the room, and the tools — and Winston treats all three as engineering variables. On time: 11 a.m. is his ideal, because by then most people are properly awake, nobody has just eaten, and nobody is yet fatigued. On place: the single most important property is light. Dim rooms tell the human brain it is time to sleep — so keep the lights full up, even when the AV person insists the slides will look better in the dark. His reply is perfect: it is extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids. The room should also be cased — visited in advance, the way a bank robber cases a bank — so there are no surprises on the day; and it should be reasonably populated, more than half full, because a sparse room makes everyone wonder what more interesting thing is happening elsewhere.

Then, tools. For informing — teaching, explaining — Winston uses a blackboard, for three reasons: graphic quality; speed (your handwriting pace roughly matches the pace at which people can absorb ideas, which slide-flipping does not); and target — the board gives your hands somewhere to point, solving the novice speaker’s sudden, mortifying awareness of their own hands. Seymour Papert pointed at the board constantly, even at things unrelated to what he was saying — and it worked. Props work better still: Ibsen’s glowing stove in Hedda Gabler, Papert’s duct-taped bicycle wheel, Lazarus’s pendulum gently kissing his nose to prove conservation of energy. Why do chalk and props beat slides for teaching? Winston’s answer: empathetic mirroring — your mirror neurons fire as if you were writing on the board, or feeling the steel ball swing. You cannot mirror a static image.

Step by step

  1. Pick the time — late morning, clear of meals and fatigue.
  2. Keep the lights full up. Dim means sleep; negotiate with AV before, not during, the talk.
  3. Case the room in advance. Walk the space, find the weirdness, intervene or adapt. Worst case, imagine the seats filled with disinterested farm animals.
  4. Size the room to the crowd — more than half full, or the emptiness becomes the story.
  5. Match the tool to the purpose: board for informing, props for physical truth, slides only for exposing.

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

The lights negotiation. Winston’s first instruction to any AV crew is “keep the lights full up.” When they counter that slides look better in the dark, he answers that it is extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids. The room’s alertness is worth more than the projector’s contrast ratio.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

Papert’s irrelevant pointing. Winston watched Papert lecture twice — once for content, once for style — and realized Papert was constantly pointing at board material that had nothing to do with what he was saying. It still worked. The gesture itself, a live human hand directing attention, carries the signal; the content being pointed at barely matters.

THE EDGE CASE

The duct-tape wheel. Spin a bicycle wheel, torque the axle, and ask which way it goes: mechanical engineers applying the right-hand rule score about fifty percent — a coin flip with a fancy education. But tape a marker on one segment and ask what happens to that piece when you blow on it, and the answer becomes obvious piece by piece. The prop doesn’t just demonstrate — it forces the right framing.

THE BRIDGE Reading a recipe photo is information; watching someone knead dough, your own hands tense in sympathy — your mirror neurons rehearse the motion. A blackboard and a swinging steel ball give the audience’s motor system something to imitate. A static slide gives it nothing to do. What people get wrong

BONUS MYTH“Slides can teach.” Slides expose ideas; boards inform. Teaching needs the audience’s mirror system engaged — live writing, live motion, physical props — and a deck of slides provides none of it.

You cannot compete with closed eyelids — the room is either your ally or your opponent, and that is decided before you say a word.

MODULE 5 · CRIME SCENE

The Slide Crimes — and the Fixes

“You have too many, and they have too many words. — How did you know? — Because it’s always true.”

The core idea

Winston’s view of slides is precise and unforgiving: slides are for exposing ideas — job talks, conference talks — not for teaching them. And the diagnosis is always the same. When a stranger at Logan Airport asked him to critique a job-talk deck, Winston answered without ever having seen it: you have too many slides, and they have too many words. How did he know? Because it is always true.

The crimes are enumerable. Reading your transparencies — the audience knows how to read, and watching you recite annoys them. Too many words — and here is the mechanism: you have one language processor, and it can read the screen or listen to the speaker, not both. A student’s experiment proved the stakes: subjects remembered what they read on the slides, not what the speaker said — and one wrote in the after-action report, “I wish you hadn’t talked so much. It was distracting.” Standing far from the screen, creating a tennis match of attention; slides should be condiments to what you say, not the main event. The laser pointer, which forces you to turn your back — you lose eye contact, and “we could all leave, and he wouldn’t know”; the fix is a baked-in arrow: “look at the guy at the end of arrow number one.” The too-heavy deck — print it out and lay it on a table, and the density becomes visible; a talk needs air. And the hapax legomenon: the impenetrable slide you may use exactly once per talk, like the infamous Afghanistan governance chart, deployed precisely to show complexity that cannot be walked through.

The fix is subtraction: strip the background junk, strip the words until only keywords remain, strip the logos, strip the title — you can say the title — and never use a font smaller than about forty points.

But why does the audience remember the slide instead of the speaker?

Because reading and listening compete for the same single language processor, and reading wins — it is self-paced, concrete, and right there. In the fraternity experiment, half the material was spoken and half was on slides, then reversed for a control group; whichever channel carried the words, the slides took the memory. Dense slides don’t reinforce your talk; they replace you, and then the audience complains that your talking was the distraction.

The fix, in order

  1. Print the deck and lay it on a table. Density you can’t see on a screen becomes obvious on paper.
  2. Strip the background junk — decoration is distraction.
  3. Strip the words — keep only keywords; say everything else out loud.
  4. Strip the logos and the title — you are the title.
  5. Bake in arrows — retire the laser pointer and keep your eye contact.
  6. Enforce a minimum font (~40 pt). Small fonts exist to smuggle in too many words.

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

The airport diagnosis. A stranger in Terminal A, en route to give a job talk, asks Winston to critique his slides. Winston has never seen the deck, but he knows: too many slides, too many words — “because it’s always true.” Most decks are one subtraction away from competence.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

Dense slides don’t reinforce your talk — they replace you. In the experiment, the audience remembered the slide text better than the spoken words, then complained that the speaker’s talking was distracting. Every word you put on the screen is a word they stop hearing from you.

THE EDGE CASE

The hapax legomenon. One slide per talk may be impenetrable — the Afghanistan chart, shown precisely because it cannot be walked through, to demonstrate impossibility. One is a rhetorical device; two is a pattern of laziness. The same rule licenses a rare text-heavy historical slide — like the 1957 Dartmouth proposal — provided you give the audience time to read it.

MODULE 6 · HIGH STAKES

Job Talks, Fame, and the Sendoff

“Your ideas are like your children. You don’t want them to go into the world in rags.”

The core idea

The final module handles the occasions where the stakes are highest. First, inspiring: when Winston surveyed everyone from incoming freshmen to senior faculty about what inspired them, three answers emerged — someone who told them they could do it; someone who helped them see a problem in a new way; and, from everyone, someone who exhibited passion about what they were doing. Passion is contagious and, unlike charisma, it is honest: talk about what you think is cool.

Then the job talk. Winston asked two seasoned reviewers what they look for in a faculty candidate, and the answers came back in a microsecond: vision — a problem somebody cares about, plus something new in your approach — and evidence that you’ve done something. How long does a candidate have to establish both? Five minutes. The structure follows: open with the vision, enumerate the steps needed to realize it (you needn’t have done them all — say what needs doing, what is done, and demonstrate), and close by enumerating contributions.

Then getting famous — or rather, packaging, because your ideas are your children and you don’t send them into the world in rags. Winston’s Star names the five S-words that made his arch work famous: a Symbol (the arch itself), a Slogan (“one-shot learning”), a Surprise (you don’t need a million examples), a Salient idea (the near miss — not the most important idea, the one that sticks out), and a Story (how it works and why it matters).

And finally, how to stop. The last slide should not be a collaborator list (that belongs on slide one), not “Thank you,” not “Questions?” — it should be labeled Contributions, because it stays up for twenty minutes during Q&A. And the last word shouldn’t be “thank you” either — it implies the audience endured you out of politeness. End with a joke (people think they had fun the whole time), a benediction, or a salute to the audience.

Step by step

  1. Inspire with three levers: “you can do it,” a new angle on an old problem, and visible passion for what is cool.
  2. Spend the first five minutes of a job talk establishing vision plus done-something; enumerate the steps; promise the demo.
  3. Package with the five S’s — Symbol, Slogan, Surprise, Salient idea, Story.
  4. Put collaborators on the first slide and contributions on the last — and never let “thank you” be your final word.

Three ways it shows up

THE OBVIOUS CASE

Winston’s stump speech. Vision in one breath: what makes us different from chimpanzees and Neanderthals — we are symbolic creatures who string symbols into stories. Then the steps: specify the behavior, enumerate the constraints, build a system (engineers don’t believe they understand something until they can build it), and demonstrate it. Vision and done-something, side by side.

THE COUNTER-INTUITIVE CASE

The collaborator list belongs on the first slide, not the last. A giant roll-call at the end reads as a letdown — divide your work by a thousand faculty and it looks tiny, and the audience wonders whether you did anything at all. Acknowledge people at the start; end on what you did.

THE EDGE CASE

The practice-audience trap. Rehearsing for your advisor backfires: people who know your work hallucinate content that isn’t in the talk. You need friends who don’t know the material — and you start the session by saying, “if you can’t make me cry, I won’t value you as a friend anymore.” Then the actual committee feels easy.

Visual analogy

THE CONCEPT
THE ANALOGY — SENDING YOUR CHILD TO SCHOOL
THE BRIDGE You wouldn’t send your child into the world in rags with no name on their coat — you’d make sure they could be recognized and claimed. A slogan is the name tag; a symbol is the coat. The Star isn’t vanity; it is how the world grabs hold of value that is already there.
What people get wrong

THE MYTH“Good work speaks for itself — packaging is for shallow people.”

THE TRUTHWinston’s reply is Julia Child’s: being famous, “you get used to it” — but you never get used to being ignored. Packaging doesn’t inflate the work; it makes the work’s value legible. The arch was good before the Star; the Star is why anyone remembers it.

BONUS MYTH“Ending with ‘thank you’ is polite.” It suggests the audience stayed out of politeness, wishing they were elsewhere. End with a joke, a benediction, or a salute — and let the applause do the thanking.

The last slide stays up for twenty minutes during Q&A — that is prime real estate, and “thank you” leaves it empty.

THE ONE-PAGER

Summary & Cheat Sheet

Everything in the lesson, compressed to a page you could tape above a desk.

THE FORMULAQ = K × P × T — knowledge times practice times talent, and the T is very small. Multiplication means a zero anywhere zeroes everything.
THE OPENINGNo joke in minute one — the room is still calibrating. Open with an empowerment promise: what they will know at the end that they don’t know now. State the rules of engagement: one language processor, so no laptops.
FOUR HEURISTICSCycle the subject three times · build a fence around your idea (“his is exponential, mine is linear”) · use verbal punctuation (“now the third of four”) · ask a tuned question and wait seven seconds.
TIME & PLACE11 a.m. · lights full up (“hard to see slides through closed eyelids”) · case the room in advance · more than half full.
TOOLSBoard for informing (graphic, pace-matched, a target for hands) · props for physical truth (empathetic mirroring) · slides only for exposing.
SLIDE RULESNever read them · few words, ≥ 40 pt · strip junk, logos, titles · bake in arrows instead of a laser · let the deck breathe · one hapax legomenon per talk, at most.
THE JOB TALKFive minutes to show vision (a problem people care about + a new approach) and done-something (enumerate the steps, mark what’s done, demo). Practice with friends who don’t know the work.
WINSTON’S STARSymbol · Slogan · Surprise · Salient idea · Story. Your ideas are your children — don’t send them out in rags.
THE SENDOFFCollaborators on slide one. Final slide: Contributions. Final words: a joke, a benediction, or a salute — never “thank you” as the last word.

STILL CONFUSED?

Frequently Asked Questions

Isn’t repeating myself three times insulting to a smart audience?

No — it is statistical insurance for a human one. Winston’s operating assumption is that about 20% of any audience is fogged out at any moment, regardless of brilliance; attention is a bus people keep falling off. One pass reaches roughly 80% of listeners; three passes, framed differently, reach nearly everyone. The sharpest people in the room barely notice the extra orbits, and the people who needed them will never know you saved them.

What if my venue forces me to use slides for teaching?

Then make the slides behave like a board: one keyword at a time, revealed progressively, at the pace you would write them by hand. Give the audience explicit reading time on the rare text slide, and keep yourself — not the screen — as the main event. The underlying principle is empathetic mirroring: live, paced, hand-made motion engages the audience’s motor system in a way that a wall of static text never can.

Does the seven-second pause really work on video calls?

It works even better, because silence reads as longer through a screen than in a room — speakers routinely panic and answer their own question after two seconds, which teaches the audience not to bother answering. Count the full seven. The rule’s logic is unchanged: the pause is what converts a rhetorical flourish into a real question, and the question is what pulls fogged-out listeners back onto the bus.

What if my whole talk is only five minutes?

Everything compresses but nothing disappears. The empowerment promise becomes one sentence (“in five minutes you’ll know the one fix for…”). Cycling becomes a single orbit plus a fence. Verbal punctuation becomes two landmarks instead of four. And the last slide is still Contributions — five minutes is exactly the window Winston says job-talk reviewers decide in, so the discipline is identical at any length.

Is saying “thank you” really that bad?

You will not go to hell for it — Winston says so himself. It is a weak move, not a sin: “thank you for listening” implies the audience stayed out of politeness, enduring you rather than choosing you. Once the applause has started, mouthing a thank-you is fine. But the last thing you say should be a joke, a benediction, or a salute to the audience — something that ends on your terms, not on an apology for having taken up their time.

LEAVING THE ROOM

Next Steps

  • Watch three talks you admire this week and write down one technique from each — that is how you bank the K.
  • Record your next practice run. Count your pauses, and check whether your questions are tuned — not too easy, not impossible.
  • Print your current deck, lay it on a table, and run the too-heavy check. Subtraction is the fastest improvement available to you.
  • Draft your own Winston’s Star for your current project: symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, story.
  • Assemble two naive friends for a practice session — and start it with the crying rule.
  • Watch the original lecture: Patrick Winston’s “How to Speak,” MIT — the source of every idea in this lesson.
