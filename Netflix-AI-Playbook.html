Now · The Shift

The Storming Phase: When Everyone Can Be Everything

PMs ship code. Designers write PRDs. Engineers "product." And everybody asks: what is my job anymore?

Elizabeth opens with a line that will feel familiar to almost anyone working in tech right now: "Everyone can be everything now." Product managers can ship working prototypes. Designers can draft product requirement documents. Engineers can weigh in on product strategy. And underneath all that new capability sits a very human feeling — confusion, even frustration, about what you are actually responsible for anymore.

Her framing borrows from the classic team-development model: every new technology pushes an organization through a storming phase before it reaches a forming phase. Storming is messy, loud, and full of identity questions. That is normal, and it is where we are right now. The mistake would be to panic and "put AI back into the box." The smarter move is to capture the benefits of experimentation while deliberately reducing the costs.

At Netflix that means three guardrails, repeated in almost every part of the conversation: clarity on the source of truth for data, guardrails on shipping to production (test before large changes), and a constant re-statement that humans remain responsible for outcomes. If an agent wrote the code, a human still owns what it does in production. Fluidity across roles is healthy when the business problem is clear — but it works only because the accountability never gets fluid.

Step-by-Step: Moving Through the Storm

  1. AI accelerates prototyping. Anyone can sketch an idea, write an initial script, or assemble a first version of an analysis without waiting for a specialist team.
  2. Role confusion hits. "If I can do their job, what is my job?" The storming phase is where identity questions surface — and they surface loudly.
  3. Teams experiment broadly. Product and design move faster on early shaping; engineers are told what is coming so it isn't a surprise.
  4. Guardrails arrive. Source-of-truth data, review before production changes, and clear ownership of outcomes keep experimentation from becoming chaos.
  5. Storming settles into forming. A new division of labor emerges — more fluid, but still anchored in expertise and accountability.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

A product manager prototypes a feature with AI-generated code and shows it to users weeks before the engineering team would have had a sprint slot. Speed up, confusion included.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

Business stakeholders in finance, content, and advertising — not data scientists — use AI to distill past experiments and bring back an initial hypothesis. The "data person" isn't replaced; the doorway moved earlier.

Edge case · where it can break

An AI agent writes code that passes tests but nobody fully understands. The human still owns the production quality — which is precisely why understanding, not just generating, remains the job.

◆ Visual Analogy: A Kitchen Remodel

What storming actually feels like

During a kitchen remodel, the house is a mess — countertops stacked in the hallway, three tradespeople in one room, nothing where it used to be. That's storming. Nobody concludes the kitchen is obsolete. You put up dust barriers (guardrails), agree on the plan (source of truth), and eventually the chaos resolves into a kitchen that works better than before. The remodel is the point — not the mess, and not the old kitchen either.

Elizabeth's answer is emphatic: the storming phase is temporary, and it is a phase — not the destination. The roles aren't dying; they're being re-plumbed. The real risk isn't that everyone becomes redundant — it's that organizations grab the benefits of AI without the guardrails, and end up with the costs: chaos, low-quality output, and nobody accountable.

Why This Matters

If you're confused about your job right now, you are not behind — you're in the storm, exactly where the model predicts. Understanding that this is a transition, not a verdict, tells you what to do next: keep experimenting, keep your discipline's lens sharp, and make sure your name is still attached to outcomes.

The Craft Endures: Why Specialists Still Matter

Everyone can build now. Great engineering, great data science, and great creativity are still scarce.

Here is the question underneath the whole episode: if we all become builders, do we still need separate functions? Elizabeth's answer is a confident yes — and it's not nostalgia. AI has removed a lot of friction: finding an old experiment, pulling together an analysis, drafting a first prototype. But it hasn't removed judgment. Each discipline still holds a distinctive lens that the tools don't have.

A data scientist is still the person who asks whether the data can be trusted and how much is data versus judgment. A product manager is still the person who checks whether the what — the problem being framed — is actually the right problem. An engineer still owns the how: does this scale, what does high quality look like, what problems will this create when we deploy it? A designer still owns coherence — what a good experience looks like and how it fits the whole. Those are the "nuggets of comparative advantage," and Elizabeth calls the expertise behind them craft excellence.

She also noticed something about the last two and a half years: the roles that changed most are the ones that used to be bottlenecks early in the product lifecycle. PMs, designers, and data scientists now get much further — prototyping, writing code, shaping ideas — before engineering needs to be "front of the line." But that only works when the problem is important, the engineering partner is aware, and people aren't "throwing spaghetti at the wall." The roles that changed least? The ones built on deep, hard-to-fake judgment — which is exactly why that judgment got more scarce, not less.

Step-by-Step: Fluidity With Anchors

  1. Remove the friction. AI answers "what research did we do, and what did we learn?" in seconds instead of email chains.
  2. Move further, faster. Product, design, and data science shape testable hypotheses earlier in the lifecycle.
  3. Hold the lens. Each function still applies its comparative advantage — trust, framing, scale, coherence.
  4. Keep the partner in the loop. Prototypes aren't spaghetti; engineers know the problem being solved before work starts.
  5. Recognize scarcity. Craft excellence didn't dissolve — it became the differentiator between good and great output.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

An engineer is still the one who asks whether a service scales to millions of streams and what breaks when it deploys. The "how" is craft.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

Designers now write code and PMs prototype — yet the scarce talent is more design-systems thinking, not less. Enabling non-designers to build coherent products requires stronger design expertise at the core.

Edge case · where it can break

The "member of technical staff" trend — no title, be anything — sounds like the end of specialties. Elizabeth's take: the disciplines still carry distinct judgment that generalists borrow but don't replace.

◆ Visual Analogy: Synthesizers, Not Musicians

What a tool does — and doesn't do

Every musician can now buy the same synthesizer and make a passable track in an afternoon. Yet great players are still scarce — because the tool generates sounds, not taste. Knowing what good sounds like, when a chord change serves the song, and how to fix a mix that's gone wrong is the craft. AI is the synthesizer; craft is still the musician.

The opposite happened. Elizabeth says great engineering, great data science, and great creativity are still scarce — and craft excellence is the thing she finds herself defending at every level. What changed is that specialists now speak more languages (the tools gave them that). What didn't change is the judgment underneath.

Why This Matters

For your career: the way to stay valuable is not to become generic — it's to keep sharpening the lens only your discipline has, while adding the ability to move fluidly across steps that used to be blocked. If you can do that, you're not competing with the tools. You're the person deciding whether the tools are solving the right problem.

Check Your Understanding

After two and a half years of AI, which roles moved furthest in the product lifecycle before engineering was needed?

Correct. AI removed friction early in the lifecycle, so product, design, and data science get to testable hypotheses faster — with engineering aware of the problem. The engineering "how" still gates scale and quality.

Systems Thinking: The Skill Everyone Needs

Fewer narrow specialists. More people who can look across domains and find the shared building blocks.

When Lenny asks what Netflix is hiring more of, Elizabeth doesn't name a function — she names a mindset: systems thinkers. People who can look across all the business domains, abstract the pattern, and say, "here are the building blocks we're going to need." She gives two concrete versions. In engineering, that means more distributed-systems and infrastructure profiles building paved paths — common infrastructure that solves a problem once so local teams don't each rebuild their own stack. In design, it means more people building design systems and templates so that everyone, designers or not, ships experiences that feel like one coherent Netflix — instead of "Frankensteins."

Why now? Three forces. First, velocity: platforms give most teams 80% of the way there, and with more bets being made across the business, leverage matters more than ever. Second, agents: as AI agents do real work across multiple systems, someone has to provide the scaffolding — context, access, identity, security, and a shared notion of what "good" looks like. Third, scale kills tribal knowledge: an organization of thousands can no longer rely on "find the one person who knows this." That was already true before AI; AI made it urgent.

The counterweight is that narrow, deep specialization feels more limited than it did 5–10 years ago. Netflix still needs a few world-class experts — encoding, playback, payments — but the general rule has flipped: more adaptable generalists, more people who can say "I can learn that quickly," and specialists who still ask whether the old tool is still the right tool.

Step-by-Step: Developing the Skill

  1. Take your task. Whatever you're building — a feature, a model, a campaign.
  2. Step out one click. Ask: what am I assuming is true about the broader space? Not the whole company strategy — just one zoom out.
  3. Test for scale. Would this approach work across multiple content types, teams, or platforms? Could it become a shared capability?
  4. Build or borrow the paved path. Use the shared building blocks — or contribute a new one so the next team doesn't rebuild it.
  5. Leave it stronger. Would a colleague — or your manager — be better off because of how you solved this?

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

Central infrastructure builds a paved path for authentication and data access so dozens of local teams don't each invent their own — and don't each create a security risk.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

Designers get hired less for "design this one feature" and more for building templates and brand systems — so non-designers can ship coherent experiences. Systems thinking replaced pixel-pushing as the core ask.

Edge case · where it can break

A payments expert is still valuable — provided they keep asking whether the old tooling is still the right way to think about the problem. Subject-matter depth + willingness to question = the new specialist.

◆ Visual Analogy: City Planning vs. Building One House

The systems-thinking zoom-out

A homeowner asks: how do I make my kitchen efficient? A city planner asks: if a thousand homes are built, where do roads, water, and power go so none of them collapses into chaos? Systems thinking is the planner's move — you don't design the whole city, but you design so the city can exist. That's the "one click out."

◆ Toggle Analogy

Two ways to see the same skill

Elizabeth's second trick is even more practical: think about how doing your job helps your manager do theirs. If you consider not just product and tech but finance, content, and the rest of the business, you naturally zoom out — and you start seeing how the pieces need to come together, and how the whole can beat the sum of its parts. Engineers get the same effect by asking: did I leave a better version of this system for my colleagues?

No — Elizabeth is explicit: "You don't have to boil the whole ocean." You don't solve Netflix's overall strategy or competitive position. You take the thing you're responsible for and zoom out exactly one level, question your assumptions, and get back to work. And don't stay in the questioning state too long — otherwise you're stuck and not making progress.

Why This Matters

This is the skill showing up in hiring, in promotions, and in how work gets done. It's also the through-line of the whole episode: systems thinking is how you build the paved paths, the guardrails, and the shared building blocks that let hundreds of people and thousands of agents move fast without turning the company into chaos.

AI Fluency: Ladders, Hiring & Learning

An aspiration layered over every level — not a new set of level-specific requirements.

Netflix famously had no career ladders for most of its history — it was a high-trust, low-process culture. When they did add ladders, the question became: how do AI expectations change them? Elizabeth's answer is deliberately structural: instead of rewriting each level's criteria, Netflix layered an AI fluency aspiration across all talent, at every level, in every function.

What is AI fluency? It's a "tough thing to define," she admits, and it varies by function and career stage. It means an experimentation mindset. It means knowing where AI is useful and where it isn't — tech where it's useful, not tech for tech's sake. It means having actually built something with AI. And it applies at the most senior levels, too: executives need deep fluency even if they never write code. The definition evolves "by the quarter, if not month or day," because the technology itself moves that fast — which is exactly why it shouldn't be nailed to specific levels or roles.

The same overlay shows up in hiring: interviews now explore how candidates use AI, how comfortable they are with change, and coding interviews explicitly allow AI tools, because that's what the job now requires. And junior talent? Netflix still hires juniors — interns and new grads, a program that only started a few years ago. Younger people tend to be more open-minded and more native to new ways of working. But craft mastery is still the point: juniors still own quality, still review and test code, still learn what good looks like through mentorship. It runs both directions — earlier-career talent will teach older folks plenty.

Step-by-Step: How AI Fluidity Shows Up

  1. Set an aspiration, not a checklist. AI fluency is an overlay on all ladders — varied by function and level, never a fixed requirement list.
  2. Define it by behavior. Experimentation mindset, knowing where AI helps, having built something — these are the observable signals.
  3. Change hiring to match. Interviews explore AI use and comfort with change; coding interviews allow AI tools.
  4. Keep investing in juniors. Interns and new grads bring openness and fresh perspective — and still learn craft through ownership and mentorship.
  5. Expect it to keep moving. The definition shifts by the quarter. Fluency is a posture, not a certificate.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

A coding interview now lets candidates use AI tools — because that's literally how the work gets done. The interview tests judgment over rote recall.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

AI fluency isn't a junior skill. Executives at the senior-most levels of Netflix are expected to have deep fluency even when they don't write code as part of their day jobs.

Edge case · where it can break

Agent-written code can be faster but "very hard to follow" — you get better performance but can't explain why, and can't fix it when it breaks. The learning curve of rationalizing agent output is one of the steepest right now.

◆ Visual Analogy: Driving With a GPS

You don't build the engine — but you must know when it's broken

The GPS tells you every turn and you never look under the hood. But when the check-engine light comes on at midnight, the GPS can't help you. Elizabeth's version: you may not write lines of code in Python or C++, but you must understand how code, computer systems, and products work — so you can tell if the output is good, and fix it when it isn't. Understanding is the engine you can't outsource.

Flashcards: Test Your Recall

Elizabeth's concern is the opposite: with agents doing the writing, people may stop learning. But mastery is still scarce, and the company still invests in teaching what good looks like. The difference between writing code and understanding systems is the difference between generating output and being accountable for it.

Why This Matters

If you're evaluating your own career: fluency is a behavior, not a badge. Keep experimenting, keep building small things with AI, keep being able to say where it helps and where it hurts. That's the overlay every ladder is looking for — and it's the thing that will keep you hirable as the definition keeps moving.

Now · The Tools

AI at Netflix: From Prize to Production

Recommendations are the visible tip. The real leverage is in data distillation, production, and post-production.

Ask people what AI does at Netflix and they'll say: the recommendation row. Elizabeth's point is that this is the oldest chapter of a much longer story. Netflix ran the Netflix Prize in 2006–2009 — a $1 million open competition to improve the recommendation algorithm by a few percentage points — making it one of the earliest companies to bet its product on machine learning at scale. (Lenny notes that back then, people insisted, "that's not AI, it's just machine learning" — and now we call it all AI. The label changed; the work didn't.)

Personalization is still central, and it gets harder as the catalog grows: films, TV, games, live events, podcasts. The problem — right title, right person, right moment — only becomes more important with more breadth. But the two use cases Elizabeth highlights as most impactful today go well beyond recommendations.

First, data analysis and distillation — her personal favorite. Netflix has decades of experiments, consumer insights, and stakeholder input. AI is "very powerful at distilling information, looking across a broad set of things" — so instead of emailing someone to ask what research exists, you find it instantly and jump straight to "is there something actionable here?" Second, content creation and production: generating promotional assets at scale, localizing subtitles and dubs, pre-visualization (bringing a creator's vision to life before the set), and post-production tools — Netflix acquired Inner Positive, founded by Ben Affleck, which can relight, reframe, reshoot, and change dialogue after filming. In every case, the filmmaker still leads; the tools amplify the vision.

Step-by-Step: From Data to Your Screen

  1. Distill. AI synthesizes decades of experiments and research into starting insights — a head start, not the final answer.
  2. Personalize. Match the right title to the right person at the right moment — harder with every new format added.
  3. Produce. Pre-visualization lets creators see the vision before the set; assets and localization are generated at scale.
  4. Post-produce. After shooting, tools can relight, reframe, reshoot, and change dialogue — always guided by the filmmaker.
  5. Connect. Trailers, images, and artwork at scale help titles find their audiences around the world.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

The recommendation algorithm: personalize the right title for the right person at the right moment — the problem the Netflix Prize kicked off in 2006.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

Post-production: Inner Positive (from Ben Affleck) can relight, reframe, reshoot, and change dialogue after a scene is already shot — a whole new creative layer that has nothing to do with recommendations.

Edge case · where it can break

Localization at scale — subtitles and dubs for every market — is a step-function leap, but only works if the results are checked and the source-of-truth data is right. Speed without verification is just fast noise.

◆ Visual Analogy: The Library & the Librarian

Why distillation is a superpower

Netflix's institutional memory is a library with decades of research, every experiment ever run, and notes from every stakeholder. Before AI, using it meant finding the one person who remembered where things were. AI is the librarian who has read everything: ask a question in plain language, get the relevant volumes in seconds. The librarian gives you a head start — you still have to decide what the books mean for your problem.

Recommendations are one chapter of a 20-year story. The high-impact uses today include data distillation (finding decades of experiments in seconds), pre-visualization (seeing the vision before the set), post-production (relighting, reframing, changing dialogue via Inner Positive), and localization at scale. AI is "at the center" of every part of the business — even though Netflix isn't branded as an AI company.

Why This Matters

The breadth of entertainment is exploding — films, TV, games, live, podcasts. Every new format makes discovery harder, which makes AI more essential, not less. Understanding AI's full footprint changes how you think about where value is created: not just in the algorithm, but in every step that gets a story from a creator's mind to your screen.

Always · The Culture

Excellence as an Operating System

High agency, talent density, autonomy, top-of-market pay — the values that made Netflix, now echoed by the top AI labs.

Lenny points out something striking: the early Netflix culture deck — high agency, autonomy, talent density, bottom-up thinking, fast experiments, top-of-market pay — is basically the operating manual of today's frontier AI labs. Elizabeth's response reframes the whole thing: those cultural elements were never the goal. The goal was excellence, and the culture was the operating system designed to produce it.

Calling it an operating system is precise. Inputs are processed in a specific order. First, talent density is the non-negotiable — you can't have confidence in decisions at every level without exceptional people at every level. Second, push decisions as deep as possible, with real accountability — people carry "a lot of keys," and knowing you're trusted makes you want to do your best work. Third, get comfortable with risk: not avoiding failure, but recovering quickly. The live-events launch was "a wonderful example" — imperfect, fast, and the team learned and got better. Fourth, selflessness: it's about Netflix and its members, not personal preference or personal success.

Then come the parts that are "unnatural for humans." When you disagree with a decision that isn't material, you let people make it and learn — you don't veto. When planning gets hard, you resist adding process: Elizabeth has never heard anyone say they found the perfect planning system, but she has seen more process add time without better outcomes. And the keeper test is the feedback hygiene that holds it together — used as much to say "I would fight so hard to keep you" as to say "we've really tried and I don't see the path." The corollary: if a person leaving would feel like relief, you should have had that conversation sooner.

Step-by-Step: Running the System

  1. Hire dense. Talent density is the non-negotiable input — everything else assumes you started here.
  2. Push decisions deep. Give people real keys and real accountability, and let judgment live where the work is.
  3. Take risks, recover fast. Don't avoid failure — blameless retros and fast learning beat protective process.
  4. Resist the process reflex. When things go wrong, the instinct is to add rules. The discipline is to ask: is there a more creative way that gets better outcomes?
  5. Run the keeper test both ways. Affirm the extraordinary — and have the hard conversation when someone isn't passing, sooner rather than later.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

Netflix's live events push: a massive, risky launch that everyone knew would be imperfect — taken on anyway, learned from fast, and made better.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

When planning is hard, the fix is not more process. Adding constraints to a hard problem feels like simplifying it — but Elizabeth's experience: more process, more time, same or worse outcomes.

Edge case · where it can break

The keeper test is usually read as a firing tool — but it's equally used as a way to tell someone "you're extraordinary, here's where you shine, and here's how to be even better." The positive half of the coin is how you keep great people.

◆ Visual Analogy: The Engine That Runs on Trust

An operating system needs all its parts

Talent is the fuel — without it, nothing runs. Autonomy is the throttle — it converts fuel into motion. Accountability is the steering — it keeps the motion pointed at outcomes for members. Risk tolerance is the cooling system — it lets the engine run hot without seizing up. Excellence is not a component; it's what the whole system produces when every part is tuned. Remove the fuel and no amount of throttle helps.

It's cited that way, but Elizabeth says it's used equally to celebrate the extraordinary: "How am I doing on your keeper test?" — and the lion's share of the time the answer is, "I would fight so hard to keep you," followed by strengths, impact, and how to be even better. It's feedback hygiene and an anchor for honest conversation — in both directions.

Why This Matters

If you lead a team or are building one, this module is the playbook the frontier AI labs are copying — high agency, dense talent, decisions pushed deep. The uncomfortable parts are the point: letting people make decisions you'd make differently, resisting the process reflex, and having the keeper-test conversation in both directions. Culture like this is "constant diligence," not a one-time setup — and it's exactly what attracts and keeps the people who make everything else in this lesson possible.

Next 5–10 Years · The Horizon

The Future of Entertainment: One Seamless Journey

Film, TV, games, live, podcasts, vertical clips — with humans still at the heart of every story.

What does entertainment look like in five to ten years? Elizabeth's answer is that it's already changing: entertainment is not going to be one thing. Netflix is going beyond film and TV into games, live events, podcasts, and a vertical video feed ("Clips") — because consumers expect variety across formats, devices, and moments of the day. The experience she describes is a seamless journey: you listen to a podcast in the morning, watch a documentary at lunch, play a cloud game on your phone on the commute — all on one service, across TV and mobile, with discovery that feels effortless.

That seamless layer is the hard problem. More content means more fragmentation, and "we've got to make discovery and engagement much easier than it feels today." This is exactly where the earlier modules connect: systems thinking builds the platform, AI fluency runs it, and the culture's tolerance for risk lets Netflix launch imperfect new formats (like live) and iterate. The product/tech/design trinity's job is to make "a lot of complexity invisible."

On AI in Hollywood, Elizabeth describes a spectrum, not a war: some creators say absolutely no AI; a growing number want to explore what gen-AI makes possible; most sit in between. Netflix's stance is creator enablement — support whatever tools each creator wants, never prescriptive. And on the question of fully AI-generated entertainment: "I have a hard time picturing entertainment that doesn't have humans at the heart of it." Storytelling is "one and the same with humanity" — the scarce, valuable skill is knowing what connects with people. AI will amplify stories; it won't replace the storyteller.

Step-by-Step: Building the Journey

  1. Expand the formats. Film and TV plus games, live, podcasts, and vertical video — entertainment becomes many things.
  2. Cross the devices. The same journey flows from TV to mobile, matching moments of the day.
  3. Solve discovery. With breadth comes fragmentation — making it easy to find the next thing is the core challenge.
  4. Enable every creator. Support the no-AI creators, the explorers, and everyone between — never one prescriptive way.
  5. Keep humans at the heart. AI amplifies storytelling; it doesn't replace the human understanding of what connects.

Three Examples

Obvious · everyday version

Tonight you open Netflix, watch a movie, and it's over. That single-format evening is already a minority of how entertainment is consumed — and shrinking.

Counter-intuitive · the twist

The future journey runs across formats: Bill Simmons podcast in the morning → Quarterback documentary at lunch → FIFA cloud game on your phone on the train. Same service, many worlds, one thread.

Edge case · where it can break

A creator who refuses AI entirely still gets fully supported. The spectrum is the strategy: enablement, not enforcement, is what keeps the broadest range of stories coming.

◆ Visual Analogy: One Ticket, Many Stages

A festival, not a single screen

Imagine a festival with dozens of stages — a main theater, a live arena, a game hall, a podcast tent, a short-film corner. One wristband, and you wander between stages depending on mood and time of day. The magic isn't any single stage; it's that wandering feels effortless. That's the future Netflix is building — and discovery is the festival map that makes it work.

Elizabeth is direct: "I have a hard time picturing entertainment that doesn't have humans at the heart of it." Storytelling is "one and the same with humanity" — knowing what connects with people is a scarce, valuable skill. AI will play a material part in production and polish, but watching characters without that humanity is "less compelling." Technology amplifies the story; it doesn't replace the storyteller.

Why This Matters

This reframes what product and tech are actually building: not another screen, but the seamlessness between screens and formats. It's also the through-line back to module one — the storming phase will produce these new formats, and the forming phase will decide which ones survive. The teams that combine systems thinking, craft, AI fluency, and a risk-tolerant culture are the ones that will define what entertainment becomes.

◆ Toggle Analogy

The "tell me a story" test

There's a quote (misattributed to Salman Rushdie) that Elizabeth loves: when a child is born, they first ask for food and water and projection — and then they ask, "tell me a story." Storytelling predates every screen, every platform, every algorithm. Technology can amplify and reinvent how stories reach us; it can't replace the human need they answer.

FAQ

Does Elizabeth think AI will replace engineers?
No — but it changes what engineers do. You may not write lines of code, but you must understand how systems work: to judge whether output is good, and to fix it when it breaks. Agent-written code is often faster but harder to follow — rationalizing it is the steep new learning curve.
What exactly is the keeper test?
Ask: would I fight to keep this person? It's used in both directions — to celebrate extraordinary performance ("I would fight so hard to keep you" + growth feedback) and to force the hard conversation when someone isn't passing. Corollary: if someone leaving would feel like relief, act sooner.
Is Netflix actually using AI in production and post-production?
Yes, extensively. Examples include pre-visualization (seeing a vision before the set), localization (subtitles/dubs at scale), promotional assets and trailers, and post-production via Inner Positive (founded by Ben Affleck) — relighting, reframing, reshooting, and changing dialogue after filming, always led by the filmmaker.
What does "step out one click" actually look like day to day?
Take the task you own and zoom out exactly one level: what am I assuming is true about the broader space? Does this scale across content types? Could it be a shared capability? Don't boil the whole ocean and don't stay in the questioning state too long — one click, then get back to work.
Does everyone at Netflix need to be an AI expert now?
No — everyone needs AI fluency, which varies by function and level: an experimentation mindset, knowing where AI is useful (and where it isn't), and having built something with it. Even executives are expected to be fluent — but nobody has to be a model builder.

Next Steps

01

Do the one-click-out exercise this week. Pick one task you own and write down what you're assuming about the broader space. Then check: does your approach scale across teams or content types?

02

Audit your paved paths. What building blocks does your team keep re-creating? Find one shared capability worth building once — and volunteer to build it.

03

Run the keeper test as a positive ritual. Ask your manager (or a teammate) the question — and be ready to say, concretely, why you'd fight to keep the people around you.
