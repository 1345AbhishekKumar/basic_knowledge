What Is Psychology?

The child of philosophy and literature

The Big Idea

Psychology is the study of the mind and behaviour. Its name comes from Greek: psyche (mind or soul) + logos (study). It was born from two parents — philosophy (the father) and literature (the mother) — and it inherits from both.

The Two Parents

Think of psychology as a family business. The father and mother bring very different skills.

Philosophy (Father)Literature (Mother)
Core traitReason, logic, analysisEmotion, hope, meaning
Asks"Why?" — Why do we die? What is real? What is a good life?"It's okay — the story continues."
MethodArguments, thought experimentsNarrative, characters, catharsis
ResultUnderstandingComfort

Psychology is what happens when the father's questioning mind marries the mother's storytelling heart. It asks how the mind works, and it uses stories (case studies, metaphors, narratives) to explain what it finds.

Analogy: The Same Question, Three Ways

Philosophy: "Why do we dream?"
Literature: "Dreams are beautiful messages from the soul."
Psychology: "How does the sleeping brain generate dreams, what do they do for memory and emotion, and why do we need them?"

Why Did Humans Invent Philosophy At All?

Here's the deepest answer you'll find in this course:

We invented philosophy because we know we're going to die.

Animals live in the present. A deer doesn't lie awake at night thinking about its own mortality. But humans evolved self-awareness — the ability to imagine the future, including our own absence from it. That knowledge is terrifying.

So we built defences:

  • Religion — "You'll live on in an afterlife."
  • Stories — "The story continues after you."
  • Legacy — "Your children, your work, your name will carry on."
The Root of Everything

You can trace psychology all the way back to one fact: we are the only animal that knows it will die. Everything else — gods, myths, philosophies, therapies — flows from our attempt to cope with that.

Deep Dive: Terror Management Theory

Modern psychologists Jeff Greenberg, Tom Pyszczynski, and Sheldon Solomon built a whole research programme around this idea, called Terror Management Theory. Their finding: when people are reminded of death (even subtly — like walking past a funeral home), they cling harder to their cultural worldview, defend their group more aggressively, and punish outsiders more harshly.

In one famous study, judges who were reminded of mortality gave harsher sentences to a prostitute. The logic: "Death is scary — so I must be right, and others must be wrong."

This is psychology doing what the father and mother do together: taking a deep philosophical question ("What does it mean to know you'll die?") and testing it with experiments.

The Four Big Questions Psychology Tries to Answer

  1. How does the mind work? (perception, memory, attention, thought)
  2. Why do people behave the way they do? (motivation, emotion, instinct, learning)
  3. What goes wrong? (mental illness, trauma, disorder, suffering)
  4. How can we help? (therapy, education, social change, self-knowledge)

What Psychology Is NOT

Common Myths

Myth 1: "Psychology is just common sense."
Truth: Common sense is often wrong. For centuries, common sense said the Earth was the centre of the universe.

Myth 2: "Psychologists can read your mind."
Truth: They study patterns. They're not psychic.

Myth 3: "Psychology is a soft science — not real science."
Truth: It uses experiments, statistics, and peer review. It's just harder than physics because the subject can talk back.

Myth 4: "Psychology will tell me who I really am."
Truth: It offers tools and frameworks — not a fixed answer.

Why Psychology Is So Hard

Imagine trying to study a mirror by holding up another mirror. That's psychology.

The scientist and the subject are both human. The subject knows they're being studied. The scientist has their own biases. And the subject changes as soon as you look at them.

The Replication Crisis

In 2015, a massive project tried to repeat 100 famous psychology studies. Only about 36% of them produced the same results. This doesn't mean psychology is fake — it means it's messy, and many early findings were overstated. It's a science in progress.

Check Your Understanding

Why is philosophy called the "father" and literature the "mother"?
Philosophy is the rational, questioning side (the father). Literature is the emotional, comforting side (the mother). Psychology combines both: it uses reason but also deals with feelings, stories, and hope.
What is the deepest root of philosophy and psychology?
Our awareness of death. Because we know we'll die, we invented gods, stories, and philosophies to cope. Psychology inherited this concern.
What's the difference between a psychologist and a psychiatrist?
A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who can prescribe medication. A psychologist usually has a PhD or PsyD and focuses on testing, therapy, and research — but generally cannot prescribe drugs.

Ancient Roots

Greece, India, and the Islamic Golden Age

The Big Idea

Long before psychology was a science, three great civilisations were already asking deep questions about the mind: Ancient Greece (personality and virtue), Ancient India (consciousness and the self), and the Islamic world (mind, body, and soul).

Part 1: Ancient Greece and Rome

Hippocrates (460–370 BC): The Body Heals Itself

Hippocrates was a Greek physician — often called the "father of medicine." He made two huge contributions:

  • The Hippocratic Oath — the promise doctors make to "do no harm." Still used today, in modified form.
  • The theory of the four humors — the body contains four fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) and health is about keeping them balanced.

His approach was surprisingly modern: let the patient rest, and the body will heal itself. Today we'd call this supporting the immune system.

Galen (129–201 AD): The Four Personalities

Galen was a Roman physician (of Greek origin) who worked with gladiators. He took Hippocrates' idea and turned it into a theory of personality.

His claim: whichever humor dominates in your body determines your personality type.

BloodAirSanguine — cheerful, social, optimisticExtroverted

Yellow bileFireCholeric — passionate, ambitious, short-temperedType A
Black bileEarthMelancholic — artistic, thoughtful, sadSensitive

Galen also said you could adjust your temperament — through diet, exercise, and lifestyle. Too much choleric energy? Do calming activities. Sound familiar? That's basically modern advice about stress management.

Analogy: The Cooked Meal

Galen said personality is like a cooked meal. Each humor is an ingredient. The taste depends on how much of each you put in. Too much salt and the dish is ruined. Change the proportions, change the taste.

Socrates (470–399 BC): Know Thyself

Socrates was the father of Western philosophy. He shifted the focus from the physical world to the inner world.

His key claims:

  • The happy person is the one who knows their true self.
  • Virtue is the ultimate goal — and a virtuous person is also a happy person.
  • Truth is found through questioning, not accepting.

His method — the Socratic method — was to ask a series of probing questions until the other person discovered their own ignorance, and then, from that cleared ground, began to build real knowledge.

Why This Matters Today

Modern Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) uses a version of the Socratic method. The therapist doesn't tell you your beliefs are wrong — they ask questions until you see it yourself. Socrates invented the first therapy technique, 2,400 years ago.

Aristotle (384–322 BC): Four Roads to Happiness

Aristotle was Socrates' student's student — he studied under Plato. He said there are four ways to happiness:

  1. Sensual pleasure — sex, food, comfort.
  2. Material possessions — wealth, property, status.
  3. Ethical superiority — being a good person, having virtue.
  4. Logical understanding — knowing how the world works.

He called the highest form of happiness eudaimonia — not "feeling good" but "living well and flourishing." It comes from fulfilling your potential as a rational, social being.

Analogy

Aristotle's eudaimonia is like the difference between a sugar rush and a healthy meal. Pleasure is a sugar rush — quick, intense, gone. Eudaimonia is the healthy meal — slower, harder, but it sustains you.

Part 2: Ancient India

Ancient India had two streams of thought:

  • The Vedas — about society, religion, and ritual. The outer world.
  • The Upanishads (about 2,700 years old) — about the individual's inner journey. The inner world.

The Upanishads are basically the world's first psychology textbook. They explore consciousness, the self, and liberation from suffering.

The Two Selves

  • Jiva — the everyday, visible self. Your name, your body, your personality. The Upanishads say this is a kind of illusion (Maya).
  • Atman — the true, hidden self. The unchanging reality behind the mask. Some texts say Atman is Brahman — the ultimate reality of the universe.

Life's purpose: to realise Atman. When you fully know your true self, you reach moksha — liberation from the cycle of birth and rebirth.

The Key Insight

You are not your body, your thoughts, or your personality. Those are the mask. Your real self is deeper. And you can only find it by training your awareness.

The Four States of Consciousness

The Upanishads — particularly the Mandukya Upanishad — divide consciousness into four states:

  1. Waking — ordinary consciousness. You perceive the outside world.
  2. Dreaming — you perceive an inner world.
  3. Deep sleep — no content, but pure awareness remains. You exist, but you don't know anything.
  4. Turiya — the "fourth." Pure consciousness itself, beyond waking, dreaming, and sleeping. Reached only through deep meditation.

This is remarkably similar to modern discussions about consciousness — the idea that there's a base layer of awareness that exists even when there's no content.

Mind and Nature

In Hindu philosophy, humans are not separate from nature. We are nature. There's no gap between us and the animals, the rivers, the trees. This brings humility — we're not special, just one part of a greater whole.

Why This Still Matters

Modern mindfulness and yoga — now used worldwide to treat anxiety, depression, and chronic pain — come directly from these ancient Indian practices. What the Upanishads discovered through meditation, modern science is now confirming through brain scans.

Part 3: The Islamic Golden Age (8th–12th Centuries)

While Europe was in the early Middle Ages, the Islamic world was preserving and expanding Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge. Muslim philosophers made major contributions to psychology.

Al-Kindi (801–873)

He wrote about the nature of the soul and argued that the mind can be understood through reason and observation. He also worked on optics and perception — how we see things.

Al-Farabi (872–950)

He divided consciousness into two levels:

  • Individual consciousness — your internal awareness. Knowing what you know.
  • Active consciousness — your ability to receive external information and grow your inner world.

He also argued that rational ability lets us make moral choices — something animals can't do. He linked our choices to internal organs (heart, brain, liver) and the "heat" they contained. Hotter = more aggressive (men). Colder = more compassionate (women). Not accurate, but a first attempt at biological psychology.

Ibn Sina / Avicenna (980–1037): The Floating Man

Ibn Sina was a philosopher, doctor, psychologist, and theologian. He wrote The Canon of Medicine, which was used in European universities for 600 years.

The Floating Man Thought Experiment

Imagine a man suspended in the air, with his limbs not touching anything and his eyes covered. He has no sensory input at all.

Would he still know he exists?

Ibn Sina said yes. Even without senses, the mind would still be aware of itself. Therefore, the mind is separate from the body.

This is almost exactly what Descartes argued 600 years later with "I think, therefore I am." Ibn Sina got there first.

Ibn Rushd / Averroes (1126–1198)

Ibn Rushd lived in Islamic Spain and was one of the great commentators on Aristotle. His key ideas:

  • Ideas come before matter (like Plato). The material world is a shadow of a perfect idea.
  • The soul is universal, but becomes individual inside each human body.
  • Once the individual mind gains enough knowledge, it can rise up and become part of the universal consciousness — a kind of immortality.

He also made a very practical argument: material rewards motivate people more than spiritual ones. He thought the Islamic promise of sensual pleasures in the afterlife was a better motivator than the Christian promise of pure spirituality. This was one of the first seeds of materialism arriving in Europe.

The Bridge to Europe

Ibn Rushd lived in Islamic Spain — right next to Christian Europe. His work, and the work of other Islamic scholars, helped European thinkers rediscover Greek philosophy. This directly helped start the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution.

Deep Dive: How Did These Ideas Travel?

In the 12th and 13th centuries, European scholars in places like Toledo, Spain, translated Arabic texts into Latin. This is how Aristotle, Galen, and the Islamic philosophers entered European universities.

Thinkers like Thomas Aquinas then tried to combine Aristotle's philosophy with Christian theology. The result was a worldview in which reason and faith could coexist — a crucial step toward modern science.

So the history of psychology isn't a straight line from Greece to Europe. It's a circle: Greece → Islamic world → back to Europe. The "Western" tradition was built on Indian, Persian, and Arabic foundations too.

Birth of a Science

From Descartes to the first psychology lab

The Big Idea

Between 1600 and 1900, science broke religion's grip on the mind. Once people stopped believing God was protecting them, they had to solve their own problems. That gap — between a cold universe and a suffering mind — is where psychology was born.

Step 1: The Scientific Revolution Shakes Everything

Before 1500, almost everyone in Europe believed:

  • The Earth is the centre of the universe.
  • God created everything and watches over you.
  • Your suffering has a divine purpose.
  • An afterlife in heaven waits for the good.

Then came Copernicus (Earth orbits the Sun), Galileo (proved it with a telescope), and Newton (explained the laws of motion). Suddenly, the universe looked like a machine, not a divine parent.

The Psychological Shock

For thousands of years, God was like a parent — protective, loving, waiting for you after death. Science said: "Santa isn't real." The result was a psychological vacuum. If no one is protecting you, who will? The answer, in the 18th and 19th centuries, was: you, using reason.

René Descartes (1596–1650): "I Think, Therefore I Am"

Descartes was a French philosopher and mathematician (he invented coordinate geometry). He wanted to find one thing he could not doubt.

So he doubted everything:

  • Maybe my senses are lying to me.
  • Maybe the world is a dream.
  • Maybe an evil demon is deceiving me.

But one thing survived: the fact that he was doubting. And if he's doubting, he must be thinking. And if he's thinking, he must exist.

Cogito Ergo Sum

"I think, therefore I am." This is the most famous line in philosophy. It proves that the thinking self is the one thing you can never doubt.

Cartesian Dualism

Descartes argued that reality has two kinds of substance:

  • Matter — physical stuff. It has size, shape, and location.
  • Mind — thinking stuff. It has no size or shape.

The mind and body interact — but how? Descartes said through the pineal gland (a tiny organ in the brain). This is the mind-body problem, and it's still unsolved today.

Analogy

The mind-body problem is like a ghost driving a car. The ghost (mind) can push the pedals (body) — but how? Ghosts don't have hands. And yet, they clearly interact.

Step 2: The Enlightenment — Reason Becomes God

The 18th century is called the Enlightenment — "the age of reason." Thinkers believed that human rationality could solve all problems.

But there was a problem: reason can build machines. It can't cure loneliness. The more comfortable life became, the more people seemed to suffer psychologically.

The Rich Get Sad

Poor people in the 1800s struggled with hunger and disease. Rich people had everything — and yet many were miserable. Science could fix physical pain. It couldn't fix mental pain. So people started asking: why do we suffer?

Step 3: Schopenhauer and the "Blind Will"

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) — nicknamed "the great pessimist" — was the first philosopher to really attack the Enlightenment's optimism. His 1819 book, The World as Will and Representation, changed philosophy forever.

His claim:

  • We think we want money, fame, beauty, and status.
  • But it's not really us who wants them. It's a hidden, impersonal force inside us — the blind will.
  • The blind will has no goal, no shape, no limit. It just pushes. And it uses us to push.
Analogy

The blind will is like a background process running on your phone. You never opened it. You can't see it. But it's draining your battery and using your data.

Schopenhauer's most famous line:

The Pendulum

"Life swings like a pendulum backward and forward between pain and boredom."

Schopenhauer was heavily influenced by the Upanishads. His "will" is basically the Indian life-force, translated into rational European language.

Deep Dive: Schopenhauer's Influence on Freud and Jung

Schopenhauer's "blind will" is basically Freud's "id" — the unconscious, instinctual part of the mind. Freud even said Schopenhauer was one of his main predecessors.

Schopenhauer's idea that our conscious mind is a thin layer over a vast, impersonal force became Jung's "collective unconscious" too.

So when people say Freud invented the unconscious, that's not quite right. Schopenhauer described it in 1819. Freud gave it a clinical framework — and made it famous.

Step 4: Darwin's Bomb (1859)

Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859. His theory:

  • All living things are related by common descent.
  • Species change over time through natural selection.
  • Individuals vary. Those with useful variations survive and reproduce. Those without them die out.

For psychology, the impact was huge:

  1. Humans are animals. Our minds evolved just like our bodies.
  2. The mind can be studied scientifically — like any other natural phenomenon.
  3. Behaviour has a function — it helps us survive and reproduce.
Why This Was Dangerous

If humans evolved from animals, then we're not special creations of God. Our minds are not divine — they're biological. This shattered religious authority over the mind and opened the door for psychology.

Step 5: The First Psychology Lab (1879)

Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) opened the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig in Germany in 1879. This is usually marked as the birth of psychology as a science.

His method was introspection — asking trained observers to report exactly what they felt when exposed to a stimulus.

  • He'd show a light, or play a sound, or give a smell.
  • Then he'd ask: what did you experience? How intense was it? How did it feel?

He broke consciousness into three categories: representation, willing, and feeling. (We'll cover this in more detail in Lesson 5.)

Limitations of Introspection

Introspection has a fatal flaw: you can't check someone's report. If I say my headache is a "7" and you say yours is a "3," how do we know we're using the same scale? Introspection is subjective — and that's a problem for a science.

What the Mind Is Made Of

Matter vs. ideas, and the three levels of consciousness

The Big Idea

Is the mind made of physical stuff (matter) or non-physical stuff (ideas)? Modern psychology bets mostly on matter — but the debate is far from over.

The Two Great Camps: Materialism vs Idealism

Analogy: The Cake

Materialist: "A cake is flour, eggs, and sugar. That's all it is."

Idealist: "No — a cake is the idea of cake. The ingredients are just a rough copy of that idea."

The Computer Analogy

The best way to understand the mind is to think of a smartphone:

Psychology sits in the middle. It studies the operating system — how the brain produces thoughts, feelings, and behaviour.

The Hard Problem of Consciousness

Here's the deepest puzzle in all of psychology:

The Hard Problem

We can explain how the brain processes information — that's the "easy problem." But we can't explain why any of it feels like anything. Why does red look red? Why does pain hurt? Why is there subjective experience at all?

Philosopher David Chalmers (1966–) named this the "hard problem of consciousness." Nobody has solved it.

This is why psychology can never be fully reduced to physics. You can map every neuron and still not explain why a sunset is beautiful to you.

Marx: The Materialist Champion

Karl Marx (1818–1883) took Hegel's philosophy and made it materialist. His famous line: "Religion is the opium of the masses."

His argument:

  • Poor people don't have enough material things.
  • So they invent religion to numb the pain — like a drug.
  • Religion says "your suffering will be rewarded in heaven," which keeps them from rebelling against the system that's exploiting them.

His psychology in one line: we are animals made of matter, seeking matter, and inventing ideas to cope when we don't have enough.

The Marx Critique

Marx's theory is powerful but one-sided. It ignores the fact that ideas — stories, beliefs, values — can also drive people to change their material conditions. Marxism itself is an idea, and it changed the material world.

Analogy: The Well

Your conscious mind is a torch shining into the well. It can only light up the top. The deeper you go, the darker it gets — and the harder it is to see what's down there. But what's down there still affects everything above it.

Freud's Three-Part Self: Id, Ego, Superego

Freud later developed a second model — the structural model of the mind:

  • Id — the primitive, instinctual part. Wants pleasure now. "I want it and I want it now." Present from birth.
  • Ego — the rational, realistic part. Mediates between the id, the superego, and reality. "Let's find a way to get what you want without getting hurt."
  • Superego — the moral part. Internalised rules from parents and society. "That's wrong. You should be ashamed."
Analogy: The Carriage

The id is a wild horse — powerful, blind, wanting only to run.

The ego is the driver — trying to steer the horse toward what's actually good for them.

The superego is the passenger in the back — shouting "You're doing it wrong! What would people think?"

Mental health is roughly the ego doing a decent job of keeping the horse and the passenger from killing each other.

The Three Basic Instincts

According to psychoanalysis, we have no choice about these three:

  1. Food — to survive
  2. Sex — to pass on genes
  3. Company — because survival depends on the group

The Free Will Problem

If the unconscious runs most of the show, how much free will do we actually have?

The Libet Experiments

In the 1980s, Benjamin Libet found that brain activity related to a decision appears about 300 milliseconds before the person is consciously aware of deciding. In other words, your brain seems to "decide" before "you" do.

This doesn't prove free will is an illusion — but it does show that our sense of conscious decision is not the whole story.

Why Mental Illness Is More Common in Rich Countries

This is a fascinating and well-documented pattern. Some evidence:

  • In poor countries, life is a daily struggle for survival. The mind is focused outward.
  • In rich countries, survival is handled. So the mind turns inward — and creates its own problems.
  • Boredom, loneliness, meaninglessness, and anxiety become the main enemies.
  • Horror movies, dangerous sports, and addictions replace the chaos of survival.
The Paradox of Comfort

When life is hard, you don't have time to be depressed. When life is easy, you have all the time in the world — and the mind fills it with trouble.

Consciousness: Structure or Stream?

Wundt, James, and the study of awareness

The Big Idea

Two schools fought over consciousness. Structuralists said it's a machine you can take apart. Functionalists said it's a river you can only watch flow.

Structuralism: Wundt's Approach

Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) believed consciousness is a structure — like a house. You can break it into parts and study each one.

He divided consciousness into three categories:

CategoryWhat It IsExample
RepresentationPerception of images, sounds, smells — or memory and imagination of them.Seeing a pizza, imagining a pizza, remembering a pizza.
WillingThe internal urge to act or not act. Hard to study — it's purely internal.Reaching for the pizza. Or deciding not to.
FeelingHow you feel after the fact.Enjoying the pizza. Or feeling guilty.
The Simple Formula

You sense something → you act → you feel. Wundt called this the structure of consciousness.

Wundt's method was introspection: trained observers reported their inner experiences in controlled experiments. He measured reaction times, studied attention, and tried to find the "elements" of thought.

The Problem with Introspection

Two people can experience the same thing and report different things. There's no way to verify. Introspection died out as a method because it couldn't produce reliable, repeatable results.

Functionalism: William James's Approach

William James (1842–1910) was an American philosopher and psychologist (and brother of novelist Henry James). He was inspired by Darwin.

He didn't ask "What is consciousness?" He asked "What does it DO?"

James's Key Metaphor

Consciousness is not a structure. It's a stream — a continuous flow of thoughts, feelings, and sensations. It never stops. This gave us the term "stream of consciousness."

Analogy: House vs River

Wundt sees a house — walls, doors, windows. You can list the parts and study them one by one.

James sees a river — always moving. Try to grab it, and it slips through your fingers. Study the parts, and you've killed the thing you're studying.

James also pointed out that we'd be overwhelmed by sensory overload if our mind didn't pause sometimes to reflect. Those pauses let us sort information and make sense of the world. He called this the "fringe" of consciousness — the vague sense of context around your focused attention.

James's Self: "I" and "Me"

James also split the self into two parts:

  • The "I" (the knower) — the self that experiences. The subject. Pure awareness.
  • The "Me" (the known) — the self you can describe. Your identity, your roles, your traits. "I am a student, a brother, a runner."

This distinction is still used by psychologists studying identity and self-awareness.

The James–Lange Theory of Emotion

This is one of the most counter-intuitive ideas in psychology:

The James-Lange Theory

You see a wild animal. You run first. Then you feel fear — because your brain notices your heart pounding, your legs moving, your breath fast.

In other words: you don't run because you're scared. You're scared because you're running.

Another example: you feel happy because you notice yourself smiling. You feel sad because you notice tears on your cheek.

James-Lange: "I'm scared because I'm shaking."

Emotions vs. Feelings

The Dutch psychologist Nico Frijda (1927–2015) made a crucial distinction:

Analogy: The Iceberg

Emotions are the iceberg under the water — invisible, huge, and uncontrollable. Feelings are the tip you can see and name. You can't choose whether you feel fear when a car swerves at you. But you can choose whether to dwell on it, worry about it, or let it go.

Ebbinghaus and Memory

Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850–1909) tested his own memory using lists of real words and nonsense syllables (like "DAX," "BOK," "ZIF"). He found three things:

  1. Meaning matters. You remember real words far better than nonsense. Meaning is tied to memory.
  2. Time matters. The longer you study something, the better you recall it.
  3. Delay matters. The longer you wait before recalling, the more you forget.

The Forgetting Curve

Ebbinghaus discovered that forgetting is not linear — it's steep at first, then levels off. You forget most of what you learn in the first few hours. This is why:

  • Spaced repetition works — revising over time beats cramming.
  • Sleeping after studying helps — the brain consolidates memory during sleep.
  • Testing yourself (active recall) beats re-reading.
Why This Is Useful

This is why revising works. And why you forget everything a week after the exam. If you want to keep something, revisit it — at increasing intervals.

Pierre Janet and the Unconscious

Pierre Janet (1859–1947) was a French doctor working with trauma patients in Paris in the 1880s and 1890s. He noticed something odd: his patients were safe now, but they acted like they were still in danger.

He called this dissociation — the past trauma is still inside you, telling you you're not safe.

Janet's Big Insight

Trauma doesn't just disappear. It gets stored in a separate part of the mind, where it can be triggered later by things that remind you of the original event.

This is the basis of modern PTSD research. Janet came before Freud, so some call him the real pioneer of the unconscious.

Janet also said that dissociation could happen even without past trauma — some people are just more predisposed to it. This hints at what Jung would later call the "collective unconscious" — inherited fears and patterns.

Timeline of Consciousness Research

  • 1879: Wundt opens the first psychology lab, studies consciousness by introspection.
  • 1890: William James publishes The Principles of Psychology, argues consciousness is a stream.
  • 1885: Ebbinghaus publishes his memory experiments, discovers the forgetting curve.
  • 1889: Janet publishes his work on dissociation and trauma.
  • 1920s: Behaviourism rejects consciousness as unscientific.
  • 1950s: Cognitive revolution brings consciousness back.
  • 1990s–today: Neuroscience and philosophy of mind tackle the "hard problem."

Behaviorism

Action speaks louder than words

The Big Idea

Don't ask people what they think — watch what they do. Behaviourists believed the mind is too private to study, but behaviour can be measured, predicted, and changed.

Nature vs. Nurture

Are we born the way we are, or do we become that way? Behaviourists leaned heavily toward nurture — the environment shapes us. Later research showed it's both, but behaviourism began as a strong nurture argument.

Pavlov and the Dogs

Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936) was a Russian scientist (and son of a priest) who won a Nobel Prize in 1904 for his work on digestion. He studied dogs and saliva.

He noticed something: dogs would drool not just when they saw food, but when they saw the person who brings food, or heard a bell.

TermWhat It MeansExample
Unconditioned stimulus (US)Something that naturally triggers a responseFood
Unconditioned response (UR)The natural responseDrooling

Conditioned response (CR)The learned response to the CSDrooling at the bell alone

Other Pavlovian Concepts

  • Extinction: If the bell rings repeatedly without food, the drooling stops.
  • Spontaneous recovery: After a break, the response can come back. Extinction isn't permanent.
  • Generalisation: The dog drools at similar sounds, not just the exact bell.
  • Discrimination: The dog learns to drool at one bell but not another.
Analogy

Your phone buzzes. Your heart jumps a little — is it a message? You've been conditioned. The buzz means nothing by itself. You taught your brain what it means.

Thorndike and the Cats

Edward Thorndike (1874–1949) put cats in puzzle boxes. To get out and get food, they had to solve the puzzle (press a lever, pull a string, etc.).

He found they learned by trial and error. Each success made the next attempt faster. He called this the Law of Effect:

Law of Effect

Good outcomes strengthen a behaviour. Bad outcomes weaken it. Simple as that.

Tolman and the Cognitive Map

Edward Tolman (1886–1959) was a behaviourist who accidentally undermined behaviourism.

He put rats in a maze. Some rats got food at the end; some didn't. The rats with food learned the maze quickly. But here's the twist: when he later put food in the maze for the rats that had never received any, they learned it immediately — faster than the rats that had been rewarded all along.

Latent Learning

The rats had been learning the maze all along — they just had no reason to show it. Tolman called this latent learning, and argued the rats had built a cognitive map of the maze in their heads.

This was a huge blow to strict behaviourism: it showed that learning can happen without rewards, and that animals have internal mental representations.

Other Key Behaviourists

Karl Lashley (1890–1958)Removed parts of rats' brains — they still remembered. Memory isn't stored in one spot; it's spread across the whole brain. This is why we have "muscle memory" too.
Konrad Lorenz (1903–1989)Ducklings bond with the first moving thing they see, but only during a certain age window. He called it imprinting. Miss the window and the bond can't form.
Zing-Yang Kuo (1898–1970)Raised kittens in cages with rats. The cats didn't eat the rats — they played with them. So maybe instinct isn't as fixed as we think.

John Watson and Little Albert

John Watson (1878–1958) took Pavlov's idea and tested it on a human baby. He also wrote a famous 1913 manifesto declaring that psychology should be a science of behaviour only — no introspection, no consciousness.

His experiment:

  • He showed 9-month-old "Little Albert" a white rat. Albert wasn't scared.
  • Then he made a loud scary noise every time the rat appeared.
  • Soon Albert was terrified of the rat — and also of anything white and fluffy (rabbits, fur coats, Santa's beard).
Ethics Warning

This experiment could never be done today. It proved that fear can be conditioned — but it also caused lasting harm to a baby. Modern ethics codes were partly created because of studies like this.

B. F. Skinner and Operant Conditioning

B. F. Skinner (1904–1990) put rats in boxes with a lever. Press the lever, get food. The rats kept pressing — but only if food actually came.

Reinforcement and Punishment

Something AddedSomething Removed
Behaviour increasesPositive reinforcement (give a reward)Negative reinforcement (remove something unpleasant)
Behaviour decreasesPositive punishment (add something unpleasant)Negative punishment (take away something pleasant)
Analogy: The Seatbelt Alarm

Negative reinforcement: You put on your seatbelt to make the annoying beeping stop. The behaviour (wearing a seatbelt) increases because you removed something unpleasant (the beep).

Note: "negative" here doesn't mean "bad." It means "subtraction."

Schedules of Reinforcement

Skinner found that how often you reward a behaviour matters hugely:

  • Fixed ratio — reward every 5th press. Steady, high rate.
  • Variable ratio — reward after a random number of presses. Highest rate, hardest to extinguish. This is how slot machines work.
  • Fixed interval — reward the first press after 2 minutes. Slow, then bursty.
  • Variable interval — reward at random times. Steady, slow rate. Like checking your phone.
Why Social Media Is Addictive

Social media uses variable ratio reinforcement. You don't know when you'll get a like. Sometimes you scroll and get nothing. Sometimes you get a big hit. That unpredictability is exactly what makes it so compelling.

Skinner's Other Findings

  • Positive reinforcement works better than punishment. Carrots beat sticks.
  • Different rats responded differently — so genetics matters too. Nature AND nurture.
  • Free will, he said, is mostly an illusion. We do what has worked before.
  • He proposed programmed learning — teaching machines and immediate feedback — which influenced modern education technology.

Joseph Wolpe and Systematic Desensitisation

Joseph Wolpe (1915–1997) figured out a clever trick: you can't be anxious and relaxed at the same time.

So he developed a step-by-step method:

  1. Teach the patient deep relaxation.
  2. Ask them to imagine a mildly scary version of their fear (e.g., a spider in a distant cage).
  3. If they stay relaxed, move to a slightly scarier version.
  4. Repeat until the fear is extinguished.

This is called systematic desensitisation. It's still used today for phobias, PTSD, and anxiety. It went against the psychoanalytic idea that you must dig into the past trauma. Instead, Wolpe focused on the symptom in the present.

In Simple Words

Want to get over a fear of spiders? You have to touch the spider — but gently, and while feeling safe. Add enough sugar and the bitter thing tastes sweet.

Why Behaviourism Declined

By the 1950s, behaviourism was the dominant school in America. But it had fatal flaws:

  1. Language. Noam Chomsky argued that children learn language too fast and too creatively to be explained by conditioning. They produce sentences they've never heard.
  2. Latent learning. Tolman's rats showed that learning happens without rewards — which requires internal mental maps.
  3. Insight. Köhler's chimpanzees showed sudden problem-solving — not just trial and error.
  4. The cognitive revolution. The invention of computers gave psychologists a new metaphor: the mind as an information processor.
The Legacy

Behaviourism didn't disappear. It became the basis for behaviour therapy, which is still widely used. But the strict version — "only behaviour matters" — was replaced by cognitive psychology, which asked what happens inside the mind.

Check Your Understanding

What is classical conditioning?
Learning by association. A neutral thing (a bell) gets linked to something meaningful (food) until the neutral thing alone triggers the response.
What is operant conditioning?
Learning through consequences. Behaviours that get reinforced increase; behaviours that get punished decrease. Skinner's rat pressing a lever for food is the classic example.
What did Skinner discover about punishment?
Positive reinforcement (rewards) works better than punishment. Carrots beat sticks. Punishment can suppress behaviour temporarily but doesn't teach a better alternative.
What is latent learning, and why did it matter?
Tolman's rats learned a maze even without rewards, then performed perfectly once a reward appeared. This showed learning can happen without reinforcement — and that animals have internal mental maps. It undermined strict behaviourism.
How did Wolpe treat phobias?
By pairing the feared thing with relaxation. Since you can't be relaxed and terrified at once, the brain gradually re-learns the association. This is called systematic desensitisation.

Freud vs. Jung

The battle of the unconscious

The Big Idea

Both agreed the unconscious runs the show. They disagreed on one thing: is it your own unconscious, or one you share with all humans?

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939): The Context

Freud was born in what is now the Czech Republic, but spent most of his life in Vienna, the capital of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. It was one of the most sophisticated cities in the world.

Yet many of his patients — wealthy, educated people — were mentally ill. Why?

  • Materially, they had everything.
  • But they had lost faith in God and the Church.
  • They had to find meaning for themselves, alone.
  • In a big city, they were surrounded by people but deeply lonely.

Freud concluded: the problem is inside. Not God, not fate, not poverty — the mind itself.

The Iceberg Model (Topographic Model)

Freud's first model of the mind was like an iceberg:

LayerWhat's in itAccess
ConsciousThe tip above water. What you're aware of right now.Direct
PreconsciousJust below the surface. Memories you can recall if you try.Effortful
UnconsciousThe huge mass underwater. Traumas, urges, repressed memories.Indirect only

The iceberg floats and shifts. Different parts surface at different times. This is why repressed memories can suddenly "come up" in therapy or dreams.

Freud's Structural Model: Id, Ego, Superego

Later, Freud revised his model:

PartWhat It WantsRuleWhere
IdPleasure, now. Food, sex, aggression."I want it now."Unconscious, from birth
EgoTo survive and adapt in reality."Let's find a way that works."Mostly conscious
SuperegoTo be moral, to avoid guilt."That's wrong. You should be ashamed."Partly unconscious

Defence Mechanisms

When the ego can't handle the conflict between id and superego, it uses defence mechanisms — unconscious strategies to protect itself:

  • Repression — pushing painful memories out of awareness.
  • Denial — refusing to accept reality. "This isn't happening."
  • Projection — seeing your own unacceptable feelings in others. "I'm not angry — you're angry."
  • Rationalisation — making up logical excuses. "I didn't get the job anyway."
  • Displacement — taking out frustration on a safe target. Yelling at your family after a bad day at work.
  • Sublimation — channelling unacceptable urges into something productive. Aggression becomes sport; desire becomes art.
Analogy

Defence mechanisms are like the body's immune system — but for the mind. They kick in automatically when something threatens your sense of self. The problem is, like an autoimmune disease, they can also attack things that aren't actually threats.

Freud's Psychosexual Stages

Freud believed personality develops through stages, each focused on a different part of the body:

Anal1–3Toilet training, controlAnal-retentive (obsessive) or anal-expulsive (messy)

Freud's Four Pieces of Evidence

  1. Mental illness. Physically healthy people suffering mentally — something hidden must cause it.
  2. Dreams. Everyone dreams. Freud said dreams are the unconscious sending us messages about repressed wishes. He distinguished:
    • Manifest content — what you remember.
    • Latent content — the hidden meaning.
    • The dream "work" uses condensation (combining many ideas into one image) and displacement (shifting emotion from the real target to a safe one).
  3. Slips of the tongue. "Freudian slips." You mean to say one thing but say what you really want. "I'm glad to beat you" instead of "meet you."
  4. Catharsis. Writing, art, storytelling — letting out what you can't say directly. This is why books and movies feel so releasing.

The Talking Cure

Freud noticed something remarkable: when patients simply talked about their fantasies and memories, they felt better.

He didn't invent this — the church had confession for centuries. But he turned it into a medical treatment: psychotherapy.

Two things were happening:

  1. Letting it out. Expression releases pressure.
  2. Having someone listen. In a big lonely city, having someone truly listen is incredibly powerful.
Freud's Limitations

Freud's theories were based on a small number of patients, mostly wealthy Viennese women. He had no controlled experiments. He was also criticised for being unfalsifiable — hard to prove wrong. Modern psychology has kept some of his insights (unconscious processes, defence mechanisms, early experience) but rejected many of his specific claims.

Carl Jung (1875–1961): The Collective Unconscious

Jung was Swiss, a close friend and collaborator of Freud, and later broke away from him. His big idea: the collective unconscious.

Freud focused on your memories. Jung said we also inherit memories from our ancestors — shared across all cultures.

The Collective Unconscious

Just as your body inherits physical traits from your ancestors, your mind inherits patterns of thought, feeling, and imagery. These patterns show up in myths, religions, and stories all over the world — without any contact between cultures.

Archetypes

These inherited patterns show up as archetypes — recurring characters and themes in every story ever told:

  • The Wise Old Man / Wise Old Woman
  • The Hero
  • The Mother / The Father
  • The Trickster
  • The Devil / The Villain
  • Anima — the feminine side in a man
  • Animus — the masculine side in a woman
  • The Self — the unified whole of the psyche
In Simple Words

Why do the same kinds of characters appear in stories from Japan, Africa, Europe, and America? Jung said: because they're wired into all of us.

Jung's Three Layers of the Psyche

LayerWhat's in it
EgoYour conscious, rational self. The steering wheel.
Personal unconsciousYour own memories, including repressed ones. (Freud's focus.)
Collective unconsciousInherited archetypes from all humanity. (Jung's focus.)

Persona and Shadow

  • Persona — the mask you show the world. Different in front of your boss, your friends, your family. The Japanese call the true self honne and the public self tatemae.
  • Shadow — the part you hide. Your secrets, suppressed urges. In stories, this is the villain.

We always see the shadow in others, never in ourselves. In every war, both sides call the other side evil.

Individuation

Jung's goal for life: individuation — becoming your true, integrated self. This means:

  • Recognising your persona is a mask.
  • Integrating your shadow — accepting your dark side instead of projecting it.
  • Balancing your anima/animus.
  • Becoming whole, not perfect.

He said: "Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate."

Why Stories Matter to Jung

Humans are obsessed with stories. Children beg for them. Adults binge them. Nations are built on them.

Jung said: stories are where archetypes live. And we need them because they give our lives meaning.

Depression and anxiety often come when we lose faith in our life's story.

Analogy

Your DNA carries the memory of your body's ancestors. Jung said archetypes are like cultural DNA — carrying the memory of your species' stories.

Alfred Adler (1870–1937): The Third Man

Adler was another early follower of Freud who broke away. His key ideas:

  • Inferiority complex — we all start life helpless and dependent. How we cope with that feeling shapes our personality. Some overcompensate (become ambitious); others give up.
  • Striving for superiority — not dominance over others, but mastery over our own lives.
  • Social interest — mental health depends on contributing to something bigger than yourself.
  • Birth order — he argued firstborns, middle children, and youngest children develop different personalities. (This idea has mixed evidence.)

Critique of Psychoanalysis

Main Criticisms

1. Unfalsifiable. If a patient agrees with the interpretation, that proves it. If they disagree, that's "resistance" — which also proves it. Either way, the theory wins.

2. Small sample. Freud based his theories on a handful of patients — mostly wealthy women. Not representative.

3. Neglects culture and society. Freud focused on the individual. He ignored poverty, oppression, and social factors.

4. Overemphasis on sex. Freud saw sex everywhere. Modern psychology sees it as one drive among many.

5. Gender bias. His theories about women (like "penis envy") are widely rejected today.

What Survived

Despite the criticisms, some Freudian ideas are still influential:

  • Unconscious processes. Modern cognitive psychology confirms that much of mental life is unconscious.
  • Defence mechanisms. The idea that we protect ourselves from painful truths is accepted (though the specific mechanisms are debated).
  • Early experience matters. Attachment theory confirms that early relationships shape later development.
  • Talking therapy works. The "talking cure" is the basis for most modern psychotherapy.
  • Implicit memory. We can be influenced by things we don't consciously remember.

Check Your Understanding

What are Freud's four pieces of evidence for the unconscious?
1) Mental illness in physically healthy people, 2) dreams, 3) slips of the tongue, and 4) catharsis — expression through art, writing, or storytelling.
What's the main difference between Freud and Jung?
Freud focused on the individual unconscious (your own memories and traumas). Jung added the collective unconscious — inherited memories and archetypes shared by all humans.
What is the "shadow"?
The hidden part of you — your secrets, suppressed urges, and darker impulses. We usually see it in others (as "the villain") but not in ourselves.
What is individuation?
Jung's goal for life: becoming your true, integrated self. It means recognising your persona, integrating your shadow, and balancing the anima/animus. Becoming whole, not perfect.
Name three major criticisms of Freud's theories.
1) Unfalsifiable — they can't be proven wrong. 2) Based on a small, unrepresentative sample. 3) Neglects social and cultural factors. (Others: overemphasis on sex, gender bias.)

Psychotherapy

How to cure modern suffering

The Big Idea

Three paths to healing: take responsibility (Gestalt), find fulfilment (Humanistic), and find meaning (Existential). Later, cognitive therapy added a fourth: test your thoughts against reality.

Before We Start: The Perception Problem

How do we know reality? Two philosophical camps fought over this:

  • Rationalism (Descartes, Kant) — we have innate knowledge. Some things are built in.
  • Empiricism (Locke, Hume) — knowledge comes from experience. The mind is a blank slate.

Kant reconciled them: we get information from the outside, but our mind imposes its own structure on it. We never see reality "as it is" — only as our mind organises it.

Why This Matters for Therapy

If we never see reality directly, then how we perceive things is at least as important as what actually happened. Most modern therapy is based on this insight.

Path 1: Gestalt — Take Responsibility

Fritz Perls (1893–1970) was a German psychologist who fled the Nazis. His big claim: how you see reality matters more than reality itself.

Is your glass half empty or half full? That's your choice.

Key Gestalt Ideas

  • Here and now. Focus on the present, not the past. "What are you feeling right now?"
  • Responsibility. Nobody can make you angry. Only you can. You can't blame the world for how you feel.
  • Unfinished business. Unexpressed feelings from the past — anger, grief, resentment — linger and cause present problems. Gestalt therapy helps you express them.
  • The empty chair technique. You talk to an empty chair as if someone you're angry at or grieving is sitting there. It's a way to express feelings you've been holding back.
Gestalt in One Line

Nobody can victimise you but yourself. Everything is a choice.

Albert Ellis — Irrational Beliefs

Albert Ellis (1913–2007) said: events don't cause your emotions — your beliefs do.

The ABC Model

A — Activating event (something happens)
B — Belief (what you tell yourself about it)
C — Consequence (how you feel and act)

Most people think A causes C. Ellis said B causes C. Change B, and you change C.

Two people lose their jobs. One panics and blames everyone. The other stays calm and looks for work. Same event. Different belief systems.

He called his method Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT).

  • Irrational thoughts — black and white, fixed, absolute. "I'm a total failure."
  • Rational thoughts — flexible, adapting to reality. "That job didn't work out. What's next?"
Analogy: The Placebo Effect

Patients given sugar pills often get better — because they believe the medicine works. Belief changes the body. That's how powerful your thoughts are.

Path 2: Humanistic — Find Fulfilment

Erich Fromm (1900–1980)

Fromm mixed Marx with Freud. He said our suffering comes from separation anxiety:

  • First, we separated from nature — when we developed reason, we realised we were different from other animals.
  • Then, we separated from each other — modernity broke up families, tribes, and communities.

We're the loneliest species on the planet. His cure? Love. Not romantic love — the creative capacity to reconnect with nature and other people, through art and creativity.

His book Escape from Freedom argued that freedom is terrifying. When people feel too free, they often escape into conformity, authoritarianism, or consumerism — anything to feel safe again.

Carl Rogers (1902–1987)

Rogers said we suffer because we're too rigid. We want reality to match our wishes. When it doesn't, we get frustrated, anxious, then ill.

His cure: be open. To new experiences, new ideas, new emotions. Trust yourself.

He said we're not human beings — we're human becomings. Always changing.

Rogers's Three Conditions for Growth

1. Unconditional positive regard — being accepted without conditions. "I accept you as you are."

2. Empathy — truly understanding the other person's experience.

3. Congruence — being genuine, not hiding behind a professional mask.

When these three are present, people naturally move toward growth and healing.

Abraham Maslow (1908–1970)

Maslow's famous Hierarchy of Needs has two sections:

SectionNeeds
Deficiency needs
(survival)		Air, food, water, sleep, warmth, exercise → safety → belonging → recognition
Growth needs
(fulfilment)		Cognitive (knowing) → Aesthetic (beauty) → Self-actualisation → Self-transcendence (helping others)

Self-actualisation means becoming everything you're capable of becoming. Maslow said self-actualised people tend to be:

  • Creative
  • Spontaneous but ethical
  • Comfortable with solitude
  • Focused on problems outside themselves
  • Capable of "peak experiences" — moments of intense joy, wonder, and connection

The top of the pyramid — self-transcendence — is about becoming greater than yourself by helping others. That's close to the Eastern idea of enlightenment.

Path 3: Existential — Find Meaning

Existentialism says: existence precedes essence. You are born first, with no fixed purpose, and then you define yourself through your choices.

In therapy, this means: you are responsible for creating meaning in your life.

Viktor Frankl (1905–1997)

Frankl spent three years in Nazi concentration camps. He survived. And he wrote Man's Search for Meaning.

His conclusion: if you have a strong enough "why", you can survive almost any "how."

Frankl's Most Famous Line

"Suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds meaning."

Meaning isn't created — it's discovered. Through work, through creativity, and through loving others.

He called his therapy logotherapy (from logos, meaning "meaning"). He said:

  • Life has meaning under all circumstances.
  • Our main motivation is finding that meaning.
  • We have the freedom to find meaning even in suffering.

Rollo May (1909–1994)

May wrote The Meaning of Anxiety (1950). His advice: stop labelling experiences as "good" or "bad." Accept them all equally.

Why? Because every experience — even painful ones — is a brick in the building of your life. Once you accept that, you grow.

He also said anxiety isn't always a problem. Some anxiety is a signal that something important is at stake — that you're facing a choice that matters.

Path 4: Cognitive Therapy — Test Your Thoughts

Aaron Beck (1921–2021) said: let's stop relying on the invisible unconscious and start working with perception, which we can actually observe.

His method: compare your thoughts against reality. Is the world really as terrifying as you think? Is it really as perfect as you imagine?

Common Cognitive Distortions

  • All-or-nothing thinking — "If I don't get an A, I'm a failure."
  • Catastrophising — "If I fail this test, my life is over."
  • Mind reading — "They didn't say hello; they must hate me."
  • Fortune telling — "I know I'll mess this up."
  • Overgeneralisation — "I failed once, so I always fail."
  • Personalisation — "It's my fault they're unhappy."
Paris Syndrome

Some Japanese tourists imagine Paris as a romantic paradise. When they meet real Parisians — sometimes cold and rude — the shock is so big they need therapy. That's a perception-reality gap.

Beck's motto: "Don't trust me — test me."

Flow: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934–2021) described the state of flow: when you're so absorbed in something that you forget time, forget yourself, and feel completely clear.

How to reach it: the task must be slightly harder than your current skill. Too hard = anxiety. Too easy = boredom. Just right = flow.

Analogy

It's like a video game that's perfectly tuned to your level. Not frustrating, not boring. You just... play. Hours vanish.

Cognitive Psychology

Mind vs. computer

The Big Idea

Behaviourism said the environment controls you. Psychoanalysis said your unconscious controls you. Cognitive psychology said: you can take control. The mind is not a hindrance — it's a powerful, adaptable machine.

The Computer Revolution

Cognitive psychology was born around the same time as the modern computer. Both happened in the 1940s and 50s. And the timing matters:

  • The computer gave psychologists a new metaphor for the mind: an information processor.
  • It also gave them a new tool: information theory, which could be applied to human memory and attention.
  • Alan Turing's code-breaking work in WWII showed that machines could "think" in a limited way.

The Information Processing Model

The mind is like a computer:

  1. Input — sensory information comes in.
  2. Encoding — the mind transforms it into a usable format.
  3. Storage — it's kept in memory.
  4. Retrieval — it's brought back when needed.
  5. Output — behaviour, speech, decisions.

Perception: You're Not a Passive Camera

Wolfgang Köhler (1887–1967) studied chimpanzees. He found they didn't just learn by trial and error — they had moments of insight. A sudden "aha!"

Leon Festinger (1919–1989) introduced cognitive dissonance: when reality contradicts your belief, you feel uncomfortable — and you'll twist reality to keep your belief.

The Doomsday Cult Study

A cult believed the world would end on a specific date. It didn't. Instead of admitting they were wrong, the members became more convinced. This is cognitive dissonance in action. When the belief is strong enough, evidence doesn't change it — it strengthens it.

Roger Shepard (1929–2022) called perception an "externally guided hallucination." Your mind builds a 3D model of the world inside your head. You think it's real — because it matches what's outside.

Attention: A Limited Resource

Donald Broadbent (1926–1993) studied fighter pilots. He found that accidents happened when pilots were overloaded with information.

His conclusion: attention has a bottleneck. Only one message gets through at a time.

Other Attention Findings

  • The cocktail party effect — even in a noisy room, you can focus on one voice. But if someone says your name across the room, you notice.
  • Dichotic listening — when different messages are played in each ear, people can only report the one they're attending to.
  • Inattentional blindness — when you're focused on one thing, you can miss something obvious. The famous "invisible gorilla" experiment showed that people counting basketball passes didn't notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through.
Analogy: The Spotlight

Attention is like a spotlight on a stage. It illuminates one area clearly while the rest stays dark. You can move the spotlight, but you can only light up one thing at a time.

Memory: Not a Hard Drive

Unlike a computer, you cannot delete memories. Good and bad, they stay.

Bluma Zeigarnik (1901–1988)We remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones. Waiters remember who hasn't paid. This is why taking breaks helps learning — the unfinished task stays active in memory.
George Miller (1920–2012)Short-term memory holds about 7 ± 2 chunks of information. Beyond that, we get overwhelmed. So we "chunk" things into categories. (Phone numbers are chunked: 555-1234, not 5551234.)

Elizabeth Loftus (1944–)Memory can be false. Leading questions, other people, and later events all distort what you remember. This changed how courts treat witness testimony.
Daniel Schacter (1952–)The "seven sins of memory": it's fleeting, absent-minded, blocking, misattributing, suggestible, biased, and it holds onto trauma.

Loftus's Key Findings

  • The wording of a question changes memory. If you ask "How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" people report higher speeds than if you say "hit."
  • False memories can be planted. In one study, she convinced participants they'd been lost in a shopping mall as children — a completely made-up event.
  • Confidence ≠ accuracy. A witness can be absolutely certain and completely wrong.
Why Forgetting Is Good

If you remembered every single detail of every day, you'd be buried in useless information. Forgetting is a feature, not a bug. Your brain is constantly pruning what doesn't matter.

Social Psychology

How groups change us

The Big Idea

We like to think we're independent thinkers. We're not. Groups have enormous power over what we say, do, and even what we see.

The Self Needs Others

Hegel said: you can't have a self without other selves. Jacques Lacan added: your identity is built through the language of others.

Imagine you're the only person in the universe. No one gave birth to you. No one ever spoke to you. Would you even have a personality? Probably not.

Virginia Satir said the family is a factory that makes us. We bump against each other, give feedback, criticise, and slowly become who we are.

Conformity: Solomon Asch (1955)

Asch showed people two cards. One had a single line. The other had three lines of different lengths. Which line matches?

Easy, right? Except: everyone else in the room was secretly told to give the wrong answer first.

Result: Over 30% of real participants went along with the group — even though they could see the group was wrong.

Variations of the Asch Experiment

  • Group size: One dissenter didn't change much. Three or more increased conformity. Beyond 5, it levelled off.
  • Unanimity: If even one other person gave the correct answer, conformity dropped dramatically.
  • Private vs public: When answers were private, conformity almost disappeared. We conform to avoid social discomfort, not because we actually believe the group.
What This Shows

We'd rather be wrong together than right alone. Group pressure can bend what we're willing to say — and sometimes what we're willing to see.

Obedience: Stanley Milgram (1961)

After WWII, people asked: why did ordinary Germans follow terrible orders? Milgram tested it.

He told participants to give electric shocks to a "learner" who screamed in pain. A man in a lab coat said: "Please continue."

Result: 65% went all the way to the maximum 450 volts. Only 35% refused.

And crucially — the participants were distressed. They hated it. But they obeyed anyway.

Variations of the Milgram Experiment

  • When the authority figure gave orders by phone, obedience dropped to 21%.
  • When the authority was a peer (another participant), obedience dropped further.
  • When the learner was in the same room, obedience dropped.
  • When two authority figures disagreed, obedience dropped.

This shows that situational factors matter enormously — not just personality.

Philip Zimbardo: The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)

24 ordinary students. Randomly split into "guards" and "prisoners." Placed in a fake prison in a university basement.

Within days, the guards became abusive — denying food, using the toilet as a punishment, humiliating the prisoners. The experiment had to be stopped after 6 days.

Zimbardo's conclusion: given the right role and the right situation, good people can do terrible things.

Criticisms of the SPE

The Stanford Prison Experiment has been heavily criticised. Later analysis suggests:

  • Participants may have been acting — playing the role they thought the experimenter wanted (demand characteristics).
  • Zimbardo himself played a role as "prison superintendent," which may have encouraged the guards.
  • A later BBC prison study in 2002 found very different results — the prisoners actually took control.

So the experiment is important historically, but its conclusions are now debated.

The Bystander Effect

In 1964, a woman named Kitty Genovese was murdered in New York. Newspaper reports claimed that 38 people saw or heard the attack and did nothing.

Whether or not the number was accurate, the story prompted Darley and Latané to study when people help and when they don't.

The Bystander Effect

The more people present, the less likely any one person is to help. Why? Two reasons:

  • Diffusion of responsibility — "Someone else will do it."
  • Pluralistic ignorance — everyone looks to everyone else to figure out if this is an emergency. If nobody acts, everyone assumes it's not serious.

This is why modern first-aid advice says: point to one person and say "You — call an ambulance." It breaks the diffusion of responsibility.

Cognitive Dissonance in Social Life

Leon Festinger showed that when our actions contradict our beliefs, we feel uncomfortable — and we often change the belief to match the action, not the other way around.

Elliot Aronson expanded this in The Social Animal (1972). He studied a shooting in 1970 where an armed officer shot anti-war protesters. He found that people spread false rumours that the protesters were bad people — to justify the shooting and reduce their own discomfort.

Analogy

Cognitive dissonance is like a mental itch. You'll scratch it by changing your belief, changing your behaviour, or adding a new belief that bridges the gap.

Social Identity Theory

Henri Tajfel (1919–1982) showed that simply putting people into arbitrary groups — even based on something trivial like preferring one painter over another — creates in-group favouritism and out-group hostility.

We don't need a real reason to prefer "us" over "them." The group itself is enough.

Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (1954) showed that two groups of boys at a summer camp became hostile when made to compete — and that hostility could be reduced by giving them a shared goal (superordinate goals).

Groupthink

Irving Janis (1918–1990) studied bad decisions made by groups — like the Bay of Pigs invasion. He found that when a group is:

  • Highly cohesive
  • Isolated from outside opinions
  • Led by a strong, directive leader
  • Under pressure to decide quickly

...it tends to suppress dissent and make bad choices. This is groupthink.

The cure: encourage dissent, invite outside experts, and have a "devil's advocate."

Other Key Social Psychology Findings

Robert Zajonc (1923–2008)The mere exposure effect: the more you see something, the more you like it. This is why advertising works. Couples who live together for 25 years even start to look alike.
Kurt Lewin (1890–1980)You can't understand a system until you try to change it. Social change meets resistance — until people see the benefit.
William Glasser (1925–2013)Choice theory: we seek pleasure and avoid pain — but we do it best inside a community. His reality therapy is about taking responsibility for your choices.
Ignacio Martín-Baró (1942–1989)Trauma is often a community problem, not just an individual one. Oppression, dictatorship, and war create mental illness. His cure: liberation psychology.
Martin Seligman (1942–)Found that good social relationships are one of the strongest predictors of happiness. He founded positive psychology — the study of what makes life worth living.

What did the Asch experiment show?
That group pressure can make people give a wrong answer they know is wrong. Over 30% conformed. When one other person dissented, conformity dropped sharply.
What percentage of Milgram's participants went to the maximum shock?
65%. And they were visibly distressed while doing it — showing how powerful authority pressure is.
What is the bystander effect?
The more people present, the less likely any one person is to help. This happens because of diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance.
What is groupthink?
When a cohesive group suppresses dissent and makes bad decisions because everyone is afraid to speak up. The cure is encouraging dissent and outside opinions.

Child Psychology

How children think differently

The Big Idea

A child is not a small adult. Children think, learn, and feel in fundamentally different ways — and some abilities only exist at certain ages.

Language: Chomsky vs. Skinner

B. F. Skinner said children learn language through conditioning — parents reward correct sounds and discourage incorrect ones.

Noam Chomsky (1928–) disagreed. He said the ability to learn language is innate — built into the brain.

Chomsky's Evidence
  • Almost all children learn to talk at roughly the same age, all over the world.
  • Children produce sentences they've never heard before — showing they're not just imitating.
  • Children make systematic errors (like "goed" instead of "went") — showing they've internalised rules, not just copied.
  • His universal grammar theory says children already know the basic structure of language without being taught.

Chomsky won the debate. Language is now seen as both innate and shaped by environment — nature and nurture working together.

Analogy

Language is like a body organ. It grows. It has a schedule. And there's a critical window — learn it young and it's easy; learn it late and it's hard.

Piaget: Learning by Doing

Jean Piaget (1899–1980) was a Swiss psychologist who interviewed children in free-flowing conversations.

His key insight: children learn by acting, not by listening. Adults think first, then act. Children act first, then maybe think.

Piaget's Famous Line

"Intelligence is what you use when you don't know what to do."

Conservation example: Pour the same amount of water into a tall thin glass and a short wide glass. A pre-operational child says the tall glass has more — because they focus on height, not volume. A concrete operational child knows they're the same.

Vygotsky: Learning Is Social

Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) disagreed with Piaget's focus on the individual. He said: learning is social. Parents, teachers, and peers shape not just what we know, but how we think.

Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)

Vygotsky's key concept: the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help.

  • Below the ZPD: Too easy — the child can already do it.
  • Inside the ZPD: Challenging but achievable with guidance.
  • Above the ZPD: Too hard — the child gets frustrated.

Teachers should target the ZPD. This is called scaffolding — providing support that is gradually removed as the child becomes competent.

Attachment: Bowlby and Harlow

John Bowlby (1907–1990) studied children evacuated during WWII. Those without a stable mother figure struggled with social and emotional development. He called the worst case affectionless psychopathy.

But Harry Harlow (1905–1981) tested Freud's idea that babies attach to whoever feeds them. He gave baby monkeys two fake mothers:

  • Wire mother — with a milk bottle attached.
  • Cloth mother — soft and cuddly, no food.

The monkeys went to the wire mother only to eat — then ran back to the cloth mother for comfort and safety. Attachment is about security, not just food.

Analogy

A parent is not just a restaurant. A parent is a base camp — a safe place you return to before exploring the world again.

G. Stanley Hall: Adolescence

G. Stanley Hall (1844–1924) described adolescence (roughly 11–15) as a period of "storm and stress" — emotional turmoil and rebellion.

Why? Because historically, this is when a child left the nest to find a mate. Love hits teenagers like a volcano.

He said: "Adolescence is when the very worst and best impulses in the human soul struggle against each other."

Bandura: The Bobo Doll (1961)

Albert Bandura (1925–2021) showed children an adult being aggressive towards a Bobo doll (punching, kicking, hitting with a mallet).

Then he left the children alone with the doll.

Result: The children who saw the aggressive adult were far more aggressive themselves. Children learn by observing and imitating.

Important

Bandura disagreed with the idea that watching violence "gets it out of your system." He said: exposure to aggression makes aggression more likely, not less.

Kohlberg: Moral Development

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987) studied 56 boys over 20 years. He used moral dilemmas — the most famous being the Heinz dilemma:

Heinz's wife is dying of cancer. A druggist has the cure but charges 10 times what it costs to make. Heinz can't afford it. Should Heinz steal the drug?

Kohlberg wasn't interested in the answer — he was interested in the reasoning. He found three levels:

  1. Pre-conventional — Good and bad = reward and punishment. "I won't steal because I'll get in trouble."
  2. Conventional — You understand why society calls things right or wrong, and you follow the rules to fit in. "Stealing is against the law, so it's wrong."
  3. Post-conventional — You go beyond conformity. You might break an unjust rule because it's morally right. "Her life is worth more than the druggist's profit, so stealing is justified."

Psychology of the Sexes

Why men and women want different things

The Big Idea

Men and women have different biological costs when it comes to reproduction. Those costs created different psychological strategies for finding a mate.

A Note Before We Start

This lesson describes general patterns and averages, not rules about individuals. There's huge overlap between men and women — most differences are small. And this topic is politically sensitive, so read it as "what the research suggests," not "what must be true about you."

The Biology First

Humans are dimorphic — male and female bodies are different. But the biggest difference isn't size. It's cost.

Trivers' Parental Investment Theory

Robert Trivers (1943–2016) proposed a key idea in evolutionary biology:

Parental Investment

The sex that invests more in offspring becomes the choosier sex. The sex that invests less competes for access to the choosier sex.

In humans, women invest far more (pregnancy, birth, breastfeeding). So women are the selectors, and men compete.

Analogy

Imagine buying a lottery ticket. For one person it costs ₹10. For the other it costs their entire life savings. Who's going to be more careful about which ticket they buy?

What Women Tend to Look For

Research by David Buss and others found women generally prioritise:

  • Ability to provide — resources, stability, financial security
  • Status — respect from others, competence, power
  • Ambition & intelligence — signs of future success
  • Height & strength — protection
  • Older age — usually means more resources and maturity

One surprising finding: women who earn a lot themselves still tend to want a partner who earns the same or more. This is called hypergamy.

What Men Tend to Look For

  • Youth — a sign of fertility
  • Beauty — a cue for healthy genes
  • Chastity — historically, a way to be confident about paternity

Why chastity? Because human females don't show when they're fertile (unlike most animals). So historically, a man could never be certain a child was his. The "safest" strategy was to choose a virgin. Today, DNA testing solves that problem.

The Simple Summary

Men compete for resources. Women compete for beauty and youth.
Men show off cars and status. Women show off appearance. Both are peacocking.

Hormones and the Brain

Paul Zak (1962–) found that women release more oxytocin — sometimes called the "moral molecule" because it appears when you help or care for someone. Estrogen encourages its release.

Testosterone blocks oxytocin. This helps explain male aggression.

Women also have a relatively larger hippocampus (long-term memory), which helps them read emotional subtleties — but taken to an extreme, it becomes neuroticism.

Simon Baron-Cohen: Empathy vs. Systems

Simon Baron-Cohen (1958–) argues:

  • The female brain is wired mostly for empathy — reading people, emotions, and social cues.
  • The male brain is wired mostly for systems — how things work, rules, patterns, problem-solving.

His evidence: autism is far more common in boys. Autistic children struggle with social connection but often have intense focus on systems (numbers, computers, patterns). He calls autism "the extreme male brain."

But — about 17% of men have an empathising brain, and 17% of women have a systemising brain. There's lots of overlap.

A Note on Autism

Baron-Cohen's theory is influential but debated. Many autistic people and researchers now prefer the neurodiversity framework — autism is a different way of being, not a defect. The "extreme male brain" theory also doesn't explain why some girls are autistic but diagnosed later.

Maccoby: The Schooling Surprise

Eleanor Maccoby (1917–2018) reviewed 1,600 studies. Most differences were small. But one kept showing up: girls do better in school.

Why? Modern schooling is mostly about sitting still and listening. Research suggests females tend to be more agreeable and better at that. More than 90% of prisoners are men — the same trait (aggression/risk-taking) that causes problems in class also causes problems in life.

Jordan Peterson and the Controversy

Jordan Peterson (1962–) is a Canadian psychologist who argues that male and female brains are wired differently — women for empathy and nurturing, men for problem-solving and systems.

He has faced strong criticism from feminists who argue that emphasising biological differences perpetuates stereotypes and limits opportunities.

The debate is genuine and important. Most scientists agree that some differences exist on average, but that they are small, overlapping, and heavily shaped by culture.

The Biggest Picture

Evolution says: females are the selectors. A society with one man and 100 women survives. A society with 100 men and one woman does not.

That's why:

  • Men historically went to war; women were evacuated first.
  • Over 90% of workplace deaths are men.
  • Men take bigger risks — to be noticed, to provide, to compete.

And it's why, 8,000 years ago, for every one man who reproduced, about 17 women did. Only a minority of men "won."

But Times Are Changing

In rich countries, women now earn their own money, and birth rates are falling. Men are no longer needed as providers or protectors in the same way. Nobody knows exactly where this leads — we're all part of the experiment.

Critiques of Evolutionary Psychology

  • Just-so stories. It's easy to make up an evolutionary explanation for anything after the fact. The challenge is testing it.
  • Ignores culture. Many gender differences vary hugely across cultures — which suggests they're not purely biological.
  • Overgeneralises from the past. We don't know for sure how our ancestors lived. Hunter-gatherer societies varied enormously.
  • Political misuse. Evolutionary claims have been used to justify sexism, racism, and eugenics. We should be careful.

Why do women tend to be more selective about mating?
Because the biological cost is far higher for women — pregnancy, painful birth, and historically a high risk of death. Higher cost means higher selectivity. This is Trivers' parental investment theory.
What is hypergamy?
The tendency for women to choose partners who are equal to or above them in status, income, height, or age. Interestingly, it's even stronger in women who are financially successful themselves.

The Paradoxes

Why psychology can never fully succeed

The Big Idea

Psychology has one impossible problem: the observer is also the observed. The moment you measure a person, they change. This makes psychology an approximate science — powerful, useful, but never finished.

Zeno's Paradox of the Mind

Zeno said: to cross a room, you must first cross half of it. Then half of what's left. Then half again. You can never arrive — you can only get closer.

Psychology has the same problem. As soon as you understand today's psyche, it has already changed. Psychology is always playing catch-up.

Analogy

It's like a camera trying to photograph itself. The camera is the tool and the subject at the same time. Something is always missing from the picture.

This is similar to quantum physics: the mere act of observation changes what you're observing.

Gordon Allport: Stop Looking Backwards

Gordon Allport (1897–1967) criticised psychology for being obsessed with the past.

Allport's Complaint

"People are busy leading their lives into the future, whereas psychology for the most part is busy tracing them into the past."

So he studied traits — the words we use to describe people. He found 18,000, narrowed them to 4,500, and organised them into three types:

  • Cardinal traits — the one thing that defines you completely. The explorer, the artist, the poet.
  • Common traits — general things like honesty or aggression, shared with others.
  • Secondary traits — small preferences, barely noticeable.

He also split our traits into genotype (internal, biological — what helps you survive alone) and phenotype (external, social — how you act with others).

Intelligence Isn't Fixed Either

Alfred Binet (1857–1911) developed one of the first IQ tests. But he insisted intelligence is not fixed — it can grow. It changes as a child develops.

Later thinkers pushed this further:

J. P. Guilford (1897–1987)IQ tests only measure convergent thinking — one right answer. But real intelligence includes divergent thinking: creativity, originality, fluency, flexibility.
Raymond Cattell (1905–1998)Two types of intelligence: fluid (reasoning without prior knowledge — innate) and crystallised (knowledge from experience — learned).
Hans Eysenck (1916–1997)Studied the link between genius and madness. Geniuses and psychiatric patients both showed "over-inclusive thinking" — a wider web of associations. Psychoticism seems tied to creativity.
Walter Mischel (1930–2018)The marshmallow test: children who could wait 20 minutes for two marshmallows did better in life. He argued personality changes depending on situation — behaviour reveals personality, not the other way around.
The Marshmallow Test Revisited

Later research suggested the marshmallow test is not as predictive as originally thought. Children from wealthier families waited longer — likely because they trusted that the reward would actually come. So the test may measure trust and stability, not just self-control.

Anti-Psychiatry

In recent decades, psychiatry has faced serious criticism. Three main complaints:

  1. Inventing illnesses. The list of official disorders keeps growing.
  2. Over-medicating. Millions take medication they find hard to stop.
  3. Bad at diagnosing. See below.

Rosenhan's Experiment (1973)

David Rosenhan (1929–2012) sent healthy people into mental hospitals, pretending to hear vague noises. They were all admitted.

Once inside, they acted completely normally. The staff didn't notice. They stayed about 20 days on average.

Then Rosenhan told a hospital: "We're sending in fake patients — can you spot them?" Out of 193 real patients, staff wrongly flagged 41 as fake.

What This Shows

Psychiatrists are just people. They have the same biases and blind spots as anyone. Mental illness is not always as clear-cut as a broken bone.

But Wait — A Caveat

Later investigation revealed that Rosenhan may have exaggerated or fabricated parts of his study. The hospital that was told to expect impostors also claimed he misrepresented the results. So the study is now considered unreliable — but it still sparked important reforms in psychiatric diagnosis.

Thomas Szasz

Thomas Szasz (1920–2012) wrote The Myth of Mental Illness (1961). He argued that "mental illness" is often just a label we stick on people when life gets too hard for them — a way of controlling behaviour that society doesn't like.

The Replication Crisis

This is the biggest challenge facing psychology today.

What Happened

In 2015, a huge international project tried to repeat 100 famous psychology studies. Only about 36% produced the same results. Many classic findings — like "power posing" or some priming effects — failed to replicate.

Why does this happen?

  • Small samples. Many studies used only a few dozen participants.
  • P-hacking. Researchers unconsciously run many analyses until they find a "significant" result.
  • Publication bias. Journals prefer positive results. Null findings never get published.
  • Questionable research practices. Dropping data points, changing hypotheses after the fact, etc.
The Good News

Psychology is now fixing itself. Researchers are pre-registering studies, sharing data, and using larger samples. The crisis is a sign of maturity, not failure.

The Final Paradox

The Core Problem

The more psychology learns, the more disorders it diagnoses. The more parameters it adds, the more "illnesses" it finds. It's a science with no finish line.

And the deepest mystery — consciousness itself — remains unsolved. We can map every neuron and still not explain why red looks red, or why pain hurts.

Where Does Psychology Go From Here?

  • Neuroscience. Brain imaging is getting better every year. We're learning more about which brain areas do what.
  • Genetics. We're starting to understand how genes influence personality, intelligence, and mental illness.
  • Culture. More research is being done outside Western, educated, industrialised countries. Most psychology was built on WEIRD samples (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic).
  • Technology. Smartphones, social media, and AI are changing how we think and relate. Psychology is racing to keep up.
  • Integration. The future is probably not one school but a combination — biological, cognitive, social, and evolutionary all working together.

Check Your Understanding

What did Rosenhan's experiment prove?
That psychiatrists couldn't tell healthy people from mentally ill people in a hospital setting. It showed mental illness diagnosis is far more subjective than physical diagnosis.
What's the difference between fluid and crystallised intelligence?
Fluid intelligence is raw reasoning ability without prior knowledge (innate). Crystallised intelligence is knowledge and skill learned from experience.

ArchetypeA universal pattern or character that appears in myths and stories across all cultures. Jung believed they're inherited.

BehaviourismThe school that studies only observable behaviour, ignoring inner mental states.
CatharsisThe release of strong emotions through expression — art, writing, talking, crying.
Classical conditioningLearning by association. Pavlov's dogs learned to drool at a bell.
Cognitive dissonanceThe discomfort you feel when your beliefs and actions contradict. You usually change the belief.
Collective unconsciousJung's idea that we inherit memories and patterns from our ancestors.
ConsciousnessYour awareness of yourself and the world. Includes conscious, subconscious, and unconscious levels.
Defence mechanismUnconscious strategies to protect the ego from anxiety — repression, denial, projection, etc.
DissociationWhen trauma stays in a separate part of the mind and gets triggered later.
EgoFreud's term for the rational, mediating part of the mind.
EmpiricismThe idea that knowledge comes from experience and the senses.
Episodic memoryMemories of events, recalled by time and place.
FlowA state of complete absorption in an activity, where time disappears.
FunctionalismJames's idea that consciousness is a stream, studied by its function, not its structure.
GestaltA holistic approach: the whole is more than the sum of its parts.
HeuristicA mental shortcut. Fast and usually useful, but sometimes wrong.
IdFreud's term for the primitive, instinctual part of the mind.
IdealismThe idea that everything is made of ideas or mind, not matter.
ImprintingLorenz's term for rapid bonding in young animals during a critical period.

MaterialismThe idea that everything is made of matter — atoms, cells, particles.

NeuroticismA personality trait: the tendency to experience negative emotions easily.
Operant conditioningLearning through consequences. Skinner's rats pressed levers for food.

PsychoanalysisFreud's school: the unconscious drives most of our behaviour.

ShadowJung's term for the hidden, dark part of yourself.
StructuralismWundt's approach: consciousness can be broken into parts.
SuperegoFreud's term for the internalised moral voice.
UnconsciousThe deepest level of the mind — hidden from direct awareness.
ZPD (Zone of Proximal Development)Vygotsky's idea: the gap between what you can do alone and with help.

The Whole Course on One Page

Everything you just learned, in a nutshell

Part 1: Origins

  • Psychology = philosophy (father) + literature (mother).
  • Ancient Greece gave us the four humors. India gave us meditation and self-realisation. The Islamic world gave us the mind-body split (Ibn Sina's Floating Man, 600 years before Descartes).
  • Science broke religion's grip. Schopenhauer gave us the "blind will." Darwin made us animals. Wundt opened the first lab in 1879.

Part 2: The Mind

  • Structuralism (Wundt): the mind is a machine you can take apart.
  • Functionalism (James): the mind is a stream you can only watch.
  • Three levels: conscious (lit), subconscious (dim), unconscious (dark).
  • Freud's model: id (want), ego (mediate), superego (moralise).

Part 3: Three Big Schools

  • Behaviourism (Pavlov, Watson, Skinner): watch what people do. Rewards beat punishment. Variable ratio is the most addictive schedule.
  • Psychoanalysis (Freud, Jung, Adler): the unconscious runs you. Freud = individual. Jung = collective. Adler = inferiority and social interest.
  • Cognitive (Beck, Miller, Loftus, Kahneman): you can take control. Perception, attention, memory, heuristics.

Part 4: Therapy

  • Gestalt (Perls): take responsibility. Nobody can make you feel anything.
  • REBT (Ellis): irrational beliefs cause suffering. Change the belief.
  • Humanistic (Fromm, Rogers, Maslow): find fulfilment. Be open. Love. Grow.
  • Existential (Frankl, May): find meaning. "Suffering ceases to be suffering when it finds meaning."
  • Cognitive therapy (Beck): test your thoughts against reality. Don't trust me — test me.
  • Flow (Csikszentmihalyi): challenge slightly above skill.

Part 5: Outside the Individual

  • Social: Asch (conformity), Milgram (obedience), Zimbardo (roles), bystander effect, groupthink, Tajfel (social identity), Zajonc (mere exposure).
  • Child: Chomsky (language is innate), Piaget (learn by doing), Vygotsky (ZPD), Bowlby & Harlow (attachment), Ainsworth (strange situation), Bandura (we imitate), Kohlberg (morality), Erikson (8 stages), Baumrind (parenting styles).
  • Sexes: Different biological costs → different mating strategies (Trivers). Women select; men compete. Baron-Cohen (empathy vs systems).

Part 6: The Paradox

  • The observer is also the observed. Measuring the mind changes it.
  • Psychology is always playing catch-up.
  • The replication crisis showed many findings were overstated — but the field is fixing itself.
  • Consciousness remains unsolved. That's not a failure — it's the frontier.
Final Thought

Psychology hasn't solved consciousness. It probably won't for a long time. But along the way, it has given us real tools: how to learn better, how to heal, how to understand why we do what we do.

The mystery isn't a failure. It's the whole point.
