A field guide · drawn from a conversation with Tony Fadell

Starting From Pain to Build Great Products

Tony Fadell co-created the iPod, the iPhone, and the Nest thermostat, holds more than 300 patents, and wrote the builder's book Build. In this conversation he explains how he decides what is worth building, why 1.0 decisions are opinion decisions, why you need three generations, and why marketing and storytelling are part of the product itself. This lesson turns that conversation into eight working principles.

Source: Lenny's Podcast (Lenny Rachitsky) — transcript, pasted. Guest: Tony Fadell, author of Build, founder of the Build Collective.

What You'll Learn

  1. Spot a real, long-lived pain worth solving — and pair it with a technology that has finally become ready.
  2. Make opinion-based decisions with taste when data doesn't exist yet, instead of faking data-driven confidence.
  3. Micromanage the few decisions that matter, and delegate everything else.
  4. Plan for three generations: make the product, fix the product, fix the business.
  5. Design the whole customer journey — marketing, discovery, and word of mouth included — not just the artifact.
  6. Keep humans in the loop, tell the why not just the what, and build for the long term.

Mnemonic for the whole lesson: Pain·Taste·Team·Three·Tell·Story·Humans·Tomorrow — start from Pain, let Taste decide, orchestrate the Team, plan Three generations, Tell the market where to find you, wrap it in Story, keep Humans in the loop, and think about Tomorrow.

01

Start from Pain — What's Worth Building

pain-firstwhy nowredefine the spaceNest · iPod · iPhone

When a colleague asked Fadell how he decides what is worth building, he didn't start with technology or markets. He started with pain. Most pains are old: they were created either as an unintentional consequence of some product, or as a limitation of the technology at the time that product was made. The product evolved over the years but never revolutionized itself — so the same pain kept being treated with a mild painkiller, just enough that the new problem was worth tolerating. The trick is to find that long-lived, habituated-away pain and then ask the second question: is there a new technology that can finally solve it? That's the "why now." The Nest thermostat is the cleanest example: heating and cooling is roughly half of an energy bill, and everyone hated the arcane programmable thermostat they'd been given rebates for — but nobody used it because programming it was like writing ECR code. The new technology was machine learning, which could learn your patterns instead of requiring programming. Bond the old pain with the new technology, and you don't just improve a product — you redefine the space: how it's installed (self-install, not third-party), how it's bought (retail, not through the installer), and what it costs ($249, because it saves $800–$1,200 a year and pays for itself). The iPod wasn't the iPod: it was the iPod plus iTunes plus the iTunes Music Store. The iPhone wasn't the phone: it was the iPhone plus the App Store. Think about the full system you're innovating with, not the single artifact you remember.

Step by step: finding what's worth building

  1. Find the pain. Current pain, or pain you can see on the horizon — often habituated away, so you have to discover it, not ask for it.
  2. Diagnose why it persists. The old painkiller was a limitation of the technology of its era, and the product evolved without ever revolutionizing.
  3. Find the technology that just arrived. Multi-touch, portable mass storage, lithium-ion cells, machine learning — something that wasn't possible when the pain was created.
  4. Bond them and redefine the space. Not just the product: installation, purchase, price model, and the surrounding system (iPod + iTunes + store; iPhone + App Store).
  5. Make the math obvious. $249 vs. $800–$1,200 a year in savings — the crazy opinion-based decision that pays for itself.

obvious exampleNest thermostat

Half your energy bill, an interface you hate, a programmable thermostat nobody could program. Machine learning finally made "learn my patterns" possible — and Nest reinvented install and purchase too, not just the dial.

counter-intuitive exampleGeneral Magic

The same team built the iPhone's ideas 15 years too early. Cool things nobody needed. The technology existed; the pain and timing didn't. "We made the iPhone 15 years too early" is the cautionary half of the formula.

edge-case exampleiPod's converging tech

Portable mass storage, MP3s, lithium-ion prismatic polymer cells, and low-power ARM processors all had to arrive at once. Any single one was a gadget; the convergence made the category. New technology isn't one thing — it's a stack that finally clicks.

Visual analogy — the painkiller you forgot you had

A chronic ache you've lived with for years stops registering — you've habituated away the pain, and you medicate it daily without thinking. That daily medication is the old product: "it gave you enough of a painkiller for the other problem that having this new pain was worth it." The winning product is the cure that was impossible to manufacture until last year.

❌ What people get wrong

"Start with a cool technology and find a use for it." That's the General Magic mistake — brilliant, premature, unneeded. And "just solve a pain" is incomplete too: without the second half of the formula (a technology that's finally ready), you're shipping another evolutionary painkiller, not a redefinition.

Why this matters

A real company can't be throwaway. Ideas that start from pain and arrive at the right moment build categories and compound; ideas that start from novelty win a demo day and then vanish. The formula is also your filter: if you can't name the pain and the new technology, you don't have a product yet.

From pain you go to judgment: who gets to decide which pain is worth solving? That's Module 2 — opinion-based decisions and taste.

02

Opinion-Based Decisions and Taste Makers

1.0 decisionstaste makersbenevolent dictatorshipinformed gut

When you're building a 1.0 of anything new — a new category, a device the world hasn't seen — you have very few analogies to make data-driven decisions from. If you force data-driven decisions all the way along, you get one of two things: a copy of something else (because you took the data from another product), or junk data. So Fadell's position is blunt: for a 1.0, most of your decisions are going to be opinion-based decisions, and that means you need a small set of people charged with making them — taste makers — who can walk from a blank whiteboard to an actual 1.0 spec. It's a benevolent dictatorship: "This is what's going to happen, this is the vision, and we don't know what we don't know until we ship it and get opinions from users." The gut is informed, not whimsical — it's built from asking questions, prototyping, refining with experts, and getting the right data, like the keyboard experiments on the iPhone. The team compared virtual vs. physical keyboards for months: speed, error rate, how you correct errors. The data had pros and cons on both sides, and the data alone couldn't decide — so Steve did. "We are going this way. If you're not going to get on board, get out of this room." The hardest environment is consumer: a consumer sees the product only through marketing, discovery, and key features, so you have to ship the whole ecosystem before you get real feedback. Meanwhile, functional companies run on unkind truths; dysfunctional ones run on kind lies. That's why a mission-driven jerk who tells the truth is worth keeping.

Step by step: running a 1.0 on opinion

  1. Accept that 1.0 is opinion-based. No analog data exists for a category the world hasn't seen; pretending otherwise produces copies or junk data.
  2. Appoint the taste makers. One or two people (or a very small team) charged with the opinion-based decisions from whiteboard to 1.0 spec.
  3. Feed the gut with the right data. Run the experiments — keyboard speed, error rate, correction flow — so the opinion is informed.
  4. Decide, then articulate. Explain the decision, why it was made, and how it might affect the market — in full light, because the rest of the team can't see what you see.
  5. Own the risk. "Yes, I might be wrong — and we will correct it later, and we'll take the heat." That's the job.

obvious exampleKeyboard vs. BlackBerry

Only 1–2% of mobile users had a BlackBerry. The debate: win that passionate 2%, or build for the other 98%? Data couldn't settle it — Steve did, betting on the virtual keyboard and the 98%.

counter-intuitive exampleNest's $249 price

Five to six times the price of a dumb thermostat, with no consumer data to justify it. The opinion-based call: it pays for itself in a year or two of energy savings. Data came later, from buyers.

edge-case exampleFunctional vs. dysfunctional

The Twitter chart Fadell loves: unkind truth → unkind truth → unkind truth builds functional systems; kind lie → kind lie → kind lie builds dysfunctional ones. Honesty is an opinion-adjacent policy, not a personality trait.

Visual analogy — conductor, not committee

An orchestra can't be run by vote: eighty musicians, one tempo. The conductor hears the blend no single player hears, sets the interpretation, and owns the result. A benevolent dictatorship in a 1.0 is the same — one informed point of view holding the tempo until the audience (customers) tells you what actually works.

❌ What people get wrong

"More data always makes a better decision." For an established market, yes; for a 1.0, no. And "we need a user study to de-risk this" is often CYA: a user study without the full context of marketing, price, and ecosystem gives you junk data. Consultants are hired to cover for the hard work of actually choosing.

Why this matters

Differentiation is a point of view. Consensus produces commodities; taste produces the iPod, the iPhone, the Nest. If nobody is empowered to decide, the data will decide — which means the loudest existing product will decide.

Once the taste makers decide, someone has to sweat the details that make the decision real — Module 3 — micromanage the decisions that matter.

03

Micromanage the Decisions That Matter

sweat the detailsdecision, not operationsorchestration

"Micromanager" is an insult — and Fadell is here to rehabilitate it. We've heard the phrase "sweat the details": it means micromanagement of certain details, and hands-off of other details, and you have to understand the blend of which things really matter and which don't. Early in his career he thought everything mattered and drove everybody — and himself — nuts, until it became "everybody's got to do it the way I would do it." The correction: there are only a few key things that need to be crystal clear — mostly things the customer touches, or certain manufacturing or cost constraints, or the long-term vision. Everything else, delegate. And crucially, micromanage the decision, not the operations of doing it. On the iPhone keyboard that meant making sure the right data was being collected — micro-specifying and delivering the data pieces needed to build an informed gut. Micromanagement is also the tool for a crisis, and for system-level change where a low-level fix ripples upward: "we could do that, but only if they do this" — when everything is coupled, everyone finds excuses, so you ask "why" over and over until the excuses die. The keyboard itself is the picture: hardware, software, filtering, and screen graphics were all changing and adjusting constantly, and someone had to be the orchestrator of a huge orchestra of components so it all came together harmoniously. The Nest smoke alarm's little warning — "this is about to get very loud" — is a detail you can feel: a product that crystallizes only when people pour love, attention, and care into the details nobody else will fight for.

Step by step: sweating the right details

  1. Identify the few key things. Customer-visible details, cost or manufacturing constraints, the long-term vision — usually a small set.
  2. Micromanage the decision, not the operations. Specify the data you need and how it's measured (speed, errors, corrections), then let people do the work.
  3. Delegate everything else. Hands-off details are a discipline, not a default failure.
  4. Orchestrate coupled systems. When a change ripples across layers, fix all of them at once — and ask "why" until the excuses are gone.
  5. Pour love into the crystallizing details. The ones nobody else will fight for are often the ones customers feel.

obvious exampleiPhone keyboard layers

Hardware, software, filtering, and graphics kept changing together for months. Too many variables for anyone else to hold; someone had to orchestrate all of it at once.

counter-intuitive example"About to get very loud"

Before alarming, the Nest smoke detector says it's about to get loud — so you don't get PTSD at 2 a.m. A tiny, love-poured detail, and the reason people loved the product. Customers can feel care.

edge-case exampleThe orphan product

Nest Protect stayed the best in its category for a decade — then was discontinued because no one inside Google wanted to pour attention into a "stepchild." Crystallized products need sustained love; neglect is a detail-level failure too.

Visual analogy — the orchestra conductor

Each musician is a specialist who can play their part blind. The conductor's job isn't to play any instrument — it's to hold the tempo, balance the blend, and bring dozens of moving parts to a single moment. That's micromanagement of the right things: the decision and the tempo, not the fingerings.

❌ What people get wrong

"Micromanagement is always bad." It's bad when it's everywhere — the early-Fadell mistake of "everything matters, do it my way." It's essential when applied to the few decisions that define the product. The blend is the skill; nobody teaches you which details matter, which is why you ask "why" a lot.

Why this matters

Great products die in the gap between a great decision and its delivery. With too many variables moving at once, someone must own the orchestration — or you get noise, not harmony. Details are where the opinion becomes a product.

The details you sweat get better with time — which is exactly why Module 4 — the three generations rule exists.

04

The Three Generations Rule

make · fix · fix the businessskunk worksfailure = learning

Fadell's rule, honed across the iPod, iPhone, and Nest: everything needs three generations. "You make the product, you fix the product, then you fix the business." Nobody gets it all right the first time — you'd like to, but you don't. Generation one: the product with the point of view. The first iPod sold everything it could in the first quarter — to the Mac loyalists, less than 1% of the market — and then died. Generation two: you fix the product with customer feedback; the numbers got a little better, the features got dialed in, but you still weren't making money. Generation three: you fix the business — margins, volume, distribution, reliability. The iPod only took off when it worked on Windows and had the iTunes Music Store. The first iPods and first iPhones made no money; the iPhone launched AT&T-only, on slow 2.5G, US-only. So the threshold question — "is this big enough?" — gets the honest answer: the iPod wasn't big enough for two generations. You hang in unless the idea itself is fundamentally broken, because "you only fail if you stop. If you keep iterating, keep going, that's not failure — that's learning." And while you wait, you run skunk-works projects: Windows connectivity existed behind the scenes over Steve's dead body, and the stylus was built in secret even though Steve hated it — both became defining features later. The iPod's three-generation patience didn't just build a product line; it saved Apple — $349 to try the brand instead of a $3,000 Mac commitment, then "oh, that's interesting, maybe I should try other products from this company." No iPod, no iPhone; probably no Apple.

Drag the slider through three generations

Gen 1Gen 2Gen 3
1 · Make the product. The opinion-based 1.0. Sold everything to the Mac geeks in the first quarter — less than 1% of the market — then died. The product is right; the market isn't there yet.
2 · Fix the product. Take customer feedback and dial in the features. Numbers get a little better, but you're still not making money. This is where most people quit — wrongly.
3 · Fix the business. Margins, volume, distribution, reliability: Windows connectivity, the iTunes Music Store, real revenue. Now it's a company, not a product.

obvious exampleiPod → Windows + store

Two generations of Mac-only sales plateaued. The third — Windows connectivity (via skunk works) and the iTunes Music Store — is when it took off.

counter-intuitive exampleQuitting is the only failure

Jeff Bezos says the same thing, Fadell notes: you fail a few times till you find your way. "You only fail if you stop. If you keep iterating, that's not failure — that's called learning."

edge-case exampleNest needed generations too

Two generations of the smoke detector and a few of the thermostat before the business worked — not just the product. Even a category-creator had to fix margins and volume.

Visual analogy — three coats of paint

The first coat looks wrong and patchy; the second makes it even; the third is where it finally looks finished. Rushing to judge after coat one — or worse, stopping — is how good products get killed. The same logic explains why "skunk-works" versions of Windows support and the stylus were kept alive in the background: the second and third coats were already being painted.

❌ What people get wrong

"If version 1 isn't a hit, the idea is wrong." The iPod's first two generations were bought only by loyalists — the idea was right and the business wasn't built yet. Quit only when the idea itself is fundamentally broken ("brain-damaged," as Fadell puts it), not when the market hasn't caught up. And remember: "big enough" is a moving threshold — the iPod wasn't big enough, and it still changed the company.

Why this matters

Most companies die in generation two — they fix the product but never fix the business, or they quit after generation one. Patience plus iteration is what separates learning from failure, and it's how a near-bankrupt Apple in 2001 became the company that could build the iPhone.

Generations are won by reaching people who don't know you exist — Module 5 — the customer journey and marketing.

05

The Customer Journey and Marketing

meet them where they areword of mouthpress release firstcrossing the chasm

Builders live inside their product's world — personas, use cases, their own context — and they forget that the customer lives in a completely different world where the product doesn't exist yet. "Technology is in service of the customer," Fadell says, "not we're going to jam the technology down the customer's throat." A customer only sees what they see through the lens of marketing and sales — so marketing is not the layer on top of the product; it's the doorway through which the product is seen. You build personas (single mom with kids, DINKs, seniors) and live inside them — and then you remember those people aren't aware of you, so your website, your ads, your owned and earned media must put the product into their context and make the words sing to them. When they hear the right word, they think, "Oh, they get me" — before they've ever bought anything. The best conversion is word of mouth from trusted early adopters, which is why you tailor your message to each adoption segment: early adopters first, then later adopters, then laggards — "crossing the chasm" with a different language for each. The Europe story is the cautionary tale: Apple ran its mature US marketing in a new region where adoption starts slower, and it didn't resonate with anyone. And the discipline that ties it together is the press release written before the project starts: you only get three or four key features before it becomes gobbledygook, so those three or four features define the product — and you fight to keep them. "We cut two of our three tentpole features — how are we going to sell that anymore?" OpenAI and Netscape are the modern caution: a spectacular demo with no answer to "what do I use it for every day?" Marketing isn't an afterthought — it's a design input.

Step by step: building the journey, not just the product

  1. Live in the personas. Know who the customer is in their world — and that you're not in it yet.
  2. Write the press release first. Three or four key features, max. If you can't say it in three or four, you don't know what the product is.
  3. Speak each segment's language. Early adopters first, then later adopters, then laggards — different words for each rung of the chasm.
  4. Seed word of mouth. Win the trusted early adopters others listen to; their "I should take notice of that" is the conversion engine.
  5. Rethink marketing per market. The Europe story: same messages, different adoption curve, zero resonance. Meet them where they are — every time.

obvious exampleiPod in Europe

Fourth-gen iPod, US marketing, European launch: fell flat with everyone. Europe adopts from the inside out, not coast-inward like the US. New region = new journey = new marketing.

counter-intuitive example"1,000 songs in your pocket"

Marketing lived separately from engineering and design — Steve was the hub. The team heard the tagline and thought, "that's genius." The why, in five words, decided before the product shipped.

edge-case exampleOpenAI / Netscape

Amazing demos, viral moments — and "what do I use it for every day?" The tool to get onto the internet, with nothing to do on it yet. When you don't think about marketing, you don't think about the product.

Visual analogy — the front door

You don't sell a house by mailing people the floor plan and hoping. You meet them at the door, walk them through the rooms in the order that makes sense to them, and let them picture living there. The customer's only lens is the marketing that leads them in — everything after that is confirmation.

❌ What people get wrong

"Build the perfect product and it will sell itself." No — the customer can't see your product from inside it. And "marketing is the layer on top" is backwards: marketing constrains the product, because a press release only holds three or four features. Cut two tentpole features and you've cut the story you were going to sell.

Why this matters

The product is judged entirely through the lens of marketing and sales — so if something isn't working, the gap may be the story, not the artifact. Thinking about marketing early is thinking about the product honestly.

The lens of marketing is a story — Module 6 — storytelling: the why, not the what.

06

Storytelling — The Why, Not the What

why vs. whathone it dailytruth in marketing

Stories are how humans have always passed information down and gotten people to commit to things — we go to movies for stories, we read books for stories, we were told stories as kids and watched the same ones a hundred times. So why would buying a product be different? Too often, Fadell says, when we're technology-led we talk about the what, and the what only speaks to another geek. The why is where the storytelling lives — the journey that meets your expectations or exceeds them. Steve Jobs honed the iPhone story every day for two and a half years: he didn't hand it to marketing, he knew the key features cold, he rehearsed the pitch on smart friends who weren't inside the project, and he refined it over and over. "When you saw him come on stage, it was just because he had done it 100,000 times." The same for Nest: Fadell told the thermostat story hundreds of times before launch, setting up the virus of doubt — "do you know how much you spend on your energy bill every year?" — before offering the alternative. And the ground rule is truth: Steve always said the best marketing tells the truth. Fadell's father, a salesman, would sometimes steer a customer to a competitor because the relationship and the truth mattered more than the sale. The infomercial is the caricature that teaches the technique: it dramatizes the pain (knuckles bleeding on the old cheese grater), shows the easy payoff, makes buying and returning effortless — all overstated and theatrical. Study the psychological and emotional techniques, then dial them back and apply them with truth. That's where "things start to go pop."

Step by step: getting better at storytelling

  1. Find the why. The journey you're taking the customer on, and why it matters to them — not the feature list.
  2. Hone it daily. Rehearse on smart people outside the project; refine with their reactions. Steve did it 100,000 times.
  3. Set the virus of doubt. Surface the pain your customer has habituated away — then offer the alternative.
  4. Study the infomercial, dial back the hype. Use its psychological techniques — with truth, not exaggeration.
  5. Let the story meet expectations. A story that overpromises collapses at first use; the truth holds.

obvious exampleSteve's daily rehearsal

Two and a half years of honing the iPhone story — key features micromanaged, pitched to unwashed smart friends, refined, refined. The stage ease was repetition, not magic.

counter-intuitive exampleHis dad's salesmanship

Sometimes Fadell's father convinced customers not to buy — even sending them to a competitor. Building relationship and truth outlasts any single sale, and it's still marketing.

edge-case exampleThe infomercial toolkit

Half-hour cheese-grater ads: exaggerated pain, easy payoff, easy buy, easy return — theatrical and technically fake. The techniques are real; the application is "with truth," dialed back.

Visual analogy — the teacher who made you love the subject

Your best professor didn't teach you formulas first — they taught you why math or physics was beautiful, and took you on a journey of why it mattered. "Then you're like, oh, now I get it." Storytelling in a product is the same: meaning first, features second.

❌ What people get wrong

"Storytelling is fluff on top of the product." Wrong: perfume on a bad product collapses the moment reality doesn't meet the story. The stories that work sing "from the depths of the product" — like the Nest Protect's gentle warning — which is why craft and story are the same discipline.

Why this matters

People don't buy spec sheets; they buy journeys that meet or beat expectations. The why makes a product accessible and relatable — and it's the same muscle that makes great marketing, great sales, and great products.

Stories are told by people — and in the AI era, keeping people in the loop is the craft itself: Module 7 — humans in the loop.

07

Humans in the Loop in the AI Era

no cognitive surrendertechnical debtluxury software

It's never been easier to build: type a prompt and a product spits out. And that's exactly the trap. "You're building on a really crusty foundation — short-term gain for very, very long-term loss." Fadell's evidence is the leaked Claude source code: reportedly 90–100% AI-written, and real architects recoiled — the main loop was brittle, unreadable, unmaintainable, and nobody could say whether it was secure or how you'd roll it back. Software needs layers and a mixture of experts — architects, optimizers, general coders, security reviewers — so that subsequent generations can build on something sound instead of a mass of unknowns. That's technical debt, and everybody hates it: you might be fixing something while creating more of it. The same logic maps straight onto product management: if you prompt out a result without a real marketer, a real salesperson, a real channel, a real architect, a real manufacturing manager in the loop, version one might work — but what about version five or six? AI is brilliant for prototypes — do more prototypes, they feed the informed gut — and for tightly scoped sub-segments inside an architecture you define and lock in. But an opinion-based 1.0 of something genuinely new has no model for it; there's nothing in the training data. "The things that stand out are the things that are really well thought through." That's the H&M versus luxury-brand distinction: fast fashion washes out in a season; crafted software lasts. Could you vibe-code Flighty v2? Probably — but Flighty itself, the architecture and the pixels, is luxury software. "We can use the machines, but don't cognitively surrender."

Step by step: using AI without surrendering to it

  1. Prototype with AI, freely. More prototypes feed the informed gut — that's where AI shines.
  2. Architect it yourself, then lock it in. Define the layers and structure; have the AI work inside scoped sub-segments you control.
  3. Keep the mixture of experts. Architect, optimizer, coder, security reviewer — each with a defined point of view on the result.
  4. Ask the questions prototypes can't answer. Is it secure? Maintainable? Can you roll it back? Will version five build on it?
  5. Make the opinion-based 1.0 calls yourself. For something genuinely new, there's no model for it — that's still the human's job.

obvious exampleThe leaked Claude code

AI-written at scale, and engineers called it brittle and unreadable — the main loop, of all things. It ran; nobody could safely own, secure, or roll it back.

counter-intuitive exampleFlighty is luxury software

You could probably vibe-code a Flighty v2. But Flighty itself — the architecture, the pixels, the care — is the version-one thing that has no model for it. Craft is the moat.

edge-case exampleH&M vs. the luxury brand

Fast fashion looks right for a season, then washes out; crafted goods last. Fast software is the same — "if you're going to build a real company, it can't be throwaway."

Visual analogy — the craftsman's workshop

Power tools don't make a cabinetmaker; they make a fast carpenter. The cabinetmaker decides the joinery, the wood, the proportions, and uses the tool for the cut. AI is the power tool — brilliant for speed, useless without the person who knows what's worth building and how it should be built.

❌ What people get wrong

"The AI will get smarter and fix the debt later." Not proven — what's proven is that scoped, architected use compounds. And "the AI should just figure out the product" is giving up the functions that make a product sing: marketing, sales, support, manufacturing. Abstractions don't remove the roles; they remove your awareness of them.

Why this matters

As building gets easier, the things that stand out are the things that are well thought through. Technical debt compounds across generations; luxury software compounds into companies. Don't trade the long-term company for the short-term demo.

The same judgment applies to the next device itself — Module 8 — the next device, and ethics.

08

The Next Device — Voice, Displays, and Ethics

voice-firstdisplay staysfull stackethics

What does the next iPhone look like with AI? Fadell separates "soon" from "long term." Soon: a smartphone, because the all-app interface isn't going away and we don't trust the alternative yet. Long term, barring brain-computer interfaces or retina lasers, you still need a display — the best way to visualize information is a display, and you can't glance at a map with your eyes closed. "Her" had glass, he points out. The real change is the input hierarchy: today it's tap-and-swipe primary, keyboard secondary, voice tertiary — and Fadell wants to flip it exactly: voice primary, keyboard if necessary, tap-and-swipe last. It never happened before because voice was always bolted on as a gizmo — cars added tactile buttons, then touchscreens, then voice, and nobody uses it. When voice carries real intelligence and memory — not Siri 1.0 — it can finally lead, with the other inputs as crutches while trust builds across generations. Humane's projection? "Different, not better" — you still need something to project on. Meanwhile the hardware cycle has swung back: everyone's building atoms-plus-software again, because you can't reach the next level of software without the next level of hardware — and full-stack products (Nest had to innovate software, hardware, and network; Waymo is a hardware platform) have staying power. AI also needs context: sensors in the home, audio not voice, for an anywhere assistant — the vision Nest was too early for, and why the orphaned Nest Protect, discontinued as a "stepchild," still pains him. And ethics: don't addict your users; digital food needs nutrition labels just like physical food; the iPhone's unintended consequence was social media — Apple isn't a social media company, but the platform distributes it. Steve killed the idea of porn in the iTunes store: "Is that the kind of world you want your kids to grow up in?" Principles are part of product design — users feel them, and they reward them.

Step by step: designing the next device

  1. Keep the display. Unless it's jacked into your brain or your retina, you need a screen to look at a map. The slab stays; it folds and rolls, but it stays.
  2. Flip the input hierarchy. Voice first (once it has memory and intelligence), keyboard second, tap-and-swipe third — with the old inputs kept as crutches while trust builds.
  3. Build the full stack. Atoms plus software: sensors, hardware, network, and app together. Full-stack products have staying power.
  4. Design for context and privacy. AI needs context — audio, not voice, sensors placed so they don't invade privacy. That was Nest's early vision, and it's ready now.
  5. Hold principles. Don't addict users. Add the digital nutrition labels. Ask Steve's question: "Is that the kind of world you want your kids to grow up in?"

obvious exampleVoice in cars

Tactile buttons, then touchscreens, then voice bolted on last — so nobody uses it. Voice was a gizmo without intelligence behind it. The lesson: input order follows trust, not novelty.

counter-intuitive exampleHumane's projection

Projecting onto your hand feels like Star Wars — but you still need a surface to project on. "It's different, not better." You can't escape the display; you can only move it.

edge-case exampleThe orphaned Nest Protect

Best in category for a decade, then discontinued because nobody inside Google would pour love into a stepchild. If it had been invested in, it would have been a centerpiece of the AI-in-the-home story.

Visual analogy — the refrigerator

You can stock a fridge with junk food or good food — and even good food gets eaten every five seconds if you keep opening the door. Physical food got nutrition labels and regulation; digital food has neither. The device is the fridge: the design isn't just the appliance, it's the habits and guardrails around it.

❌ What people get wrong

"The screen is going away." Only if you're jacked into your brain or your eye — otherwise you still need a display to look at a map. And "ethics are a business cost": no — "if you make your customers unhealthy, you're not going to have customers." The short-term game with no customers is the actual loss.

Why this matters

The next device will be judged on trust, context, and principles — not just pixels. The companies that win will be the ones that treat ethics as product design, keep humans in the loop, and have the patience for the three generations it takes to earn adoption.

And that brings us back to where we started — every next device begins with a pain worth solving: Module 1.

Frequently Asked Questions

Isn't data always better than opinion?

For an established market, yes — that's the right tool. But a 1.0 of a new category has no analog data, so "data-driven" means copying something else or collecting junk data. The skill is knowing which mode you're in. Fadell: "If most of your decisions are opinion-based for a 1.0, you have to have a very small set of people charged with making them."

How can micromanaging ever be a good thing?

Because it's aimed at the right target. Micromanage the decision and the data behind it — not people's operations. Early in his career Fadell micromanaged everything and drove everyone nuts; the correction was identifying the few key things that matter (customer-visible details, cost, long-term vision) and delegating the rest.

If version 2 still isn't working, when do you actually quit?

When the idea itself is fundamentally broken — Fadell's phrase is "severely brain-damaged." Otherwise you're on the three-generation curve: make it, fix it, fix the business. The iPod was bought only by Mac loyalists for two generations before Windows and the iTunes Music Store made it take off.

Will AI replace product managers?

Fadell's answer is no — but it will change the job. Product management sits between marketing, sales, engineering, distribution, and support, stitching them together; if you prompt out a result without those points of view in the loop, version one works and version five collapses. Use AI for prototypes and scoped work; keep the mixture of experts.

Do we really still need a screen in the AI future?

Yes — unless you have a brain-computer interface or a retina laser, you need a display to look at a map. The movie Her had glass, too. The change isn't the display; it's the input hierarchy flipping to voice-first as voice earns trust.

Next Steps

  2. Write your press release first. Draft a one-page press release for your current project and cut it to three or four key features. Whatever you cut tells you what your product actually is.
  3. Run a taste audit on your next decision. For your next big call, list the data you actually have, name who owns the opinion if the data is ambiguous, and decide who takes the heat if it's wrong.
