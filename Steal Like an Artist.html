MODULE 01 Steal Like an Artist

Nothing is original — and that's the good news.

A · Core Explanation

Austin Kleon's central claim is that nothing is original. This isn't cynicism — it's liberation. The idea itself is ancient: Ecclesiastes says there's nothing new under the sun, and Kleon points out that line was borrowed from Egyptian writers 2,000 years earlier. A 4,000-year-old Egyptian poet complained that "all the good words had been used." So the anxiety about originality is itself unoriginal.

Once you accept that, your job changes. You stop trying to conjure great ideas from your own skull and instead extend your "tentacles" into the world, grab material, and weld it into something new. Every creator belongs to a lineage. When Kleon discovered his newspaper blackout poems resembled earlier work, he didn't despair — he swam upstream to find his creative ancestors, building a family tree that gave his work roots and DNA. Brian Eno's term scenius captures this: creative work is collective, not the product of lone genius. You collaborate with the past, the present that receives you, and even the future — because a book does nothing until a reader opens it and activates it.

The "steal" metaphor isn't about plagiarism. It's about a jewel thief's attention — assuming every person and moment holds a nugget worth picking up. That posture makes you pay rich, specific attention to the world, which is exactly what creative people need to learn.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

Think like a jewel thief, not a forger. A forger copies one painting exactly. A jewel thief cases every room, pockets the small glittering bits from many places, and later welds them into something no single source could have made. The point isn't to pass off someone else's work — it's to keep your eyes open everywhere.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Alien is a haunted house in space. A screenwriting teacher's example: most successful movies take an existing idea and drop it into a new context. "It's Jaws in space." The transformation, not the invention, is the work.

Counter-intuitive

Scenius beats genius. We lionize the lone genius, but Brian Eno's word scenius names the truth: whole scenes — not individuals — produce the work. The Beatles needed Liverpool; punk needed the Sex Pistols and everyone in the room.

Edge case

The 4,000-year-old complainer. An Egyptian poet grumbled that all the good words had been used. Even the feeling that "everything's been done" is ancient, borrowed, and unoriginal. That should relax you.

E · Common Misconception
"Stealing is bad — I should aim for a completely original idea."
Kleon's theft is about attention and transformation, not copying. You steal from many sources, blend them, and make the result yours. The alternative — waiting for a wholly original idea — is the thing that actually stops people from making work. T.S. Eliot put it bluntly: immature poets imitate, mature poets steal.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If nothing is original, the pressure to be a genius evaporates — and the real work becomes collectable, learnable, and collaborative. You can start today by studying your influences instead of waiting to be struck by lightning.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You feel like a fraud because your style resembles someone else's.

Decision 1: Hide your influences and pretend it's all yours.

→ Consequence: You feel isolated and blocked; your work has no roots.

Decision 2: Swim upstream — research who influenced your influence, and their influences.

→ Consequence: You find a lineage, get ideas for what they didn't do, and feel part of a community.

Decision 3: Ask "What would happen if I put two of my heroes in a room together?"

→ Consequence: A third path opens that neither hero could have taken alone.

MODULE 02 Verbs Over Nouns

Forget the label you want to be. Focus on what you actually do.

A · Core Explanation

Kleon argues that nouns are more dangerous than verbs. People say "I want to be a writer" — but they usually mean the noun: the identity, the imagined status. They don't mean the verb: writing. The noun creates a high mental bar ("am I a real artist yet?"), while the verb creates a low one ("can I draw something today?").

You don't have to be a capital-A Artist to pick up a pen and sketch. Kleon calls himself "a writer who draws" — a verb-based identity, not a job title. Job titles, he says, are for other people; they box you in. If you're only a novelist, what happens when you have a great screenplay idea? If you're only a stand-up comedian, what happens when you want to make a podcast? The verbs you love — drawing, writing, telling jokes, making — are what actually activate your creative mind.

The lower internal bar matters enormously. "What would it mean to write something today?" is far more actionable than "what would it mean to be a writer?" Kleon's forthcoming book, Don't Call It Art, draws on John Baldessari's observation that kids don't call it art — they just make stuff. If you can forget the noun "art" and focus on the process — the simple pleasure of scribbling — you unlock a freer, more generative practice. The verb is where the work lives.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

A pedestal vs. stepping stones. The noun is a marble pedestal — you have to climb onto it, and everyone can see if you fall off. The verb is a set of flat stones across a stream: you just step. One requires permission; the other requires only a foot.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

"Be a writer" vs. "write 200 words." One is a distant identity; the other is a fifteen-minute action. The action is the thing that actually produces writing.

Counter-intuitive

"A writer who draws." Kleon admits he's a mediocre writer and a mediocre artist — but combining the two verbs makes him good. Two modest verbs beat one grand noun.

Edge case

The caged novelist. A novelist with a brilliant screenplay idea who won't act on it because "I'm a novelist" — the noun became a prison cell with the door wide open.

E · Common Misconception
"I can't start until I'm officially an artist / writer / musician."
There is no official body that grants artist status. Kids don't wait to be called artists before they draw — they just draw. The noun never arrives as a certificate; it's a retroactive label other people apply. The verb is available immediately.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Nouns are for other people; verbs are for you. Centering the verb lowers the bar to today, keeps you cross-disciplinary, and prevents a job title from becoming a cage around your curiosity.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You keep saying "I want to be a writer" but never write.

Decision 1: Buy a nicer notebook and research MFA programs.

→ Consequence: The noun gets fed; the verb stays starving.

Decision 2: Reframe: "Today I will write one bad paragraph."

→ Consequence: The verb gets fed; a paragraph exists that didn't before.

Decision 3: Follow the verb wherever it leads, even outside the noun.

→ Consequence: You might write a screenplay, a song, or a newsletter — and stop worrying about the label.

MODULE 03 Process & Imperfection

The work isn't expressing a pre-formed idea — it's how you discover what you think.

A · Core Explanation

A widespread misconception is that creative work happens in two steps: have an idea, then express it. Kleon flips this. Writing isn't expressing a known thought — it's figuring out what you think. You don't know it until you see it on the page. That means the process, not a perfect product, is the point. "Going through the motions" is an underrated phrase: showing up and moving your hands is how creative people actually work.

The second half is embracing imperfection. Kleon admits he still fights the urge to make something "perfect" — a book, a joke, a post everyone would call flawless. But the work he feels most connected to is obviously broken and then repaired. Imperfection lets the audience in; perfection is a closed door. Japanese aesthetics — wabi-sabi, and kintsugi (repairing broken pottery with golden glue, highlighting rather than hiding the seams) — model this.

Punk rock did it for Kleon: seeing the Sex Pistols be "terrible" made him think, "I want to do that." Improv comedy turns mistakes into gifts — you pounce on the stumble and make it look intentional. You want to mess up just enough to make things interesting. The goal isn't flawlessness; it's aliveness.

B · Diagram (Hotspot — click the dots) C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

Kintsugi. When a pot breaks, you don't throw it away and you don't glue it invisibly. You fill the cracks with gold and display the seams. The break becomes part of the object's history and beauty. Your rough drafts, failed jokes, and broken attempts are the golden seams of your work — not evidence against it.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Kintsugi pottery. A broken bowl is repaired with golden lacquer; the repair is celebrated, not concealed.

Counter-intuitive

Punk rock. Bernard Sumner saw the Sex Pistols and thought, "They were terrible — I wanted to get on stage and be terrible with them." Imperfection inspired action, not despair.

Edge case

Improv comedy. A good scene needs a stumble — someone says something weird so the others can pounce and make it look intentional. The mistake is the gift.

E · Common Misconception
"I need a perfect idea before I can start, and the result should be flawless."
Two errors in one. First, the idea often arrives through the work, not before it — writing is how you discover what you think. Second, perfection is a closed door; it gives the audience nothing to enter into. Slightly-off work invites a conversation. Kleon's most meaningful work is the obviously broken stuff he found a way to repair.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If you wait for a perfect idea and a flawless execution, you'll never ship. If you show up, go through the motions, and let mistakes become material, you'll make more — and people will connect more deeply with the honest, cracked version anyway.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: Your draft feels broken and you want to delete everything.

Decision 1: Delete it and wait for a better idea.

→ Consequence: Nothing is learned; the "better idea" may never come.

Decision 2: Ask "What is this broken part trying to become?"

→ Consequence: The flaw becomes a feature; the seam gets gilded.

Decision 3: Share the imperfect version and invite response.

→ Consequence: The audience enters through the crack; connection happens.

MODULE 04 Everything Is Connected

Don't separate family, life, and work. They feed each other.

A · Core Explanation

Kleon rejects "balance" and "separation" entirely. He sees everything as one big stew pot. Family isn't a distraction from creativity — it's a source. Cyril Connolly called the pram in the hall the enemy of art, but Kleon studied artist-mothers instead, finding a higher bar and a more fruitful model. His kids became his teachers: a four-year-old, having just acquired language, sees the world with fresh eyes — "they're poets, ecstatic poets." He scribbled down their babble.

Walt Whitman does this in Leaves of Grass: he looks at grass and talks to a child. The poet's job is to pay close attention to the ordinary, and "ordinary plus extra attention equals the extraordinary." Parenting reframes mistakes and messes too. When his son Jules drew skeletons in permanent marker on the outdoor couch cushions, a friend suggested embroidering over them — and Kleon's wife did.

This illustrates a deeper truth: we often think we care for things because we love them, but it's just as true that we love them because we care for them. Putting in the work builds the relationship. Rob Walker's "care for something" assignment proves it: care for a plant for a week and suddenly you love plants. So if you're uninspired, show up and care — connection comes from contact, not the reverse.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

The stew pot. You don't sort your life into labeled Tupperware — "work," "family," "art" — and eat from one at a time. You throw everything into one pot and let it simmer. The carrot tastes like the broth; the broth tastes like the carrot. Your parenting makes your art, and your art makes your parenting.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Scribbling your kid's babble. A four-year-old's fresh language is poetry; Kleon wrote it down. Parenting directly fed the work.

Counter-intuitive

The couch cushions. Permanent-marker "vandalism" became an embroidered collaboration between son and mother — the mess became the masterpiece.

Edge case

Rob Walker's plant. A student assigned to "care for something" for a week ended up loving plants. The caring created the love — not the other way around.

E · Common Misconception
"Family and life get in the way of art — I need to protect my creative time from them."
The "pram in the hall" idea says the stroller is the enemy of art. But Kleon studied artist-mothers and found the opposite: the constraints and fresh eyes of family life generated more, not less. Treating life as the enemy of work starves the work of exactly the material — ordinary things seen closely — that makes it alive.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If connection comes from contact, then showing up and caring — for a plant, a child, a project — is the actual engine of love and inspiration. You don't need to feel inspired first. You need to make contact first; the feeling follows.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You feel creatively empty and think you need "me time" away from your family.

Decision 1: Book a solo retreat to "find inspiration."

→ Consequence: You may relax, but you bring the same noticing habits back with you.

Decision 2: Spend an afternoon with a four-year-old and write down what they say.

→ Consequence: Fresh language and fresh eyes refill the tank in an hour.

Decision 3: Take on a small act of care (a plant, a repair, a routine) and stay with it for a week.

→ Consequence: Care builds love; love builds material. The work comes back on its own.

MODULE 05 The Proper Tension

Creative energy lives in the tension between opposites — not in eliminating it.

A · Core Explanation

Kleon calls himself both "deeply lazy" and "intensely disciplined." Left alone, he'd do nothing; yet he makes commitments in time (Robert Fripp's definition of discipline) and shows up. Neither trait alone would produce his work — the work arises from the tension between them. He generalizes: creative energy is found in the proper tension between opposites.

He also feels the pull between words and pictures. He's a mediocre writer and a mediocre artist, but together they make him "pretty good." A guitar string illustrates it: too slack, it buzzes; too tight, it snaps; music comes from the right tension. So people who try to "minimize" tension in their creative lives are missing the point — you need to find the tension that makes your work sing.

There's also the tension between structure and spontaneity. Kleon schedules the work ("I know I'm going to sit down") but not the outcome ("I don't know what's going to happen"). That "what's going to happen" magic is what creative people must get hooked on. He notes research suggesting most people max out at three to four hours of truly intense creative work per day. And stepping away is itself a tool: let a problem "cook on the back burner," go ride a bike or play, and the unconscious keeps working. Time — sleep, a 15-minute break — is an underused collaborator. You go away so you can come back, seeing the problem through a different lens.

B · Diagram (Interactive — move the slider) C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

The guitar string. A string so loose it buzzes makes no music; a string cranked too tight snaps. Music happens only at the right tension between two poles. Your laziness and discipline, your words and pictures, your structure and spontaneity — these are the two poles. Don't cut the string. Tune it.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

A guitar string. The physical, audible proof: too slack buzzes, too tight snaps, the right tension produces a note.

Counter-intuitive

Deep laziness + intense discipline. Kleon says he's both, and that eliminating either would ruin the work. The laziness is not the enemy of the discipline — it's the other pole.

Edge case

A scheduled newsletter with unknown content. He knows he'll sit down (structure) but not what he'll write (spontaneity). That gap is the magic he gets hooked on.

E · Common Misconception
"I should eliminate tension and stress to be more creative."
Minimizing tension gives you a slack string — no music. The goal is not zero tension but proper tension: enough pull between opposing forces that something resonates. Identify your opposites and tune between them. Also, don't confuse "stepping away" with "giving up" — a 15-minute break or a night's sleep is the unconscious continuing to work.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If you treat every internal contradiction as a problem to resolve, you'll flatten your work. If you treat it as fuel, you'll find a voice that neither side could produce alone. And respecting your daily limit (3–4 hours of intense creative work) keeps the string from snapping.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You're stuck on a creative problem and have been grinding for hours.

Decision 1: Keep drilling through the concrete wall.

→ Consequence: Diminishing returns; the string risks snapping.

Decision 2: Walk away — bike ride, pool, play with a kid.

→ Consequence: The unconscious keeps working; you return with a new lens.

Decision 3: Name your two opposing pulls and ask which one this project needs more of right now.

→ Consequence: You tune the string instead of cutting it — and the work starts to sing.

MODULE 06 Art Saves Lives, Not the World

Don't let a dire world stop you from making and sharing. Reach one person.

A · Core Explanation

Duffy names the real barrier: it feels self-indulgent to post about a comedy show or a podcast when the world desperately needs improving. Kleon's answer is clarifying. Art doesn't save the world. As Kurt Vonnegut said, throwing art at the world is like attacking a knight in armor with a hot fudge sundae. But art saves lives — individual people — and if you do that enough, you save the world in tiny ways.

So his unit of measure is one reader. If he can make one reader's day a little better, that's enough. And being a person who is curious, pays attention, and spends time making things is itself a contribution — because those qualities are rapidly leaving our culture. Modeling them makes a dent. Kleon thinks about his 15-year-old self in a cornfield and how music, comedy, and movies activated a spirit in him that changed how he saw the world. Art changes people, and that slowly changes everything.

He's not an activist, but he can "show people what it's like to think and pay attention and have a spine." If everyone showed up and did what they know how to do, things would improve immediately. What was worth doing six months ago is worth doing now. On the fear of humiliation: you need a private woodshed — a safe place to be bad, like Chris Rock working out material in a phone-free club. Hobbies and side projects give you safe failure. Ignorance, Orson Welles said of Citizen Kane, made it possible — he didn't know what wasn't possible. When you're starting out, you have nothing to lose. Keep a diary where you can whine and be awful. Beginner's mind is an advantage, not a liability.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

A stone dropped in a pond. You don't try to move the whole pond. You drop one stone — one reader, one show, one post — and the ripples travel outward on their own. The world is moved by accumulated ripples, not by one heroic wave.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Measuring by one reader. Kleon's success metric is whether he made one person's day a little better. That's the whole target.

Counter-intuitive

The hot fudge sundae vs. the armored knight. Vonnegut's image says art looks laughably useless against the world's problems — yet it still saves individual lives, which is the only door the world has.

Edge case

Chris Rock's phone-free club. A famous comedian still works out material in private where it can be bad. The woodshed is not optional; it's where the public work is forged.

E · Common Misconception
"It's selfish to make and share art when the world is in crisis."
This framing assumes the only valuable response is direct activism, and that art must justify itself by solving the crisis. But art's job is to save individual lives — to make one person's day better — and modeling curiosity, attention, and a spine is itself a cultural contribution. If it was worth doing six months ago, it's worth doing now. Keep the private woodshed for the bad drafts; share the ripples.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If you wait for the world to be fixed before you make and share, you'll never start. Starting small — one reader, one project, one private practice — is not a compromise; it's the only mechanism by which art actually changes anything. And the woodshed protects you while you learn.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You want to share your work but feel it's trivial next to the news.

Decision 1: Wait until the world calms down and the work feels important enough.

→ Consequence: The work never ships; the ripples never start.

Decision 2: Reframe success as reaching one person, and share it today.

→ Consequence: One reader's day improves; the work begins to travel.

Decision 3: Keep a private woodshed (a diary, a side project) where failure is safe.

→ Consequence: You can be bad in private, so you can be good in public — eventually.

Still Confused? FAQ

Isn't "steal like an artist" just a fancy way of saying "plagiarize"?

No — and the distinction is the whole point. Plagiarism is passing off one source as your own. Kleon's "stealing" is closer to what a jewel thief does: you take small glittering bits from many places, then weld them into something no single source could have produced. You steal from many, you transform, and you credit your lineage. The metaphor is really about attention — assuming every person and moment holds a nugget worth picking up. That posture makes you notice more, and noticing is the raw material of creative work. If you only copy one source, you're a forger. If you collect from many and transform, you're an artist.

If nothing is original, why bother making anything?

Because "original" was never the point. The value of a creative work isn't that it contains no borrowed DNA — it's that a particular person, at a particular moment, blended the borrowed material in a way only they could, and then a particular reader or listener activated it. T.S. Eliot said mature poets steal; the transformation is the contribution. If you wait for a wholly original idea, you'll wait forever — and the 4,000-year-old Egyptian poet who complained all the good words were used is proof that this anxiety is ancient and unproductive. Make the thing anyway. It will be new because you made it.

I really do want to "be a writer." Is that wrong?

It's not wrong — it's just less useful than it sounds. The noun describes an identity other people apply to you after the fact; there's no certificate and no ceremony. The verb is available immediately: you can write a bad paragraph today. Kleon's point is that the noun raises the internal bar ("am I a real writer yet?") while the verb lowers it ("can I write one sentence?"). You can keep the noun as a horizon if you like — just don't let it replace the daily verb. Most people who "want to be writers" don't actually want to write; they want to have written. The verb reveals which one you are.

Doesn't embracing imperfection just mean making lazy, sloppy work?

No. Kintsugi isn't "leave the pot broken" — it's an exacting craft of repair that makes the object more valuable. Punk and improv aren't about being bad; they're about not letting the fear of badness stop you from starting, and about treating the inevitable stumble as material. The point is that perfection is a closed door: a flawless thing gives the audience nothing to enter into, while a slightly-off thing invites a conversation. Kleon says his most meaningful work is the obviously broken stuff he found a way to repair. You still work hard — you just stop pretending the work has to be seamless to be worth making.

How do I find "the proper tension" if I don't know my opposites?

Look for the two pulls you already feel and have probably been treating as a problem. For Kleon: laziness vs. discipline, words vs. pictures, structure vs. spontaneity. Yours might be "I love performing but hate being seen," or "I want to be prolific but I'm a perfectionist," or "I want a quiet life but I want a big audience." Name both poles honestly. Then instead of trying to eliminate one, ask which one this particular project needs more of right now — and tune between them. A slack string buzzes; a tight string snaps; the note lives in between. Also remember the research point: most people have only 3–4 hours of intense creative work in a day, so the string has a natural tuning range.

If art doesn't save the world, is it okay to spend my life on it?

Kleon's answer is yes — because art saves lives, one at a time, and that's how the world actually changes. Vonnegut's image (attacking an armored knight with a hot fudge sundae) is honest about art's limits against large-scale problems, but the individual saving is real: a song that gets a teenager through a hard year, a joke that makes someone's day bearable. Kleon measures success by one reader. Beyond the work itself, being a person who is curious, pays attention, and has a spine is itself a cultural contribution, because those qualities are vanishing. If everyone showed up and did what they know how to do, things would improve immediately. That's not a consolation prize — it's the mechanism.
