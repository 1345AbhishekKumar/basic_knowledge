MODULE 01 Why Everyone Seems Like an Idiot

The problem isn't their intelligence. It's your decoder and theirs.

A · Core Explanation

Thomas Erikson opens his book with a confession. He found himself in a stretch of life where he felt surrounded by idiots. People misread his words, took offence at things he hadn't meant as insults, and seemed unable to grasp a point he considered obvious. His first instinct was to assume bad luck, or bad colleagues, or a general decline in human competence. Eventually he asked a better question: what if the problem is a mismatch, not a deficiency?

That question changed everything. What Erikson discovered is that most communication failures have nothing to do with intelligence. They come from differences in how people process information and express themselves. Two people can hear the exact same sentence and receive two completely different messages — one hears a straightforward request, the other hears an attack. Neither is lying, and neither is stupid. They're running different decoding software.

Erikson also argues that behaviour is not random. It's shaped by genetics, upbringing, life experience and culture — but underneath all that variation, there are patterns. Some people are naturally assertive and direct; others are naturally accommodating and indirect. Some are detail-obsessed; others genuinely cannot see the details. These patterns repeat reliably enough that they can be sorted into categories. That's the whole premise of the book: not that people are simple, but that people are predictably different in specific, learnable ways.

Once you accept that, the emotional charge drains out of a lot of daily frustration. The colleague who steamrollered your idea wasn't being malicious — she was moving fast because that's her default. The one who "never has an opinion" wasn't being useless — he was waiting for a safe moment that never came. The work is not to fix them. It's to learn their language. That's the promise of everything that follows.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

Two radios, one broadcast. A station transmits on FM. One radio is tuned to FM and plays the song perfectly. The other is tuned to AM and produces nothing but static. The static isn't evidence that the broadcast is bad, or that the second radio is broken — it's evidence that they're on different frequencies. Most of what we call "difficult people" is just this: a good signal received on the wrong band.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

The blunt email. A manager writes a three-line email with no greeting. Half the team reads it as efficient; the other half reads it as furious. Nothing in the text changed — only the receiver.

Counter-intuitive

The "difficult" person is often the most predictable one. The colleague who always pushes back in meetings isn't erratic — she's consistent. Once you know her pattern, she becomes the easiest person on the team to work with, because you can prepare for her exactly.

Edge case

Two people who both think the other is the problem. The fast, direct person thinks the slow, careful one is obstructive. The slow, careful one thinks the fast one is reckless. Both are describing the same behaviour from opposite sides of the same mismatch.

E · Common Misconception
"If people just tried harder / were smarter, they'd understand me."
This assumes understanding is a matter of effort or intelligence. Erikson's whole point is that it's a matter of style. A highly intelligent person can completely miss your meaning if your delivery style doesn't match how they process. Trying harder on your end, without adapting your style, usually just means repeating the same message louder. The fix is not more effort — it's a different frequency.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: If the problem is other people's stupidity, you are powerless — you can't fix everyone. If the problem is a decoding mismatch, you have leverage: you can learn the code. This single reframe converts a permanent complaint into a learnable skill, and it's the foundation the next five modules build on.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You keep clashing with a colleague and have started avoiding them.

Decision 1: Conclude they're unreasonable and route around them.

→ Consequence: The clash returns with the next person who shares their style. Nothing is learned.

Decision 2: Ask: "What does this person seem to want in a conversation — speed, warmth, safety, or accuracy?"

→ Consequence: You get a hypothesis you can test next time, instead of a verdict you can't act on.

Decision 3: Change one thing about your delivery — not your message — and observe.

→ Consequence: Small adjustments produce disproportionate improvements, and you now have a repeatable method.

MODULE 02 The DISA Model

Four colours, two axes, one compass. Your first map.

A · Core Explanation

The DISA model sorts behaviour into four patterns, each given a colour. D is Dominance (Red), I is Influence (Yellow), S is Steadiness (Green), and the fourth — Conscientiousness — is Blue. (You'll also see this family of models called DISC; the colour-and-letter scheme is the same idea.)

Erikson is careful about one thing: everyone is a mix. Nobody is a pure colour. Most people lean more heavily on one or two, and that lean shapes their default behaviour — how fast they talk, how much detail they want, how they react when things go wrong. The colours are not boxes to put people in. They're a vocabulary for describing tendencies you've already noticed but couldn't name.

The four types sit on two axes, which is what makes the model memorable rather than just a list. The first axis is task versus people: what gets your attention — the result, or the relationship? The second is fast versus careful: how do you move — quickly and outward, or slowly and inward? Cross those two axes and you get exactly four quadrants: Red (task + fast), Yellow (people + fast), Green (people + careful), Blue (task + careful).

The most important claim in this module is the one Erikson repeats throughout the book: no colour is inherently better or worse. Each has genuine strengths and real weaknesses, and each is essential to a functioning team. The goal is not to change who you are, or to convert anyone else. The goal is to recognise the pattern, understand the motivation underneath it, and adjust how you communicate so the message actually lands.

B · Diagram — tap the colour dots C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

A compass, not a box. A compass doesn't tell you that north is good and south is bad. It tells you which way you're facing, so you can navigate. The DISA colours work the same way: they're bearings, not verdicts. "My colleague is Blue" should feel like "my colleague is facing west" — useful, neutral, and actionable.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

The meeting with four people. One pushes for a decision (Red), one pitches a wild new idea (Yellow), one checks that everyone's comfortable (Green), one asks what the data says (Blue). A balanced team, and also a potential traffic jam.

Counter-intuitive

A strong Yellow can be a bad listener. The most socially skilled person in the room is often the one who talks over everyone. Warmth and dominance are not opposites — enthusiasm can drown out quieter voices just as effectively as aggression.

Edge case

The same person, two colours. A manager who is Red at work and Green at home. The colour isn't a soul — it's a pattern that responds to context, stakes, and who's watching.

E · Common Misconception
"This is just astrology with extra steps — I don't fit neatly into one colour."
You're not supposed to fit neatly into one colour. Erikson says explicitly that everyone is a mix, and that people change with context, stress, and life stage. The model isn't a claim about your essence; it's a claim about tendencies — and tendencies are testable. If you lean Red and someone gives you a long, detailed, emotionally warm explanation, notice whether you get impatient. That impatience is data. The value isn't the label; it's the prediction.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Without shared vocabulary, "she's difficult" is where the conversation ends. With the model, "she's Red and I'm Blue, so we're clashing on pace and detail" is where the conversation starts. Naming a pattern makes it adjustable.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You're about to run a project kickoff with a mixed team.

Decision 1: Run it the way you'd want it run.

→ Consequence: Roughly a quarter of the room is engaged; the rest quietly disengage.

Decision 2: Open with the outcome (for Reds), the vision (for Yellows), the reassurance (for Greens), and the data (for Blues).

→ Consequence: Everyone hears their own language in the first ten minutes and stays in the room.

Decision 3: Assign follow-ups that match strengths — Red leads, Yellow presents, Green coordinates, Blue verifies.

→ Consequence: Fewer bottlenecks, less friction, and nobody is being asked to do the thing they're worst at.

MODULE 03 Red & Yellow — The Fast Lane

Both move quickly. One is chasing the result; the other is chasing the room.

A · Core Explanation

Red (Dominance) is the colour of control. Reds are action-oriented, decisive and assertive. They thrive where they can take charge and make things happen, which is why they so often end up in leadership whether or not anyone appointed them. Their communication is direct and blunt: they cut to the chase, they don't sugarcoat, and they'd rather hear a hard truth in ten words than a soft one in a hundred. To a Red, results matter more than process or feelings. That is a genuine asset in a crisis and a genuine liability in a sensitive conversation.

The cost of Red is impatience. A Red who is pushing for a decision can overlook details, rush past objections, and alienate the people who needed thirty more seconds to think. They can read as domineering or insensitive — especially to Greens and Blues, who experience that pace as pressure rather than momentum. And they lose respect for people they perceive as hesitant, which is worth knowing if you're the hesitant one.

Yellow (Influence) is the colour of energy. Yellows are optimistic, charismatic, and full of ideas — the person who makes a room lighter and generates three new possibilities before anyone has finished the first one. They excel wherever imagination and enthusiasm carry the day: marketing, sales, the arts, anywhere the human connection is the product. They are natural networkers and natural motivators, and their optimism can restart a project that was losing momentum.

The cost of Yellow is follow-through. Excitement about a new idea is not the same as commitment to it, and Yellows can lose interest the moment the novelty fades. They can overlook details, miss deadlines, and — without meaning any harm — dominate conversations so thoroughly that quieter people never get to speak. Under stress, both Reds and Yellows get faster and louder, which is exactly why pairing them without a Green or Blue nearby tends to produce motion without completion.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

An arrow and a firework. A Red is an arrow: one direction, high speed, arrives. A Yellow is a firework: spectacular, illuminating, and gone in eight seconds — leaving a sky full of sparks, some of which are genuinely brilliant ideas and some of which nobody ever wrote down. Teams need both, but a team of only arrows and fireworks is a team with no one doing the follow-up.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

The Red who ends the meeting. Forty minutes of circular discussion, then: "OK — we do X by Friday. Anything else?" Everyone leaves with a decision. Some leave feeling run over.

Counter-intuitive

The Yellow's weakness is not laziness. Yellows don't fail to follow through because they don't care — they fail because the novelty was the fuel, and novelty is perishable. Understanding this lets you design around it: give them the launch, not the maintenance.

Edge case

The Red who is wrong, confidently. Reds trust their instincts, which is an advantage right up until the instinct is bad. Because they project certainty, nobody challenges them — and the room follows the arrow off a cliff.

E · Common Misconception
"Reds are just aggressive, and Yellows are just flaky."
Both descriptions confuse a strength with its failure mode. Red's directness is what makes a stuck project move; it only becomes "aggression" when it costs them the room. Yellow's enthusiasm is what makes people want to join; it only becomes "flakiness" when nothing is ever finished. The behaviour is identical in both readings — what changes is whether the strength is being used well or leaking. Erikson's point is that you can't remove the trait without removing the strength, so you manage the leak instead.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Reds and Yellows are usually the most visible people in any organisation — the ones who set the pace and get heard. If you can't communicate with them, you can't influence the direction of anything. And if you are one, knowing your failure mode is the difference between being seen as a leader and being seen as a problem.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You need a Red's approval and a Yellow's buy-in, in the same conversation.

Decision 1: Give one combined pitch — context, philosophy, data, feelings.

→ Consequence: The Red checks out at minute two; the Yellow never gets to the exciting part.

Decision 2: Lead with the bottom line and the date (Red), then the vision and the possibility (Yellow).

→ Consequence: Both are engaged within sixty seconds. The detail can come after the yes.

Decision 3: Give the Red a decision to make and the Yellow a launch to own.

→ Consequence: The Red gets closure, the Yellow gets momentum, and the follow-through lands on someone else — where it belongs.

MODULE 04 Green & Blue — The Deep End

Both take their time. One is protecting the people; the other is protecting the accuracy.

A · Core Explanation

Green (Steadiness) is the colour of stability. Greens value harmony, calm and predictability. They are the reason teams don't tear themselves apart: patient, dependable, loyal, and genuinely good at listening. They are the emotional glue of a group — the person who notices that someone's been quiet and asks, gently, outside the meeting. Greens commit for the long haul; once they're in, they're in. They are also, for the same reasons, resistant to change, uncomfortable with confrontation, and prone to going along with a decision they privately disagree with rather than risk a scene.

That last trait is the one to watch. A Green's silence is not agreement. It's often the sound of someone calculating whether speaking up is worth the discomfort. Left unaddressed, that silence curdles into resentment — and because Greens avoid direct conflict, it tends to come out sideways: passive resistance, quiet withdrawal, or a sudden resignation that shocks everyone.

Blue (Conscientiousness) is the colour of precision. Blues are analytical, methodical and detail-focused, and they care about being correct in a way that goes beyond mere tidiness. They want the structure, the facts, the source, the edge cases. In any team, they're the ones who catch the mistake nobody else saw — the error in the contract, the number that doesn't reconcile, the assumption that everyone else waved through. Blues are invaluable in research, finance, engineering, and anywhere the cost of being wrong is high.

The cost of Blue is speed. Blues can become perfectionists who slow decisions past the point of usefulness, and their focus on logic can read as coldness to more emotionally driven people. They also struggle without clear instructions or in environments that demand improvisation — which, to a Red, looks like obstruction. So the two careful colours share something important: they are the ones most often misread as "the problem" by the fast colours, when in fact they are doing the work the fast colours skip.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

An anchor and a checklist. A Green is the anchor: invisible when the sea is calm, absolutely decisive when the storm arrives. A Blue is the pre-flight checklist: tedious right up until the moment it catches the thing that would have brought the plane down. Both are boring in good times and irreplaceable in bad ones — which is precisely why they get undervalued until it's too late.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

The Blue who saves the contract. Everyone is ready to sign; the Blue asks about the clause on page eleven. It's wrong. The deal would have been legally exposed. Nobody thanks them, because nothing happened.

Counter-intuitive

A Green's silence is the loudest warning in the room. When the most agreeable person on the team goes quiet and stops volunteering, that's not contentment — it's a decision being made internally that you're about to find out about the hard way.

Edge case

The Blue who can't start. Given no clear brief and told to "just improvise," a Blue doesn't become flexible — they freeze. The trait that makes them excellent with structure makes them helpless without it.

E · Common Misconception
"If they'd just speak up / just decide / just be more flexible, we'd be fine."
Each of those demands asks a careful person to act against their grain in the exact moment they feel least safe. A Green asked to "just decide" in a tense meeting is being asked to do the one thing their whole style is organised to avoid. A Blue asked to "just improvise" is being asked to abandon the verification that makes them valuable. The productive move isn't demanding the trait change — it's changing the conditions: give the Green advance notice and a private channel; give the Blue a brief and a deadline.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Fast colours get the credit because their contribution is visible. Careful colours prevent disasters, and prevention is invisible by definition. If you only reward the visible half of the team, you'll eventually lose the half that was quietly keeping you out of trouble.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You need a decision from a room that includes a Green and a Blue.

Decision 1: Push for a vote in the meeting to keep momentum.

→ Consequence: The Green agrees to avoid conflict and resents it; the Blue withholds because the data isn't ready. You get a decision that quietly doesn't hold.

Decision 2: Send the material 48 hours ahead and invite written concerns before the meeting.

→ Consequence: The Blue arrives prepared and the Green arrives with concerns already on paper — where they're safe to raise.

Decision 3: Explicitly ask the Green for objections in private, and thank them for raising them.

→ Consequence: You surface the real objection before it becomes passive resistance, and you've taught the Green that speaking up is safe.

MODULE 05 Adaptation — On the Listener's Terms

The single most useful skill in the book: change your delivery, not your message.

A · Core Explanation

The central practical claim of Erikson's book is short: communication happens on the listener's terms. What that means is that you don't get to decide what your message was — the person receiving it does. If your perfectly reasonable sentence lands as an attack, then as far as the conversation is concerned, an attack is what happened. You can argue about intent afterwards, but you've already lost the room.

Adaptation is the fix, and Erikson is emphatic about what it is not. It is not changing your personality, compromising your values, or becoming fake. It's adjusting your delivery — pace, structure, warmth, level of detail — to match how the other person actually processes. A Red needs the bottom line first; a Yellow needs the vision and the energy; a Green needs reassurance and time; a Blue needs facts and structure. Same message, four different envelopes.

It starts with self-awareness. You cannot adapt deliberately if you don't know your own default setting, because your default is invisible to you — it just feels like "normal." If you're Red, your directness feels efficient; it may land as harsh. If you're Yellow, your enthusiasm feels generous; it may land as overwhelming. If you're Blue, your precision feels responsible; it may land as cold. If you're Green, your accommodation feels kind; it may land as evasive.

The reason adaptation is worth the effort is that it's the only lever you actually control. You can't make someone else more patient, faster, warmer, or more logical. You can change how you open a conversation. Erikson also makes a deeper point: real growth comes from interacting with people who are not like you. Staying inside your own colour is comfortable and stagnant. The stretch is where the skill develops.

B · Diagram C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

Four different power sockets. The electricity is identical. The plug shape is not. You can have the most reliable current in the world, but if you push a UK plug into a European socket, nothing charges — and blaming the socket is not a strategy. Adaptation is carrying an adaptor. The message never changes; the connector does.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Leading with the ask. To a Red: "I need a decision by Thursday — here's the one-line recommendation." Same request to a Blue: "Here are the three options, the data behind each, and my recommendation."

Counter-intuitive

Being clearer can make it worse with a Yellow. A crisp, bulleted, formal brief that would delight a Blue can drain all the energy out of a Yellow and kill their engagement. Clarity isn't universally good — it's colour-dependent.

Edge case

Adapting to a Green in a crisis. The instinct is to raise urgency. But pressure makes a Green withdraw — so the adaptive move is to slow your voice down, name the concern explicitly, and give them a private channel. Urgency communicated calmly.

E · Common Misconception
"Adapting my style means being fake / manipulative."
Adaptation is only manipulative if the underlying message is something the other person wouldn't endorse. If your message is honest and your goal is mutual understanding, then choosing the format most likely to be understood is just competence — the same as speaking more slowly to someone learning your language. The opposite of adapting isn't authenticity; it's laziness dressed up as authenticity. "This is just how I am" usually means "I'd rather not adjust than be understood."
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Adaptation is the mechanism that converts everything else in this lesson into results. Knowing the four colours changes nothing until you change what you do on Monday. And it's the one variable entirely under your control — you don't need anyone's permission or cooperation to start.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: You're about to send a difficult message to someone whose colour you've figured out.

Decision 1: Write it the way you'd want to receive it — it's clear to you.

→ Consequence: It lands well with people who share your colour. Everyone else misreads it.

Decision 2: Rewrite the delivery: headline first for Red, warmth and possibility for Yellow, reassurance and a private channel for Green, evidence and structure for Blue.

→ Consequence: The message survives contact with a different brain.

Decision 3: Check the landing — ask "does that land the way I meant it?"

→ Consequence: You find out whether your adaptation worked, instead of assuming it did. That's how the skill improves.

MODULE 06 Stress & Group Dynamics

Stress doesn't create new behaviour. It exaggerates what was already there.

A · Core Explanation

Erikson's most practically useful insight is that stress amplifies personality. It doesn't turn a Green into a Red or a Blue into a Yellow. It takes whatever was already the dominant trait and turns the dial up — usually past the point where the strength becomes a liability. In other words, the moment you most need someone's strengths is the moment their weaknesses are most likely to show.

A stressed Red becomes more controlling and more impatient. They push harder, dismiss alternative views faster, and make snap decisions without weighing the fallout. They stop listening, because listening feels like delay. A stressed Yellow becomes scattered and impulsive: talking more, jumping between tasks, leaving a trail of half-finished things. Sometimes their humour still lightens the room — but often it just adds noise to a situation that needed less.

A stressed Green retreats further inward. They go quiet, avoid decisions, and agree to things they don't agree with rather than risk friction. To the fast colours this reads as apathy or stubbornness, when it's actually overwhelm. A stressed Blue becomes more critical and more perfectionist: fixating on minor details, demanding more analysis, refusing to sign off until everything is flawless. To everyone else this looks like obstruction; to the Blue it looks like due diligence.

Notice what happens when you put stressed colours together. A stressed Red pushing for a decision meets a stressed Blue demanding more data — and both experience the other as the reason the project is failing. A stressed Yellow's chaos meets a stressed Green's withdrawal, and the Green silently absorbs the mess. Nobody is behaving badly. Everybody is behaving predictably. Which is good news, because predictable problems have predictable interventions. Leaders who know the pattern can give a Red autonomy and a clear goal, a Yellow freedom and company, a Green reassurance and safety, and a Blue structure and time — before the blow-up rather than after it.

B · Diagram — move the stress dial C · Visual Analogy
Analogy

A fader on a mixing desk. Every colour is already on the track. Stress doesn't add a new instrument — it pushes the fader up on the loudest channel until it clips and distorts the whole mix. A distorted Red sounds like aggression. A distorted Blue sounds like obstruction. Same musician, same instrument, too much gain.

D · Three Real-World Examples
Obvious

Deadline week. The Red becomes dictatorial, the Yellow becomes chaotic, the Green goes silent, the Blue asks for one more week. Every one of those is the normal trait, amplified.

Counter-intuitive

The stressed Green looks like the calm one. Withdrawal is easily mistaken for composure. The quietest person in a crisis may be the most overloaded — they've simply stopped signalling, which is the opposite of what their team assumes.

Edge case

Two stressed people blaming each other accurately. The Red says the Blue is blocking progress. The Blue says the Red is cutting corners. Both statements are true, and neither is the problem. The problem is the pressure, and the fix is upstream of both of them.

E · Common Misconception
"Once things calm down, everyone will go back to normal."
The traits don't reset just because the deadline passes. What stress does is reveal the failure mode permanently enough that colleagues start to believe it is the person. "He's impossible under pressure" becomes the story, and the story outlives the deadline. This is why Erikson emphasises early intervention: catch the stress behaviour while it's still situational, before it calcifies into a reputation. Relief arrives; the label tends to stay.
F · Why It Matters
Why it matters: Almost every "difficult person" story at work is a stress story. If you can recognise the amplified pattern, you can intervene on the pressure instead of on the person — which is both more effective and considerably kinder.
G · Practical Application

Scenario: Your team is under pressure and the mood is deteriorating.

Decision 1: Push harder and repeat the deadline. Urgency is the motivator.

→ Consequence: Every colour amplifies. The Red escalates, the Yellow scatters, the Green withdraws, the Blue digs in. The team is now working against itself.

Decision 2: De-escalate by colour: give the Red control and a clear goal, the Yellow an audience, the Green safety and a private channel, the Blue structure and time.

→ Consequence: Each person's failure mode loses its fuel. The strengths come back online first.

Decision 3: Name the pressure out loud: "We're all in the exaggerated version of ourselves right now."

→ Consequence: The behaviour gets reframed as situational rather than personal — which stops it becoming a permanent label.

Memory Techniques

1. The traffic-light-plus-water mnemonic. Red = stop-and-go leader (fast, task). Yellow = sunshine (fast, people). Green = grass (steady, people). Blue = deep water (steady, task). Two are warm and fast; two are cool and slow.

2. Two questions, four quadrants. When you meet someone, ask only: "Fast or careful?" and "Task or people?" Those two answers place them on the compass. You don't need a test — you need two questions.

3. The strength/failure pairing. Memorise each colour as a pair, never alone: Red = decisive/impatient. Yellow = inspiring/scattered. Green = loyal/avoidant. Blue = accurate/paralysed. The pair is the colour — you can't have one half without the other.

4. Spaced recall. Re-read one module tonight, one tomorrow, one next week. The module checkmarks are saved in your browser, so you can see which quadrants you've already walked.

Still Confused? FAQ

Is this just a horoscope for colleagues?

It becomes a horoscope the moment you use it to explain away behaviour instead of to predict it. The test is simple: does the model give you a falsifiable expectation? If you conclude your colleague is Blue, you should now be able to predict that a vague, urgent, emotionally-framed request will produce resistance, while a structured one with data will produce cooperation. If that prediction fails repeatedly, your read is wrong and you should update it. Astrology doesn't ask you to update. A useful model does. Erikson is also careful to say everyone is a mix, people shift with context, and no one is a pure type — which is exactly the humility that keeps a framework from hardening into a stereotype.

What if I genuinely don't know what colour I am?

Start with the two axes rather than the four colours. Ask yourself: when a decision is on the table, do I move fast or do I want more time? And when I'm in a group, is my attention on the outcome or on the people? Your answers put you in a quadrant. Then check the failure mode — that's usually the fastest diagnostic. Do you get told you're too blunt (Red), too scattered (Yellow), too quiet (Green), or too critical (Blue)? People rarely report their strengths accurately but they almost always remember their criticism. And if you truly feel like two colours at once, that's normal and expected: most people have a primary and a secondary, and the secondary often shows up in a different context, like home versus work.

How do I deal with someone who is a different colour and senior to me?

Adaptation doesn't require authority — it only requires you to change your own delivery, which you can always do. With a senior Red, lead with the recommendation and the deadline, never with the journey. With a senior Yellow, open with the possibility and the energy, then anchor it to a concrete next step before the meeting ends. With a senior Green, slow your pace, avoid surprise, and give them room to raise concerns without an audience. With a senior Blue, send the material in advance with sources and be ready for detail questions. Note that seniority often amplifies whatever the person's colour already is, because senior people get less feedback and more reinforcement. That makes the pattern stronger, not subtler — which actually makes it easier to work with once you've seen it.

What if the other person is under heavy stress and their colour seems to have changed?

It hasn't changed — it's been amplified. That's the core of module six. A stressed Green can look like a stubborn wall, a stressed Blue can look like a saboteur, a stressed Red can look like a bully, and a stressed Yellow can look like they've stopped caring. In every case, the underlying trait is the same one you already knew; the volume is just past the point of distortion. The practical implication is that you should adapt to the amplified version, not the baseline version. Give more reassurance, more structure, more autonomy or more company than you normally would — and, crucially, name the pressure out loud. People behave less extremely when they believe the extremity is situational rather than a verdict on their character.

Can a team be all one colour? Should it be?

It can, and it usually looks great right up until it doesn't. A team of all Reds moves at extraordinary speed and never checks anything. A team of all Yellows generates dazzling ideas and ships none of them. A team of all Greens is harmonious and paralysed by change. A team of all Blues is impeccable and far too slow to compete. The value of a mixed team isn't diversity for its own sake — it's that each colour's failure mode is another colour's strength. The Red's recklessness is caught by the Blue; the Blue's paralysis is broken by the Red; the Yellow's chaos is contained by the Green; the Green's inertia is disturbed by the Yellow. The catch is that a mixed team only gets this benefit if the members understand the model. Without that shared vocabulary, the same mix produces friction instead of complementarity — which is, more or less, the whole argument of the book.

What's the single thing to take away if I forget everything else?

Communication happens on the listener's terms. Everything else in the book is downstream of that one sentence. The colours give you a vocabulary for figuring out what those terms are; adaptation is what you do with the answer; and the stress section tells you when to expect the terms to shift. But if you only remember one thing, remember that you do not get to decide what your message was — the person receiving it does. That single reframe turns a hundred daily frustrations from moral judgments ("why is this person like this?") into engineering problems ("what delivery would land with this person?"). Engineering problems are solvable. Moral judgments are not. That's why the book works.
