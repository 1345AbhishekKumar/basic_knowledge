The Billionaire Who Automates Everything

Thomas Peterffy, 81 and worth about $80 billion, built Interactive Brokers into a $120 billion firm with 71% profit margins — 3,000 people, most of them engineers. His rule never changed: turn the job into steps, turn the steps into code, and route around anyone who blocks the machine. Here is that method in five lab procedures.

What You’ll Learn

  • Turn any repeated job into steps a machine can run.
  • Price by math and hedge every position you take.
  • Route around each rule that blocks your machine.
  • Quit the old game and sell the platform instead.
  • Stay lean: few people, low prices, steady work.

Purpose: use this to remove dull work from your own job or firm — even one spreadsheet. One clear win: you will be able to sketch the automation loop for any repeated task.

01 / PROCEDURE
First bench in the lab

You are a programmer, not a trader.

Peterffy’s own words. Every job he met became a card, a program, a library.

Core explanation. Raised poor in post-war Budapest — cutting gum into five pieces to sell, dragging a bathtub of scrap with eight kids — Peterffy learned that small steps earn real money. Landing in New York in 1965 with $65-a-week road-drawing work, he met the untouched Olivetti Programma 101, learned its 100 English words in a night, and stacked working cards into a library that cut 20-minute sums to 30 seconds. Colleagues lined up at his desk each morning. Later, consulting through Aranyi into Jarecki’s silver desk, he kept the same stance: he was a programmer among traders, and the proudest line of his life is that the important people in his firm are programmers too.

PROCEDURE 01
  1. Watch the repeat. Find the sum done daily by hand.
  2. Write the card. Break it into steps; save each working one.
  3. Stack the library. Label functions; share the pile.
  4. Stay the programmer. Keep building; let others opine.
Card to library
20 minutes by hand becomes 30 seconds by card.

Everyday examples

What: You retype the same weekly report from three sheets. → So what: One saved formula turns 20 minutes into seconds, like his road sums.

What: You keep a folder of working email replies. → So what: A labelled pile beats rewriting — his card stack method.

What: You learn the one tool nobody touches. → So what: The ignored machine is often the open door.

Analogy: Recipe cards — write once, cook in seconds forever.

❌ What people get wrong: He was a trading genius — in fact he calls himself a programmer, and so is his team.

💡 Why this matters: Code compounds; intuition does not. The library pays daily.

🔗 Links to: Module 2 — the library now needs fair prices to compute.

Mnemonic: W-C-S — Watch, Card, Stack Recall it in the quiz

Answer: Watch the repeat, Card the steps, Stack the library. Missed one? Re-read Procedure 01.

02 / PROCEDURE
Price it right

Let math quote, hedge everything.

Sheets in pockets, models on paper — then the nerve to trust them.

Core explanation. At Jarecki’s bullion desk Peterffy had no framework — “how do you decide when to buy, when to sell?” — so he built one: programs turned data into bid and ask quotes (= prices to buy and sell at) on green-bar paper, runners racing them to the pit while rivals guessed. He wrote his own options-pricing equation from price, volatility (= jumpiness), and time left, testing it quietly on silver until nearly every trade won. Starting alone in 1977 with $200,000, pockets stuffed with folded fair-value sheets (IBM in the breast pocket, DuPont in the trousers), he lost half his capital fast — then rebuilt from 1977 to 1982 by hedging (= offsetting) every trade and obeying the sheets.

PROCEDURE 02
  1. Model fair value. Price, jumpiness, time left — one equation.
  2. Print the sheet. Fresh quotes daily; carry them everywhere.
  3. Hedge each trade. Every bet gets its offset; no naked risk.
  4. Obey the paper. Follow sheets when fear screams.
Quote, hedge, obey
Math decides; the human executes and offsets.

Everyday examples

What: You set a max price before a flea market. → So what: A written fair value stops impulse overpaying.

What: You pair every risky promise with a backup. → So what: Hedging turns a blowup into a bad day.

Analogy: A seatbelt — the hedge never drives, it just saves the drive.

❌ What people get wrong: Models remove losses — in fact he lost half his stake first, then added hedging.

💡 Why this matters: A priced, hedged book survives long enough for the edge to pay.

🔗 Links to: Module 3 — good prices are useless if the floor blocks you.

Mnemonic: Q-H-O — Quote, Hedge, Obey

Answer: The asset price, its jumpiness (volatility), and time left to expiry.

03 / PROCEDURE
Walls into doors

When blocked, build a stranger door.

Banned devices, distant screens, hijacked feeds — press play for the 16-year hack chain.

Core explanation. Exchanges kept outlawing his edge, so he rebuilt it stranger each time. Hurt knee ending floor standing, he fed Quotron prices by cutting its wire and reading pulses on an oscilloscope (= screen showing voltage wiggles), giving his 1980s computer the whole market live. Snubbed by specialists, he hired tall women traders and phoned orders in — “making money hand over fist” — until forced to become a market maker. Answer: a 1983 handheld with touchscreen, synced by sprinting clerks; banned in Chicago pits, he answered with wall monitors flashing color-coded digits readable from 30 feet. On Nasdaq he hijacked the terminal line — and when ordered back to keyboards, built typing metal fingers that hammered keys faster than hands.

Press play to step through the stages.

PROCEDURE 03
  1. Name the wall. Ban, distance, or snub — say it plainly.
  2. Build the odd door. Handhelds, colors, wires, fingers.
  3. Ship in days. Colors learned in a day or two; robot in a week.
  4. Keep the machine. Offer the mannequin; keep trading.
Static chain — same insight on paper
Four bans, four stranger doors, 1971–1987.

Everyday examples

What: The form bans uploads; you print, scan, send. → So what: Ugly doors still open — ship the workaround.

What: A filmmaker wins a $10,000 bet trading a year on your two-week course. → So what: If the system is simple, anyone can run it — that is the point.

What: Colored pencils on a kitchen table birth the wall code. → So what: Cheap prototypes beat long meetings.

Analogy: Water past stones — blocked here, flowing there, never arguing.

❌ What people get wrong: Rule-bending meant cheating — in fact he met each ruling with a disclosed device and kept trading openly.

💡 Why this matters: Arbitrary walls are a gift: each one you pass becomes your moat.

🔗 Links to: Module 4 — in 1987 the machine finally trades alone; now sell it to all.

Mnemonic: W-O-S — Wall, Odd door, Ship

Answer: Built metal typing fingers that hammered orders faster than hands — the office sounded like bursts of typing.

04 / PROCEDURE
Sell the shovel

Quit the race you won; sell the track.

Timber Hill made $25M then $50M — and he still walked toward the harder problem.

Core explanation. By the late 1980s his network ran New York to Hong Kong; Goldman’s offers climbed to $900 million and he named $3 billion to end the talk — he was not selling. In 1993 he launched Interactive Brokers, an elegant answer years early for a market still stubbornly analog. When exchanges finally went screen-based, the floor traders who mocked his sheets became his customers — “they needed a way to continue their business from their office.” He skipped the 2000s speed war of microwave towers against Citadel (“not interesting to me anymore”), took the firm public in 2007 via a Dutch auction saving $80 million in bank fees, and shut Timber Hill in 2017 to run a pure brokerage.

PROCEDURE 04
  1. Price the exit high. $3B means “not selling.”
  2. Launch before need. Build the platform years early.
  3. Enroll old rivals. Mockers become customers.
  4. List it cheap. Dutch auction; skip the road show.
From pit to platform
Mockers in, speed war out, brokerage left.

Everyday examples

What: Your shortcut script helps three teams. → So what: Stop selling hours; sell the script as the product.

What: A rival copies you, then pays for your tool. → So what: Yesterday’s mocker is tomorrow’s subscriber.

Analogy: Gold-rush shovels — diggers fade, the shovel shop stands.

❌ What people get wrong: The IPO raised his capital — in fact he owned nearly all of it; the listing was advertising.

💡 Why this matters: Platforms outlive edges; owners of the track collect every race.

🔗 Links to: Module 5 — the platform only wins if it stays brutally lean.

Mnemonic: E-C-L — Exit high, Customers from rivals, List cheap

Answer: It cost billions and bored him — building the best trading platform was the harder, fresher challenge.

05 / PROCEDURE
Run it forever

Charge less, keep little, work daily.

4 million customers, $700B guarded, 3,000 staff — and the chairman learning sales at 81.

Core explanation. What remains is a tight machine: 4 million customers, over $700 billion in client assets, just 3,000 employees — most of them engineers — turning $5.2 billion of revenue into $3.7 billion of profit in 2024. The creed is automate everything, then pass the savings on: fees so low that rivals quit competing on price, the Costco logic he admires without ever visiting. He stepped down as CEO at 75, still owns near 70%, and runs the sales desk nobody wanted — “learning as I go.” Asked his pride, he names the money saved for people by efficient markets; asked his secret, he answers hard work and common sense, never having read a business book.

PROCEDURE 05
  1. Hire engineers. Most seats build; few administer.
  2. Cut the fee. Charge less; make rivals surrender.
  3. Keep working. New desk at 81; learn it live.
  4. Measure pride in savings. Efficiency shared with users.
Lean forever loop
Automate, cut price, grow, repeat.

Everyday examples

What: You halve your fee after automating delivery. → So what: Lower prices from machines beat louder marketing.

What: You take the unloved task at work. → So what: The empty desk teaches fastest — his sales desk at 81.

What: Three hours of talk earns $1.7B on screen. → So what: Systems earn while you speak; that is the goal.

Analogy: A mill wheel — same water, more flour, cheaper bread.

❌ What people get wrong: Lean means tired — in fact his leanness funds the lowest fees and the longest run.

💡 Why this matters: Efficiency shared with users is a moat no ad budget crosses.

🔗 Links to: Module 1 — the wheel restarts: watch the next repeat.

Mnemonic: E-C-W — Engineers, Cut fees, Work on

Answer: The money saved for people by making markets more efficient — not the billions made.
