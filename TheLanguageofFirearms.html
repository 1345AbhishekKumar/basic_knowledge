Module 01 · Measurement

Caliber — The Diameter That Decides Everything

Let's start with the single most important number on any box of ammunition: caliber. In its purest form, caliber is simply a diameter — the internal diameter of a firearm's barrel, or equivalently, the diameter of the projectile it fires. In metric countries this is refreshingly direct: a "9mm" firearm has a bore of roughly nine millimeters and fires a projectile about nine millimeters wide. The United States historically stuck with inches, giving us names like .30 caliber — lands measuring approximately 0.300 inches, or 7.62mm.

But here's the nuance that trips up almost every beginner: the word "nominal" is doing heavy lifting. Barrels aren't smooth tubes — they're cut with spiraling lands and grooves (the rifling) that spin the bullet for stability. The nominal caliber usually matches the land diameter, while the bullet itself is sized to the slightly larger groove diameter so it engraves into the rifling. A 9mm Luger actually fires a 9.01mm (.355") bullet; a .308 Winchester fires 7.82mm. That tiny overshoot is deliberate — snug enough to grip the rifling, not so tight that pressure spikes dangerously.

Imperial naming adds a second trap: the number sometimes describes the case, not the bullet — .45 ACP refers to the approximate case diameter. The modern metric system avoids all of this with one elegant formula: Bullet Diameter × Case Length, as in 9×19mm Parabellum or 5.56×45mm NATO. The first number tells you the projectile will fit the barrel; the second tells you the case will fit the chamber and bolt face. Read both, and you can speak ammunition fluently anywhere on Earth.

Step by Step — Decoding a Metric Designation

  1. Read the first number — bullet (bore) diameter in millimeters: "7.62" in 7.62×39mm.
  2. Read the second number — case length in millimeters: "39" means a 39mm case.
  3. Check for suffix letters — "R" means rimmed case (7.62×54mmR); NATO designations carry military spec.
  4. Never assume same-first-number = same cartridge — 7.62×39, 7.62×51, and 7.62×54mmR share a diameter family and nothing else.
  5. Cross-reference the headstamp — the markings on the cartridge base must match the barrel marking before loading.

Three Ways This Shows Up

OBVIOUS CASE
9×19mm Parabellum — the textbook designation

The world's most common pistol cartridge reads exactly as designed: 9mm bullet, 19mm case. Police, military, and civilians on every continent can pick up this box and know precisely what's inside — the metric formula is the closest thing ammunition has to a universal language.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE
.308 Winchester vs. 7.62×39mm — same-ish number, different worlds

Both carry a "seven-point-six-something" flavor, yet the .308 fires a 7.82mm bullet from a 51mm case while the AK's 7.62×39 fires a 7.90mm bullet from a 39mm case. The imperial .308 name refers to the land diameter; the metric name refers to the bullet. Neither fits the other's rifle — the shared digits are a coincidence, not a compatibility promise.

EDGE CASE
.45 ACP — when the name describes the wrong part

The "45" in .45 ACP (11.43×23mm) historically refers to the approximate case diameter, while the actual bullet measures 0.452" (11.48mm). Meanwhile 5.7×28mm fires the same 5.69mm bullet as a .223 but from a completely different case. Lesson: names are labels, dimensions are truth — always verify against a spec sheet.

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYThink of a cartridge like a house key. The bullet diameter is the key's blade width — it must match the lock's pins (the bore) or it physically won't turn. The case length is the key's length — it must reach the right depth in the chamber. A key that's almost right doesn't "almost open" the door; it jams. Same with ammunition: close enough is not a category that exists.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG"9mm means exactly 9.00 millimeters." It doesn't — the real bullet is 9.01mm (.355"), sized to the grooves, not the lands. The companion error is assuming a bigger number means a bigger bullet across naming systems: .45 ACP's number describes the case, and gauge (next module) runs backwards entirely.
◎ WHY THIS MATTERSEvery firearm is an engineered pressure vessel, and the bore–bullet match is the seal. Wrong diameter means either a bullet that tumbles without engaging the rifling, or one that can't exit at all — pressure finds the weakest point, and that point can be the gun in your hands. Literacy in the Diameter × Case Length formula is the cheapest safety equipment ever invented.

Module 02 · The Shotgun Exception

Gauge — The Scale That Runs Backwards

Just when you've mastered measuring bores with a ruler, shotguns tear up the rulebook. A shotgun's size isn't a direct length at all — it's expressed in gauge, a unit inherited from the old British avoirdupois weight system. The definition is wonderfully archaic: the gauge number is the count of pure lead balls, each exactly the bore's diameter, that together weigh one pound. A 12-gauge barrel passes lead spheres such that twelve of them make a pound. A 10-gauge's balls are heavier, so fewer fit the pound — which is why smaller gauge numbers mean larger bores.

That inverse relationship is the single most counter-intuitive fact in small-arms measurement. A 10-gauge outruns a 12-gauge, which outruns a 16, which outruns a 20. Converting gauge to a real diameter requires cube-root math — Diameter(in) = 1.66992 ÷ ∛Gauge — because weight scales with volume, and volume scales with the cube of diameter. Running the numbers: a 12-gauge bore is about 18.42mm (.725"), a 20-gauge about 15.62mm (.615"), and a 16-gauge sits between at 16.89mm (.665"). The common sizes you'll actually encounter are 10, 12, 16, and 20 gauge.

And then there's the rebel: the .410 bore. It refuses the gauge system entirely and is measured in straight decimal inches — 0.410", or 10.4mm. If forced into gauge language it would be roughly a 67-gauge. For a beginner this distinction is vital: the .410 plays by caliber rules while everything else on the shotgun rack plays by weight rules. Mixing up the systems — or assuming "20" is bigger than "12" because 20 > 12 — is how people buy the wrong shells, and wrong shotgun shells don't just fail to fit; they can lodge inside a larger bore with catastrophic results.

Three Ways This Shows Up

OBVIOUS CASE
12-gauge — the default shotgun

Walk into any gun store and the 12-gauge dominates the rack: hunting, home defense, clay sports. Its name is a literal recipe — twelve bore-diameter lead balls to the pound — yielding the 18.42mm bore that anchors the entire shotgun ecosystem.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE
10-gauge > 12-gauge > 20-gauge

Arithmetic instinct says 20 is bigger than 10. Gauge says the opposite: fewer balls per pound means each ball is bigger. A waterfowl hunter's 10-gauge magnum throws a heavier payload than a 12, while a youth's 20-gauge kicks gentler with a 15.62mm bore. The scale is a division problem, not a ruler.

EDGE CASE
.410 bore — the opt-out

The .410 is measured in decimal inches (10.4mm), skipping gauge entirely. If converted, it would equal roughly a 67-gauge — a number you'll never see stamped on a receiver. Beginners who file ".410" under "small gauge number" have miscategorized it as the largest shotgun; it's actually the smallest common bore.

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYGauge works like scoops per pound of ice cream. If a pound fills 10 scoops, each scoop is huge; if it fills 20, each is small. The gauge number counts scoops — so the count goes up as the bore goes down. The .410 is the shop that sells by the gram instead and never joined the scoop club.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONGAssuming gauge is a direct measurement of length or diameter. It isn't — it's a count derived from weight, converted through cube-root math. The practical sin that follows: grabbing "20" because it's a bigger number than "12," or treating .410 as a gauge at all.
◎ WHY THIS MATTERSShotgun shells are the most commonly mismatched ammunition on Earth because the naming fights intuition. Worse, a smaller shell can physically pass into a larger chamber and wait there — a hidden obstruction the next, correctly-sized shell will detonate against. Knowing the scale's direction is literally barrel-burst prevention.

Module 03 · Anatomy & Sequence

The Cartridge & The Eight-Beat Firing Cycle

Zoom in from the barrel to the thing that travels through it. A cartridge — casually called a "round" — is a self-contained machine with exactly four parts: the case (usually brass, sometimes steel or aluminum), the primer, the propellant, and the projectile. The case is the structural backbone, and its base design dictates where it can live: rimless cases (9×19mm, 7.62×39mm) stack neatly in box magazines for autoloaders; rimmed cases like the 7.62×54mmR — the "R" says so — suit revolvers and break-actions; belted magnum cases use a ring near the base as a headspace stop to keep high-pressure loads positioned safely.

The primer is a tiny cup of impact-sensitive explosive. Strike it and a jet of flame shoots through the flash hole into the powder, which doesn't explode so much as burn furiously — generating superheated gas at pressures measured in tens of thousands of PSI (or megapascals in CIP land). That trapped pressure is the entire engine. Primers come in two flavors: Boxer, with one central anvil that pops out easily for handloaders, and Berdan, with twin anvils that make spent cases a pain to reload — a legacy split between U.S. and European military surplus. The bullet itself is tailored to purpose: FMJ for reliable feeding and penetration, hollow points that mushroom to dump energy and limit over-penetration, soft points and ballistic tips splitting the difference.

Now scale back out to the machine. In any repeating firearm those four components are consumed through the firing cycle: Feed → Chamber → Lock → Fire → Unlock → Extract → Eject → Cock. The magazine spring presents a round, the bolt strips and seats it, the breech locks gas-tight, the firing pin falls, pressure drops, the bolt unlocks, the claw pulls the spent case, the ejector flings it clear, and the recoil spring rewinds the whole thing — in a small fraction of a second. A failure at any single beat is a stoppage. Master the eight beats and you can diagnose almost any jam by asking: which beat didn't happen?

The Eight Beats

  1. Feed — magazine spring lifts the next round into the bolt's path.
  2. Chamber — the forward-moving bolt strips the round and seats it in the chamber.
  3. Lock — bolt engages locking lugs, sealing the breech against pressure.
  4. Fire — firing pin strikes primer; flame ignites propellant; pressure launches the bullet.
  5. Unlock — once pressure is safe, the mechanism disengages the bolt.
  6. Extract — the extractor claw grips the case rim and pulls it from the chamber.
  7. Eject — the ejector strikes the case base, spinning it out the ejection port.
  8. Cock — the compressed spring drives the bolt forward again, resetting the hammer/striker.

Three Ways This Shows Up

OBVIOUS CASE
9×19mm FMJ at the range — the textbook round

A brass rimless case stacks cleanly in the magazine, a Boxer primer pops out for easy reloading, the powder charge runs the slide at just the right pressure, and the full metal jacket bullet feeds reliably into anything. This is why it's the default training cartridge on the planet.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE
Hollow points deform on purpose

Instinct says a "better" bullet stays perfect. Reality: a JHP's nose cavity is engineered to mushroom on impact — trading penetration for a wider wound channel and, crucially, less over-penetration through walls or past a target. For defense, the bullet that falls apart is the safer one.

EDGE CASE
7.62×54mmR — a 19th-century rim still in service

The "R" marks a rimmed case designed for single-shots and machine-gun belts, not box magazines — rims stack like coins and jam staggered columns. Yet this 1891 design still serves today because the rim gives an extractor something enormous to grab. Meanwhile belted cases like the .300 Win Mag use a ring ahead of the groove as a headspace shelf for magnum pressures.

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYA cartridge is a one-use pressure cooker: case = pot, primer = match, powder = fuel, bullet = cork. The firing cycle is a pit crew — eight choreographed moves (fuel, seat, seal, ignite, release, pull the old tank, toss it, reset) executed in under a second. One fumbled move and the race stops.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONGCalling the whole cartridge a "bullet." The bullet is only the projectile — the part that leaves the barrel. The case, primer, and powder stay behind (the case gets ejected). This isn't pedantry: every malfunction report, reloading manual, and spec sheet uses these terms precisely.
◎ WHY THIS MATTERSThe eight beats are the diagnostic language of every firearm. "Failure to extract" and "failure to feed" aren't jargon — they name the exact beat that quit, which tells you whether to look at the extractor claw, the magazine spring, or the ammunition itself.
✦ MEMORY TECHNIQUE"Fat Cats Lazily Feast Until Everything's Eaten, Content."
Feed · Chamber · Lock · Fire · Unlock · Extract · Eject · Cock. For the four components: Case, Primer, Powder, Projectile — "Careful People Practice Precision."

Module 04 · Taxonomy

Classifying Firearms — Action & Purpose

Firearms are a family of specialized tools, and classification is how you file them mentally. The first axis is the action — the mechanical means of driving the firing cycle. Manual actions make the shooter the engine: bolt-action (lift, pull, push, lock — the accuracy king for precision rifles and big-game hunting, à la the Mauser 98 and Remington 700), lever-action (the Winchester 1873 and its Western heritage, faster than a bolt with a tubular magazine under the barrel), pump-action (slide the forend back and forward — the near-unbreakable standard for modern shotguns), and break-action (hinge the barrel open, the simplest mechanism of all, beloved in clay sports).

Self-loading actions harvest the cartridge's own energy. A semi-automatic fires one round per trigger pull and reloads itself — the dominant civilian action in pistols, rifles, and shotguns (AR-15, Glock). A fully automatic weapon keeps firing as long as the trigger is held, cycling those eight beats at astonishing speed — the domain of machine guns and military assault rifles. Selective-fire weapons switch between the two modes; that switch is precisely what defines an assault rifle, and its absence is what defines a civilian sporting rifle. Handguns split into revolvers (rotating cylinder, single- or double-action, chambered in things like 9×18mm Makarov) and semi-auto pistols (hammer-fired DA/SA or modern striker-fired like Glock), with 9×19mm as the global defense standard.

The second axis is purpose, and it explains the shapes. Rifles branch into battle rifles (full-power 7.62×51mm, hard-hitting but punishing in auto), assault rifles (intermediate 5.56×45 or 7.62×39 — the infantry balance of firepower and control), DMRs ( accurized rifles reaching 800–1,000m), sniper rifles (bolt-action, .300 Win Mag to .338 Lapua), and carbines (shortened for close quarters, like the M4). Shotguns serve combat, sport, and field roles; SMGs like the MP40 fire pistol cartridges full-auto for close work; machine guns scale from LMG (M249, one soldier) through GPMG (MG3, bipod or tripod) to HMG (.50 BMG against vehicles and aircraft). The unifying lesson: no weapon excels at everything — every platform is a trade-off engineered for a specific job.

OBVIOUS CASE
The bolt-action hunting rifle

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYA firearm collection is a toolbox. The sniper rifle is a scalpel — one perfect cut at distance. The shotgun is a broom — wide, close, forgiving. The SMG is a staple gun — fast, shallow work. The battle rifle is a sledgehammer. You don't judge a broom for not being a scalpel; you pick the tool whose trade-offs match the job.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONGConflating semi-automatic with automatic. Semi = one shot per trigger pull, full stop. The related error is reading cosmetic features (pistol grip, flash hider) as functional ones — the classification that matters is written in the action and the chambering.
◎ WHY THIS MATTERSMatching tool to task is the difference between a good decision and an expensive mistake — and it's the vocabulary you'll need for legal definitions, which hinge on action type and precise measurements (Module 06 and the Debrief cover the numbers).
✦ MEMORY TECHNIQUEManual actions: "Boys Like Playing Baseball" — Bolt, Lever, Pump (slide), Break. Self-loading ladder: Semi (1 pull = 1 shot) → Select (a switch) → Full (hold and pour).

Module 05 · System Dynamics

Operating Systems — Harvesting the Explosion

A manual action runs on your arm. A self-loading action runs on physics — and the engineering question is always the same: which slice of the cartridge's energy do you steal to run beats 5 through 8 of the cycle? There are three families of answers, each matched to a band of cartridge power.

Blowback is the minimalist answer: fix the barrel in place and let the case push straight back against a heavy bolt. The bolt's inertia alone holds the breech closed long enough for the bullet to exit and pressure to collapse. It's beautifully simple — which is why it dominates pistol-caliber guns like basic 9×19mm designs — but mass is the only lock. Push it with a rifle cartridge and the bolt opens early: ruptured cases, "case head separation," danger. Delayed blowback buys a little room with levers and links. Recoil operation instead lets barrel and bolt recoil together, locked, then separates them with a cam. In short recoil (the Colt M1911) they travel a few millimeters before unlocking; in long recoil (the Browning Auto-5 shotgun) they ride the full stroke together — rarer, but effective in heavy recoiling systems.

Gas operation — the workhorse of modern rifles — taps the barrel itself: once the bullet passes a drilled port, high-pressure gas is diverted to do the cycling. In direct impingement (AR-15/M16) that gas travels down a tube straight into the bolt carrier: light and simple, but it vents hot, dirty combustion residue into the heart of the action, raising maintenance demands. In piston systems, gas drives a separate rod. A short-stroke piston (the AK-47, per this guide's taxonomy) gives the carrier one sharp shove and returns to rest — keeping the fouling forward and earning a reputation for shrugging off adverse conditions. A long-stroke piston (the HK416) rides with the carrier the whole cycle — smooth and famously reliable. The cartridge dictates the choice: the 7.62×39's short case shapes the AK's gas port placement and stroke, while the high-pressure 5.56×45 demands precisely calculated port dimensions. Nothing here is arbitrary — every system is a tuned answer to the physics of one specific cartridge.

Long Recoil

  • How: barrel and bolt stay locked across the whole rearward stroke, then separate — barrel springs forward first to eject/chamber.
  • Pro: tames very heavy recoiling loads smoothly.
  • Con: long, heavy moving assembly; uncommon in modern small arms.
Direct Impingement
EXAMPLE: AR-15 / M16 FAMILY
  • How: gas is ported down a tube directly into the bolt carrier key, shoving the carrier rearward.
  • Pro: light, few parts, minimal reciprocating mass → easy accuracy.
  • Con: hot carbon fouling is deposited inside the action; higher maintenance cadence.
Gas Piston
SHORT-STROKE: AK-47 (PER THIS GUIDE) · LONG-STROKE: HK416
  • How: gas drives a piston rod — either one sharp tap (short-stroke) or a full accompanying stroke (long-stroke).
  • Pro: fouling stays at the gas block; renowned reliability in dirt, sand, and neglect.
  • Con: heavier moving mass, more parts, a different (often sharper) recoil impulse.

Three Ways This Shows Up

OBVIOUS CASE
The AR-15's direct impingement

Gas is piped straight into the bolt carrier — no piston, no extra rod. The payoff is a light rifle with little reciprocating mass to disturb the sight picture, which is a big reason the platform shoots so well. The bill comes due in cleaning time.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE
The "dirtier" design is the cleaner gun

The AK's piston system vents fouling away from the action, while the more "sophisticated" DI rifle pipes combustion residue into its own heart. Simplicity and grime-tolerance beat elegance when the environment turns hostile — reliability is a design choice, not a bonus.

EDGE CASE
Blowback's hard ceiling

Blowback's only lock is bolt mass, so scaling it to rifle power means an impractically heavy bolt — or disaster. Push a light bolt with an over-hot load and it opens while pressure is still dangerous: case head separation, gas in the action, potential injury. Delayed-blowback lever systems stretch the limit a little, but the physics wall is real.

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYBlowback is holding a door shut with your body weight — fine against a child, hopeless against a linebacker. Short recoil is two people braced shoulder-to-shoulder who step apart after the first shove. Gas operation is plumbing some of the steam from your own kettle back around to whistle-open the next cycle.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG"Piston is always better than DI" (or vice versa). Each is a trade package — weight, fouling, recoil feel, parts count, cost. The informed position is knowing what you're trading, not wearing a team jersey.
◎ WHY THIS MATTERSThe operating system explains why firearms that look identical feel and maintain differently, why certain cartridges pair with certain platforms, and why your cleaning schedule depends on engineering decisions made decades ago.
✦ MEMORY TECHNIQUEThree energy sources, three words: MASS (blowback) · MOTION (recoil) · GAS (piston/DI). Then: "DI = Dirty Inside; Piston = Push-and-forget."

Module 06 · Standards & History

SAAMI, CIP, NATO — And The Cartridge That Shrunk

Ammunition doesn't exist in an anarchy — it's governed. In the United States, SAAMI (Sporting Arms and Ammunition Manufacturers' Institute) publishes the rulebook, mostly in imperial units: inches, grains, PSI. In Europe, CIP does the same job in millimeters, grams, and megapascals. Both enforce the same core idea: a tolerance stack-up where a maximum-size cartridge must still fit a minimum-size chamber, guaranteeing safe, reliable, interchangeable function. Same philosophy, different rulers — which means a SAAMI-spec round and a CIP-spec round are not automatically interchangeable, especially on pressure.

NATO plays a different game: not commerce but interoperability — the ability for allied armies to share ammunition in a crisis. Its Standardization Agreements (STANAGs) codify military cartridges: 9×19 under STANAG 4090, 5.56×45 under STANAG 4172, 7.62×51 under STANAG 2310 — each layered with harsh-environment and proof-testing demands beyond civilian specs. This is where the most dangerous beginner trap lives: .223 Remington and 5.56×45mm NATO are not the same cartridge. Externally near-twins, but 5.56 military ammo is proof-tested around 58,000–62,000 PSI against .223's ~55,000 PSI, and military chambers are cut longer to support thicker case heads. Firing 5.56 in a .223-only chamber can push pressure past the design margin — a catastrophic-failure scenario. The .308 Win / 7.62×51 NATO pair carries the same caution.

The why behind all this is a story. For half a century, armies carried full-power rifles (.30-06 class) whose reach vastly exceeded actual combat — WWII showed most firefights happened under 300 meters, and full-power recoil made automatic fire uncontrollable. Germany's Sturmgewehr 44 proved the fix: an intermediate cartridge, the short 7.92×33mm Kurz, in a select-fire rifle. The Soviets answered with the 7.62×39mm M43 and the AK-47, adopted in 1949. NATO first standardized on the full-power 7.62×51 amid debate, but America's M16 and its 5.56×45 round demonstrated that small, fast, controllable rounds won the volume-of-fire argument — and in 1980, STANAG 4172 made 5.56 NATO's second standard rifle cartridge. Today the 5.56×45 and 7.62×39 are the two most widely used rifle cartridges on Earth: metric, intermediate, and born from a tactical lesson learned in 1944.

Three Ways This Shows Up

OBVIOUS CASE
5.56 NATO vs. .223 Remington — the twin that isn't

Same silhouette, different contract. The military round runs higher pressure against a longer chamber; the civilian round assumes a tighter, shorter chamber. The pairing rule is directional: .223 in a 5.56 chamber is generally workable (with slight performance trade-offs); 5.56 in a .223-only chamber is a pressure gamble you don't take.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE
Armies deliberately made their rifle cartridges weaker

Post-WWII doctrine shrank the service round on purpose. Full-power reach was wasted below 300m, its recoil made auto fire useless, and its weight limited how much a soldier could carry. The "downgrade" to an intermediate cartridge was actually an upgrade in hits per engagement — the StG44 and AK-47 proved it.

EDGE CASE
7.62×54mmR — the rimmed fossil that refused to die

While NATO and the Warsaw Pact standardized on rimless metric cartridges, Russia's 1891-vintage rimmed design soldiered on in machine guns and sniper rifles for 130+ years — proof that logistics inertia and a generous rim can outlast "obsolete." Standards win wars, but legacy sometimes outlives them.

◈ VISUAL ANALOGYStandards bodies are electrical plug standards. SAAMI and CIP agree on the voltage philosophy but publish different plug shapes and rating labels; NATO is a shared power grid built so any ally's equipment can plug into any ally's supply line. Plugging a 5.56 round into a .223-only socket is like forcing a 240V appliance onto a 110V-rated circuit — the shape fits, the rating doesn't.
❌ WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG"They're basically the same, it'll be fine." The .223/5.56 and .308/7.62×51 pairs are the most repeated interchangeability errors in the world precisely because the dimensions almost agree. "Almost" is where over-pressure lives. The chamber stamping is the law; everything else is a rumor.

That's the difference between memorizing names and understanding the system.

Field Notes

FAQ & Next Steps

Is a bigger caliber always more powerful?
No. Diameter is only one variable — case capacity, powder charge, and bullet weight complete the equation. A tiny 5.7×28mm round at rifle velocity can out-penetrate a fat, slow pistol bullet, and gauge runs backwards to begin with. Power lives in the whole cartridge, not the first number of its name.
Can I shoot 5.56 NATO in my .223 rifle?
Treat it as a "no" until the manufacturer says otherwise. 5.56 runs higher proof pressure against a longer military chamber; a tight .223-only chamber can push it past safe margins. The reverse — .223 Rem in a 5.56 chamber — is generally acceptable, with a small accuracy/velocity trade-off. The chamber stamping is the authority.
Why isn't the .410 called a gauge?
Because it opted out: .410 is a straight decimal-inch bore measurement (10.4mm). If forced into the lead-balls-per-pound system it would equal roughly a 67-gauge — a number no maker stamps. It's the exception that proves the gauge rule.
What does "magnum" actually mean?
An oversized, higher-pressure version of a standard cartridge family — think .300 Win Mag (7.62×67mm) versus .308 Win (7.62×51mm). Many classic magnums are belted cases, the belt acting as a headspace shelf to contain the extra pressure. More case, more powder, more everything — including recoil.
Are SAAMI and CIP ammunition interchangeable?
Not automatically. The two bodies share the same tolerance philosophy but publish different units and, in some cartridges, different pressure ratings and dimensional details. Cross-border ammunition should be verified against your firearm's specification before use.
Assault rifle vs. semi-auto sporting rifle — the real difference?
Capability, not cosmetics. An assault rifle is selective-fire (semi and full/burst) on an intermediate cartridge. A civilian sporting rifle like an AR-15 is mechanically restricted to one round per trigger pull. The external similarities describe shared ergonomics, not shared function.
Why did armies shrink their rifle cartridges after WWII?
Battlefield data: most engagements occurred under 300 meters, making full-power reach wasted weight, and full-power recoil made automatic fire uncontrollable. The German StG44 and its short 7.92×33mm Kurz proved intermediate rounds delivered more practical hits — a lesson codified in the AK-47, the M16, and finally STANAG 4172 in 1980.
