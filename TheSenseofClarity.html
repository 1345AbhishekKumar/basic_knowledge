I

Chapter One · The Root Cause

The Curse of Knowledge

"If I had to identify the single biggest flaw in writing and communication, it would be that."— Steven Pinker

Read

Ask people why so much writing is bad and you get their favorite folk theory: it's deliberate. Academics with nothing to say dress up banal ideas in gobbledygook; bureaucrats build a cult of jargon to keep outsiders out; the nerds are getting revenge. Pinker's diagnosis is less flattering and far more useful. He invokes Hanlon's razor — never attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by stupidity — with a twist: these aren't stupid people. They're brilliant people suffering from a very specific blindness. They simply don't know where their audience is coming from.

The technical name is the curse of knowledge, a term borrowed from economics: the difficulty of knowing what it's like not to know something you know. You can't subtract your expertise from your own brain and see the page the way a stranger does. The result is the molecular biologist at TED — a room of hundreds from technology, entertainment, and design — who launched into the talk he'd give to fellow biologists and lost everyone within four seconds. Everyone in the room knew that no one was following. Everyone, that is, except the biologist.

The curse wears three costumes: acronyms nobody has been taught, jargon that only a clique shares, and abstraction so familiar to its author that they never bother to say what anyone should actually be seeing. "The level of the stimulus was proportional to the intensity of the reaction" is, translated, "the kids looked longer at the bunny than at the truck." Psychologists call the same failure egocentrism, or an absence of theory of mind. Pinker calls it the single biggest flaw in writing — and everything else in this lesson is a workaround for it.

Counter-intuitive

"The level of the stimulus was proportional to the intensity of the reaction" sounds sophisticated. It means kids looked longer at a bunny than a truck. Jargon can hide how simple a finding is.

Edge case

Grad students immersed in a lab of five or six people become unintelligible not just to the public but to faculty in their own department. The curse operates at the scale of a lunch table.

II

Chapter Two · The Hard Mode

The Message in a Bottle

"In writing you're wrenched from the context."— Steven Pinker

Read

Parents marvel at the twenty-month-old who won't stop talking. No one marvels at the eight-year-old's writing — it comes slowly, painfully, and without applause. That asymmetry is the scientific story: speech is a natural human endowment, bootstrapped by evolution; writing is a recent cultural invention that every generation must laboriously reinstall.

Why is the installed version so much harder? First, conversation never starts from zero. Two people who sit down to talk share common ground — why they're there, what's in the air, who "she" is — and that lets them get away with shortcuts like "this," "that," and "the thing." Writing is wrenched from that context. Your reader picks the book off a shelf in another country, possibly after you're dead, and everything they need must survive on the page alone.

Second, conversation runs a live feedback loop. A furrowed brow, a quizzical look, a muttered "wait, what?" — you repair the damage in real time, and even a lecture audience tells you, by fidgeting, when you've lost them. Writing gets none of that; the page never frowns back. So the writer must simulate both common ground and feedback from inside their own skull — which is exactly where the curse of knowledge lives. Hence Pinker's first practical rule: get into your reader's head, but don't trust that ability alone. Get a flesh-and-blood person to read the thing and tell you whether it makes sense. His mother — brilliant, well-read, but not a cognitive psychologist — was his first reader; his editors and friends in other fields are the rest of the net.

The Case File · three exhibits

Obvious

The 20-month-old vs. the 8-year-old

Toddlers chatter effortlessly and parents brag about it. Eight-year-olds labor over sentences and no one brags. Speech is natural; writing must be built.

Counter-intuitive

Efficiency that becomes opacity

The very shortcuts that make conversation efficient — "this," "that," "the thing," "she" — are what make writing opaque. They depend on a context the reader never had.

Edge case

Even a live audience talks back

A speaker feels a room fidget and drift, and adjusts. A writer's silence is total — the most feedback a page ever gives you is your own doubt at 2 a.m.

III

Chapter Three · The Antidote

Make Them See It

"No one can form an image of a paradigm in their mind's eye."— Steven Pinker

Read

Pinker's second rule follows from how brains work. Understanding isn't a string of words — it isn't "blah blah blah" — it's sensory: visual, motoric, bodily, emotional, auditory. Language is a means to an end, and the end is an idea you can see. So the writer's job is to keep handing the reader mental images: don't say "stimulus" when you mean bunny rabbit, and don't stack up "level," "perspective," "framework," "paradigm," "concept" — none of which anyone can picture in their mind's eye.

Here's a counter-intuitive corollary: the lush, vivid prose of earlier centuries wasn't a decorative choice. Writers before the age of academic abstraction had no canned terms to reach for, so they appealed to images everyone shared — "the spirit of the hawk entered into our flesh" instead of "aggression" or "antisocial behavior." Constraint produced the vividness. Jargon, to be fair, is real efficiency inside a profession: a biologist can't re-explain "concentration" in every paper, and after freshman year nobody should have to. The problem is the curse — the expert forgets that a two-syllable handle that feels basic to them is opaque to everyone else.

Children are the control group. Unburdened by decades of acquired abstraction, they reach for what they can see and what you can see: clouds are water vapor; smoke is fire vapor. It lands as poetry — a fresh juxtaposition — and it works as explanation. That double action, image plus freshness, is exactly what good writing is made of, and exactly what no thesaurus of abstractions can supply.

The Case File · three exhibits

Obvious

Counter-intuitive

"The spirit of the hawk entered into our flesh" — pre-modern writers sounded lush not by choice but because "aggression" didn't exist yet to lean on. Constraint produced the imagery.

Edge case

The child stylist

"Smoke is fire vapor." Pinker's grandson out-communicates most seminar talks, because a child has no jargon to fall back on — only things you can both see.

IV

Chapter Four · The Dance

Zip and Unpack

"Generalizations without examples and examples without generalizations are both useless."— Steven Pinker

Read

A generalization erases detail. Abstract words are too coarse to pin down a referent — English probably packs a hundred concepts into every word — so when Pinker reads a bare generalization, even in his own field, nine times out of ten he thinks: I don't know what you're talking about. Give me an example. The example pins the claim to a ballpark. But the reverse law holds too: examples without a generalization leave the reader asking why they were told this in the first place.

The useful frame is a trade-off between context and compression. Five hours of poetic detail about your train ride to Boston is all context and no handle; "an easy train ride" is all handle and no content. Good writing dances between the two — the generalization zips the idea up, the example unpacks it. Pinker demos it live: "Familiar words don't have to refer to the literal meanings of their parts" earns a polite nod — until the bathroom has no bath, breakfast breaks no fast, and Christmas has nothing to do with Christ's mass. Now you understand. You couldn't have before.

Richard Lederer's wordplay digs one level deeper. If adults commit adultery, do infants commit infantry? If olive oil is made from olives, what do they make baby oil from? — olive oil is oil from olives, but baby oil is oil for babies; there is no single logic inside compounds. If a vegetarian eats vegetables, what does a humanitarian consume? The "-er" in "finger" is a fossil of a rule that died centuries ago; the "-er" in "singer" is the same rule, alive and well, turning verbs into doers. Words are sedimentary — English is fifteen hundred years of dead processes stacked like rock — and examples are how you excavate them for a reader.

The Case File · three exhibits

Obvious

Bathroom · breakfast · Christmas

Counter-intuitive

Olive oil vs. baby oil

Identical grammar, opposite logic — one is oil from olives, the other oil for babies. There is no single rule for how compound parts relate.

Edge case

Finger vs. singer

The same suffix, one a living rule ("sing → singer"), one a 1,500-year-old fossil. English is a sediment of dead rules; dictionaries are the dig sites.

Chapter Five · The Right Brain's Share

The Music of Prose

"Omit needless words. Omit needless words. Omit needless words."— Professor Willard Strunk, as recalled by E.B. White

Read

The left brain has had its say; here is the right brain's share. Pinker reads his prose aloud — or at least mutters it — because if he stumbles, his reader's inner voice will stumble too. Prose has meter: not a metronome's tick-tock, but beats, and break them badly and even silent reading lurches. Too many sibilants in a row hisses; he'll pick a synonym to defuse the cluster. A touch of alliteration sparks a little pleasure — but make it conspicuous and it feels forced.

Then comes the razor. "Brevity is the soul of wit" is itself an example of itself: "soul" does a paragraph's work in one syllable, where we'd now say "the essential element." Strunk's first rule — "Omit needless words" — contains no needless words, and he repeated it three times because it is nearly the only rule. The mechanism is economic: every syllable is cognitive work for the reader, so the same message in fewer words is less labor and, simultaneously, more beautiful.

Constraint is a tool, not a punishment. When a newspaper caps you at 800 words and your draft runs 803, the editor chops three words you don't get to choose — so you learn to fit, and the squeeze, Pinker says, improves the prose "as if by magic," burning off woolly idiom and forcing concrete language. Comedians obey the same law at the extreme: pare the joke, shorter is funnier, and drag out the setup and you step on the punchline. Brevity isn't the soul of wit because it's polite. It's the mechanism.

VI

Chapter Six · The Modern Condition

The Yearbook Morph

"Its prose style is sound — that's the best you could say for it."— Steven Pinker, on large language models

Read

Two connected puzzles close the lesson. First: why does old prose sound so elevated even when written by non-writers — Rockefeller's inscriptions, Edison on electricity liberating women, Darwin, Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.? Three reasons: they were trained on the classics and had good models to draw on; in an age without telephone or radio, prose was how you presented yourself to the world, so you cultivated it the way you'd arrange yourself in a mirror; and they were exploring virgin territory with no stock of canned clichés, so they had to forge images and metaphors by hand.

Since then, a century-long process of informalization has run through everything — men hiking in ties, women in long dresses; Mr. and Mrs. giving way to first names (Pinker was sent to detention in third grade for exactly that, and still remembers the shame); taboo language woven into everyday speech. Democratization eroded the old hierarchies; authenticity and spontaneity became the virtues; carefully crafted prose now risks sounding pompous and distancing. The conversational vernacular won.

Second puzzle: LLMs are the ultimate cliché machine — a pastiche of billions of examples — and their prose is grammatical, orderly, plain… and unmistakably, benignly generic. Why even that much quality? Perhaps fine-tuning hammered it into five-paragraph shape with human hands. Perhaps — Pinker's speculation — it's the morphed-yearbook effect: average a hundred faces and the composite is prettier than any component; average a billion sentences and the composite is clearer than most humans'. But no, this doesn't show the mind is an LLM: a child would need 30,000 years of listening to match the input, and children are situated — reading intentions in a shared world, not predicting tokens. The machine averages what has been said. The child sees what is.

§ 4

Next Steps

Find your mother

Recruit one smart reader who is not of your field — the economist, the historian, the cousin who asks annoying questions. Every draft passes through them before it passes anywhere else.

Run the 800-word squeeze

Take a 1,000-word draft and force it into 800. Watch the prose improve "as if by magic" — the squeeze burns off woolly idiom and forces you to choose what actually matters.

Do a bunny audit

Highlight every abstract noun in your last piece — paradigm, framework, level, methodology. For each one, ask: what is the bunny? If there isn't one, the word may be doing no work at all.

Mumble your drafts

Read everything aloud at least once. The stumble you hear is the stumble your reader will feel — and you can fix it while the page is still warm.
