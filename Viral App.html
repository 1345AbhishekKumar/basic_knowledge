The Viral App Playbook

Nicole Chung took her first app to 1 million users in six months and her second to $250K MRR by month eight — with 99% organic growth. This lesson unpacks the whole system: pick the niche first, engineer the feature that markets itself, and turn creators into a repeatable distribution machine.

What you'll learn

Over the next six modules you'll rebuild Nicole's entire system from the ground up — the founder who reached a million users in half a year without buying a single ad. You'll learn how to pick a niche that does half the work before you write a line of code, how to design the business around the life you actually want, how to engineer features that market themselves, how to turn UGC creators into a repeatable machine, and how to prime and convert the traffic that machine produces.

Module 01

Niche before the idea

Ideas don't arrive from your own problems — they're shaped to fit a market you've already watched closely.

Here's a confession that should quietly rewrite how you hunt for ideas: Nicole Chung has never had an app idea arrive from her own problems. Not once. Her first two apps — Glam Up, the AI beauty scanner that hit a million users in six months, and Sprout, the "Tinder for jobs" that reached $250K MRR by month eight — both began the same way. She looked at the market, picked a niche worth attacking, and only then let the idea take shape. The order matters more than founders admit. Most first-timers start with "I have this problem" and end up building something nobody else cares about. Nicole starts with "Where is attention already flowing?"

She scrolls TikTok and Instagram looking for niches where people are passionate but the apps are weak. Her litmus test is concrete: if you can find a cooking app doing $50–100K MRR in a niche full of passionate cooking videos, that's a green light. She actively avoids saturated battlegrounds like health and fitness, because saturation makes truly new ideas nearly impossible to land. And crucially, she treats the niche as the constant and the idea as the variable. Glam Up pivoted its core feature repeatedly inside the same niche; Sprout began as an engineer's AI-interview experiment and pivoted to swipe-to-apply. The niche stayed; the features rotated until one caught fire.

That rotation has a rhythm she calls Day 1, Day 2, Day 3. Day one is not posting — it's being a student of the entire niche, market and competitor apps. Day two is Figma: not the onboarding flow, but the exact screen you want to go viral on social, drawn in several variations and borrowing design components from any app that shares your audience. Day three is distribution or a simple build. And she builds the app before the content goes viral — because if a video explodes on day one with no app to send people to, you've just lost ten thousand users to thin air.

But why does keeping the niche and swapping the feature work?

Because everything you own compounds at the niche level, not the feature level. Your audience research, your hook library, your creator relationships and your understanding of what the algorithm rewards in that corner of the internet all survive a pivot. Abandoning the niche throws that capital away; abandoning the feature just spends it on a better bet.

Process The Day 1 / 2 / 3 idea test

  1. Day 1 — be a student. Research the whole niche, the market and every competitor app. Don't post a single frame yet. Most of the apps Nicole built, she didn't truly understand the industry at first — so she gave herself time to learn it.

  2. Filter the niche. Passionate audience, low saturation, and proof that someone is already making $50–100K MRR there. Three green lights, or pick another pond.

  3. Day 2 — Figma the viral screen. Design the exact screen you want to spread on social, in several variations, borrowing design components from any app that shares your audience — even apps in unrelated niches.

  4. Day 3 — build or distribute. Ship a simple version first (onboarding and paywall can wait). Never let content go viral before the app exists, or those users vanish.

  5. Pivot inside the niche. If a feature flops after you've tried variations, keep the market and swap the feature. The niche is the asset.

Examples Three ways the rule shows up

Obvious

The cooking niche. Endless passionate cooking videos, very few cooking apps, and any cooking app clearing $50–100K MRR is a validated demand signal with weak competition. That gap between attention and supply is the whole opportunity.

Counter-intuitive

Avoiding health & fitness. Huge demand, but so saturated that genuinely new ideas rarely land. For an organic, bootstrapped founder, a smaller hungry niche beats a big crowded one — saturation is a wall, not a welcome mat.

Edge case

The pivot inside the niche. Glam Up's niche was right but v1 (text tutorials) flopped for three or four months across influencers, faceless accounts, Reddit and Discord. Instead of abandoning the niche they swapped the feature to visual before/after scans and went viral. Sprout did the same: from AI interviews to swipe-to-apply.

The concept

The analogy

A niche with passionate audiences and weak apps is a pond where the fish are hungry and the anglers are few. Your job isn't to invent fishing — it's to find the pond. And a food truck and a restaurant can both serve great food, but they fund differently, staff differently and give the owner different lives. Choose the vehicle before you write the menu.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: "Scratch your own itch" is the safest place to start.

Truth: Nicole's ideas never came from personal problems — they came from observing markets. The myth survives on survivorship bias: the founders whose personal problems happened to be markets.

Myth: A saturated market is a proven opportunity.

Truth: For organic-viral bootstrappers, saturation is a wall. Proven demand with weak competition is the signal; proven demand with strong competition is a fight you can't afford.

Pick the battlefield before you sharpen the sword — a good niche forgives a bad feature, but a bad niche kills a great one.

Module 02

Design the business first

The app is only a vehicle. Decide the team, the money and the margin — and the co-founder you trust — before you start the engine.

Before a single screen of Glam Up was designed, Nicole wishes someone had forced her to answer four questions: How do you want your days to look? How big a team do you actually want? Where is the money coming from? And what margin do you need? She learned the hard way — running different team sizes across different apps — that the shape of the business determines the shape of the app, not the other way around. Glam Up started on ten to twenty thousand dollars, total. Every dollar of growth was organic and reinvested; to this day roughly 99% of her growth comes from organic content. That constraint wasn't a handicap — it was the strategy. If you're bootstrapped you can't buy distribution, so virality becomes the only funnel that scales, and that single choice cascades into every later module of this lesson.

If you do have venture money, your menu is genuinely different: Meta ads, Twitter for B2B, in-real-life events, influencer collaborations paid partly in equity. The point isn't that one path is better — it's that the path you can afford decides which tactics are even on the table. Then the margin question, which almost nobody asks early: an AI chatbot app burns API cost on every session, while a tracker app barely costs anything to serve. Your target margin quietly decides which of those you can afford to build. And the cost split — what percentage goes to product versus distribution — decides whether you'll ever be found at all. Nicole started thinking about these only after the fact; she'd rather you think about them first.

Finally, the co-founder decision, which she treats as a relationship before a transaction. Her first co-founder went from a ten-minute phone call to a company — but only because years of friendship came first. Her current co-founder is deeply technical because she isn't, and the skills complement. But skills are the secondary reason. You will spend twelve hours a day with this person, every day. Trust, chemistry and loyalty are the make-or-break; everything skill-related is replaceable, and she's comfortable saying so. The ten-minute call only looked reckless because the trust had already been built in the hours of conversation that came before it.

Show more — the co-founder detail & copycat mindset

She met her first co-founder at a tech event and they talked for hours before any business existed. Her rule now: friendship first, complementary skills second, and a hard honesty that everyone is replaceable on skills — what can't be replaced is the trust and the enjoyment of building together. On copycats, her stance is "confident, not cocky": she studies apps doing far smaller numbers than hers and implements their learnings. Threat response is research, never fear.

Process Design the business before the app

  1. Team shape. Lean team of contractors, or a big org? Nicole's new apps run tiny; Steve prefers the lifestyle-business freedom of small. Small is a design choice, not a failure to scale.

  2. Funding mode. Bootstrap scrappy or VC speed? 99% of her growth is organic; Glam Up started on $10–20K. Money changes the channels, not the need to be scrappy.

  3. Margin target. AI chatbot costs versus simple-tracker costs. Your target margin decides which you can afford to serve at scale.

  4. Cost split. What percentage goes to product/API versus distribution? Decide it deliberately, or distribution quietly starves.

  5. Co-founder for trust. Friendship before business, complementary skills, the twelve-hours-a-day test. Skills are hired; chemistry is not.

Examples

Obvious

Lean + contractors. Nicole's new apps run on tiny teams of contractors; Steve keeps his teams small for freedom. A small team that fits your life is the point, not a stepping stone you failed to outgrow.

Counter-intuitive

Walking away from a hit. Glam Up was #3 in the App Store Lifestyle category, beating Tinder and Hinge for a few days — but it wasn't sticky and the looksmaxxing audience has brutal retention, so the team moved on to Sprout. Winning the charts doesn't mean the business fits your life.

Edge case

Anti-social apps market on social. Founders building apps to cure phone addiction still use TikTok to find users — or buy Meta ads, which Nicole flags as strong for that niche. The platform you're trying to help people escape is often the only road to distribution.

The concept

The analogy

You wouldn't pick a route and then hope any vehicle survives it. The business model is the vehicle — team, funding, margin — and the app is the route you drive once the vehicle is chosen. A van and a bus both reach the destination; only one lets you stop whenever you want.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: You need venture money to grow a consumer app.

Truth: 99% of Nicole's growth is organic; Glam Up started on $10–20K and everything was reinvested. Money changes the channels available — it doesn't remove the necessity of being scrappy.

Myth: Pick a co-founder for their skills.

Truth: Skills are replaceable; trust and chemistry are not. The ten-minute call only worked because the friendship preceded it.

The app is only a vehicle; the business model is the road — choose the road before you start the engine.

Module 03

Engineer the viral feature

The feature must be a visible transformation a stranger understands in three seconds — and you test it in content before you ever code it.

Glam Up's first version was, in Nicole's own words, brown and ugly: you scanned your face and got text-based makeup tutorials. For three or four months she tried everything to make it spread — influencers, faceless accounts, her own user-generated content, Reddit, Discord. Nothing stuck. The problem was never the niche; it was that text gives TikTok nothing to show. The pivot was brutally simple: make the result visual. Scan your face, see the before-and-after of a makeup look on your own skin. Suddenly the feature was the content — every video demonstrated the product just by existing. That is the first rule of engineering virality: the feature has to produce a visible transformation or reaction that a stranger can grasp in three seconds.

The second rule is to test the feature before you code it. On her current stealth app — already six or seven hundred thousand users in soft launch — new features are mocked in Figma or a content portal, handed to creators, and only built into the app if the content goes viral. You learn in days, not sprints. The third rule is to read the signal without mercy: a dud feature tells you within days, even after you've tried variations; a real viral feature outperforms everything else by roughly ten times and you know in the first week. The fourth rule — the one that separates professionals from lottery players — is the difference between viral content and a viral format. A format is repeatable: if five of ten creators can go viral with it, you own a machine. A one-off video is just a story you tell at parties.

Finding those formats is its own discipline. Nicole warms up her own TikTok and Instagram inside the niche so her For You page becomes a live feed of the latest niche content, then she ignores influencers entirely. An influencer's viral video is contaminated by their existing fans and baseline views. The signal she hunts is an account with under a hundred followers and two-hundred-to-a-thousand average views that suddenly has one video at a million — pure format, no tailwind. If a shadow-banned account can still push a video to a million views in your niche, the format is doing all the work. Steve watched this live: a creator ran the same hook and format for months and only broke through when the B-roll improved. Format first, production second.

But why test in Figma before building?

Because the content, not the code, is the experiment. A feature that won't film well won't spread, no matter how elegant the engineering. Mocking it in Figma lets you run the real test — does the format carry a video? — for the cost of a design file instead of a build cycle.

Process Engineer, then verify

  1. Start visual. The feature must produce a shareable before/after or reaction moment. If it can't be filmed in three seconds, it can't spread.

  2. Pre-test in Figma / content portal. Have creators film the feature before it's coded. Nicole's stealth app runs ~800 creators exactly this way.

  3. Test scientifically. Vary script length (10s / 25s / 30s), the hook, the on-screen text, the angle and the emotion. Emotion and shot changes protect watch time.

  4. Read the signal fast. Flops tell you in days; a real viral feature outperforms ~10× and you know in the first week.

  5. Separate format from content. A format is repeatable — 5 of 10 creators go viral with it. That repeatability is the asset.

Examples

Obvious

The before/after pivot. Text tutorials were dead for months; visual face scans went viral almost immediately, because the demonstration was the content. The feature stopped needing explanation.

Counter-intuitive

Research nobodies, not influencers. The signal is an account with under 100 followers and 200–1,000 average views that suddenly has one video at a million. That's pure format signal with no audience tailwind. Influencers' viral videos are contaminated by their existing fans.

Edge case

The shadow-banned account. If a penalised account can still push a video to a million views in your niche, the format is doing all the work — copy it. Steve's live example: same hook, same format for months, breakthrough only when the B-roll improved.

The concept

The analogy

A cookie cutter doesn't make one cookie — it makes a hundred identical ones. A viral format is the cutter; a viral video is just one cookie. Keep the cutter. A recipe you can cook again beats a dish that only worked once by accident.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: If one video went viral, I have a viral format.

Truth: A format earns its name when ~5 of 10 creators can repeat it. One video is content; repeatability is the asset.

Myth: Study big influencers to learn virality.

Truth: Their baseline views and loyal viewers hide what actually spread the video. Study the tiny accounts that broke through on format alone.

A viral feature isn't luck — it's a visible transformation, pre-tested in content, and read without mercy within a week.

Module 04

The UGC machine

The founder proves the format first. Creators are hired to perform a system you built — not to invent virality for you.

The single biggest misconception in this whole space is that you hire UGC creators and they make you viral. Nicole is blunt about it: creators are hired to make content, not to figure out virality — those are two very different jobs, and hoping a fifteen-dollar-per-post creator will crack the algorithm on your behalf is exactly how founders burn ten to twenty thousand dollars. The real order is the opposite. The founder goes viral first. When there was no money, Nicole and her co-founder filmed everything themselves. Only the moment a format proved repeatable did they hire to scale. That sequence — prove it yourself, then systematise it, then hand it off — is the spine of the machine.

Then the machine kicks in. Source micro-influencers with around ten thousand average views — people who can go viral but have no way to monetise, so they're hungry for paid work. Interview seriously; accept fifty percent or fewer, because you want people who take it seriously. Every creator goes through a filmed onboarding course, thirty to sixty-plus minutes, rebuilt for each app: app setup, account creation with exact username, bio and profile-picture examples, the three-day warm-up, then a content bank with ideas, sample videos, scripts, emotions, captions and hashtags. Nicole writes scripts like screenplays — an emotion for every line, long and medium and short shots, zooms — because watch time is the algorithm's currency and a flat talking head dies on it. Creators post from brand-new accounts warmed up to the niche, because a fresh account lets the algorithm categorise you cleanly.

Payment is base plus bonus: ten to thirty dollars per post with a five-hundred-view minimum to even qualify for the base, then tiered bonuses at ten, fifty thousand and a million views on a decreasing CPM. And every question gets an answer within a couple of hours — supported creators do more of what you want. The whole thing is systematised into courses and Notion docs so Nicole never repeats the same sentences twice. She films the sample videos herself and only swaps in a creator's version once that creator's video actually goes viral. The obsession is the point: the machine exists so that one founder's hard-won instinct becomes a playbook other people can run.

Show more — full warm-up rules & course modules

Warm-up (3 days): follow micro niche accounts, not macro ones — follow Messi and the algorithm sees followers from every nationality and interest and can't categorise you; follow a tiny niche account and it slots you cleanly. Scroll and comment on niche accounts ~30 minutes daily, don't switch accounts often, and pause the warm-up until after international travel. Course modules: (1) app intro + setup + feature codes, (2) account creation + warm-up specifics, (3) content creation from the content bank, (4) account management — ongoing warm-up, comment-section engagement, story posts. Support replies within hours so creators feel backed and do more.

Process Build the machine

  1. Go viral yourself first. Founder or co-founder films until one format pops. Only then hire to scale. No money? That's the feature — you become the first creator.

  2. Source. UGC platforms early; later, micro-influencers with ~10K average views plus inbound communities and friend referrals.

  3. Interview seriously. Accept ~50% or fewer. The foundation has to be people who take it seriously.

  4. Onboard with a filmed course. 30–60+ min: setup, account creation, warm-up specifics, content bank, account management.

  5. Warm up accounts 3 days. Follow micro niche accounts, scroll + comment daily, never switch accounts.

  6. Pay base + bonus. $10–30/post with a 500-view minimum; tiered bonuses on decreasing CPM.

  7. Support fast. Reply within hours. Systematise everything so you never repeat yourself.

Examples

Obvious

The industry-standard deal. Base $10–30/post with a 500-view minimum to qualify; bonuses kick in at 10K or 50K views. New college creators filming simple content take the low end; creators juggling five apps won't.

Counter-intuitive

Decreasing CPM bonuses. $50 at 10K views is $5 CPM; $50 at 50K is $1 CPM; $500 at 1M is 50¢ CPM. Top performers earn less per view — which keeps your unit economics alive exactly when a video explodes.

Edge case

Brand-new accounts beat established ones. A fresh account warmed up for three days lets the algorithm categorise you cleanly — which is why even experienced creators open new accounts per campaign, and why international travel pauses the warm-up.

The concept

The analogy

Nobody hands actors a blank stage and hopes for a hit film. The director owns the script, the shots and the emotion; the actors bring it to life on the mark. Founders write the screenplay of virality — creators perform it. Paying someone to also invent the screenplay is the expensive mistake.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: I'll pay the best creators and they'll make me viral.

Truth: Format discovery is hours and days of founder research; creators execute the system you built. The $10–20K "best creator" gamble is the classic trap.

Myth: UGC is film-and-forget.

Truth: Account management — comments, stories, ongoing warm-up — is part of the job, and the course teaches it. The creator's work doesn't end at upload.

Virality you can't explain you can't scale — the machine exists so the founder's obsession becomes other people's playbook.

Module 05

Onboarding that primes

For consumer apps, onboarding isn't data collection — it's a performance of personalization that makes a stranger feel understood before the paywall.

Most founders think onboarding is where you learn about your users. For a B2B SaaS tool, sure — understand them, then help them. For a consumer app that lives or dies on paid-or-viral conversion, Nicole flips the priority entirely: number one is priming the user to convert; number two — a distant second — is understanding them. The mechanics are almost theatrical. Ten or more pages is normal; some apps run twenty or thirty. Questions like "What's your beauty goal?" or "What's your top skin concern?" aren't really data collection — they're a performance of personalisation. The user feels understood, and an understood user converts. The data is barely used; the feeling is the product.

Then comes the trick Nicole loves: insert social proof between the answers, keyed to what the user just said. They answer "acne" and the very next screen says "80% of users with acne chose this plan." Her newest app asks for location and immediately shows how many users are in that city. Steve felt the same psychology in a fitness app that asked, after his goal, "How confident are you that you'll gain muscle by October 1st?" — the specific date did something to him that a vague "how committed are you?" never could. None of this works if the copy is heavy. In the Pet Me audit, the onboarding was a wall of clustered text, a strange demand for a birthday, and a share prompt that pulled users out of the app before they ever saw the paywall — three different ways to leak a funnel you paid the machine to fill.

The research method behind good onboarding is brute force, not theory. Nicole has downloaded around a hundred apps and studied their flows; analyse twenty top converters and clear patterns emerge, including what the laggards are doing wrong. The rule is to learn only from the apps that are already converting — Twitter can describe onboarding, but you only see the patterns by going through the screens yourself. And the cardinal sin is asking too much too soon: never redirect a user away from the app (a share prompt, a message) before the paywall, because the entire point of onboarding is to keep them on the path to payment.

But why does "feeling understood" actually convert?

Commitment and consistency. Once a user has declared a goal out loud — "acne", "build muscle by October" — paying for a tool that serves that exact goal feels like keeping a promise they made to themselves. The questions aren't gathering intelligence; they're getting the user to invest in their own outcome before the price appears.

Show more — the Pet Me audit, in detail

Pet Me led with a dense "I'm a pet parent" text screen (drop-off risk), asked for a first name and a birthday that felt irrelevant, then asked users to share the app — redirecting them out of the funnel before any value. It also never asked about the pet (name, photo, birthday) the way other pet apps do, and the main screen looked blank. The fix: lead with the cute Tinder-style pet cards, put social proof on the text-heavy pages, ask for nothing that pulls users away early, and focus on one feature instead of social + booking + services at once. Delete features until one sings.

Process Build onboarding that primes

  1. Reframe the goal. Prime conversion first; learn about users second. (B2B flips this.)

  2. Go long. 10+ pages is normal; some apps run 20–30. The failure mode is heavy, not long.

  3. Ask identity-level questions. Goals, concerns, struggles — so the app feels tailored.

  4. Insert social proof between answers. "80% of users with your concern…", "1,204 people in your city" — keyed to the previous answer.

  5. Anchor commitment with specifics. A date ("by October 1st?") beats a vague "how committed?"

  6. Never redirect early. No share prompts before the paywall; keep text light.

  7. Study by brute force. Download ~100 apps, deeply analyse 20 top converters, copy patterns not laggards.

Examples

Obvious

Glam Up's beauty questions. Goals, skin concerns, struggles, product types. The data is barely used; the feeling of being understood is the product.

Counter-intuitive

The questions aren't for you — they're for them. "80% of users with your concern…" takes the previous answer and reflects it back as social proof. You're not gathering intelligence; you're staging credibility.

Edge case

Date-anchored commitment & the share trap. "How confident are you by October 1st?" and "1,204 users in Austin" compress vague interest into a personal, time-bound stake. The anti-example: Pet Me asking users to share before they'd seen any value — never redirect away from the funnel.

The concept

The analogy

A tailor asks about your wedding, your posture, your preferences — not because the answers change the cloth, but because the fitting makes the suit feel like yours. Onboarding is the fitting. The questions exist so that when the price appears, the user is already wearing the idea of the product.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: Onboarding is for collecting user data.

Truth: In consumer apps, conversion priming comes first; the questions exist so the user feels the app was built for them. Data collection is a side effect.

Myth: Keep onboarding short.

Truth: 10+ pages converts routinely. The failure mode is boring or heavy copy — not length.

Every view the machine buys is wasted if the first ten screens don't make a stranger feel recognized.

Module 06

Paywall, pricing & the retention truth

Hard wall for single-player apps, price matched to retention, tested forever — and a cold acceptance of how much retention you can actually move.

Once a primed user finishes onboarding, Nicole's default for single-player apps is a hard paywall: no payment, no product. Two-player or social apps can go premium or free — the network is the moat there — but for a tool that serves one person, the hard wall maximises revenue per download and lifetime value. Sprout proved the nuance inside that rule: users could browse jobs freely, but a right swipe — the application itself — sat behind the wall. They even A/B tested whether the jobs were visible before payment. Her newest app is harder still: you can't use it at all until you pay. The principle is to gate the action that delivers value, and to test exactly where that line sits.

Pricing follows retention. A looksmaxxing app that users churn through in a few weeks should sell weekly and monthly; stickier products can push yearly, and the whole market is drifting that way — $39.99 a year is the standard anchor, with some apps charging $89.99. The cardinal rule is to test constantly. Nicole's newest app retested pricing two months after the previous test, because distribution shifts and App Store ratings move perceived value — your old price is only valid for your old situation. And here is the counter-intuitive part: raising prices by twenty to forty percent sometimes improves conversion. Steve watched $9.99 a week beat $6.99 because it nudged users toward a $50 year. A ten-dollar-a-year app quietly tells users it isn't worth much; price is a value signal, and a weak signal repels.

Finally, the coldest truth in the whole conversation: retention is roughly ninety percent determined by niche and industry. Glam Up's audience — looksmaxxing — has some of the worst retention in consumer apps, which is precisely why the team moved on to Sprout. You can polish retention with tactics, but you cannot rescue an idea that isn't naturally sticky. In Nicole's words, tactics can improve retention, but they can't replace an idea that isn't inherently retentive. That sentence closes the loop back to module one: retention starts with the niche you chose before you wrote a line of code. The market, as always, gets the first and the last word.

Show more — big swings, screenshots & word of mouth

Nicole prioritises big swings over small ones — App Store screenshots keep getting pushed down her priority list until nothing bigger remains, because conversion rate depends on the traffic source: pre-sold viral traffic converts high even with weak screenshots, while organic search traffic needs the screenshots to do the selling. The product itself is a pricing lever too — on her newest app, family-and-friends word of mouth converts roughly twice as well as cold traffic, which is her evidence that a genuinely good product pays for itself. And in the Shop Maker audit, the advice was the opposite of discounting: a $1.99/month paywall with zero subscribers wasn't a price problem, it was a paywall-design problem — simplify the tiers, borrow the onboarding's animations, and raise the price to $50–70 a year.

Process Set the wall and the price

  1. Single-player → hard paywall. Maximise revenue per download / LTV. Social / two-player → premium or free.

  2. Gate the action, not the browse. Sprout: see jobs, pay to swipe/apply — and they tested the visibility line.

  3. Price to niche retention. Low retention → weekly + monthly; otherwise push yearly ($39.99 standard, up to $89.99).

  4. A/B test constantly. Retest every few months — distribution and ratings shift perceived value.

  5. Don't fear raising prices. +20–40% can improve conversion. Price is a value signal.

  6. Accept retention's ceiling. ~90% is set by niche; tactics polish, they don't rescue.

Examples

Obvious

Hard paywall for single-player. Nicole's newest app doesn't let you use it at all until you pay; Sprout gates the swipe, not the browse. The wall sits exactly where value is delivered.

Counter-intuitive

Raise prices when nobody's buying. Seth's headshot app had zero subscribers at $1.99/month; the fix wasn't a discount — it was a better paywall and a higher price, because price is the value signal. Nicole has seen +20–40% raises improve conversion across her own apps.

Edge case

Retention is set before you ship. Weekly pricing exists because looksmaxxing users leave in weeks; and word-of-mouth users (family and friends) convert ~2× better than cold traffic — product quality is itself a pricing lever. Also: retest every few months, because your old price is only valid for your old distribution.

The concept

The analogy

A $10 tasting menu makes you suspicious; a $90 chef's menu makes you sit up straighter. The velvet rope doesn't change what's inside — it changes what you believe is inside. Price is the first sentence of the story about value, and a discount is a sentence that apologises for the product.

❌ What people get wrong

Myth: No subscribers means my price is too high.

Truth: Usually the paywall is too weak — simplify the tiers, show value, and often raise the price. A low price reads as low quality.

Myth: I'll fix retention with streaks and push notifications.

Truth: ~90% of retention is the niche. Tactics polish a sticky idea; they don't rescue an inherently un-sticky one.

Price is the story you tell about value, and retention is the story the niche already wrote — know which one you're allowed to edit.

Explain it like you mean it

In your own words: why should a founder go viral themselves before hiring UGC creators? Type it out, then check which key ideas you covered.

Because creators are hired to perform content, not to invent virality. When the founder cracks a format first, they know it's repeatable — then the course, the scripts and the bonus structure turn that one proof into a system many creators can run. Hiring first means paying strangers to gamble with your budget.

Golden rules

  1. The niche is the constant; features are the variables.
  2. Design the life, then the business, then the app.
  3. The feature must be visible in three seconds.
  4. The founder proves the format; creators scale it.
  5. Onboarding primes; the paywall harvests.
  6. Price signals value; retention is the niche's verdict.

Right niche × visible feature × founder-proven format × systematised creators × priming onboarding × hard paywall = repeatable virality.

Still confused?

Do I have to be on camera?

Nicole did everything herself when there was no money, but if you genuinely hate being on camera, hire for it — just accept that's the consequence. Either way, the format research stays yours; you can't outsource the thinking that decides what gets filmed.

What if I have venture money?

Your channels widen: Meta ads, Twitter for B2B, in-real-life events, influencer collaborations with equity. But the niche filter and the format-testing discipline still apply. Money changes speed, not physics — a bad niche funded by VC is still a bad niche.

How many ideas should I test at once?

Not a hundred. One niche, a small set of features. Parallel ideas scramble your learning — you won't know which variable caused the result. Nicole tests a few features inside one niche, not many niches at once.

Does this work outside TikTok and Instagram?

The principles travel — niche proof, format repeatability, priming onboarding, hard paywalls. The tactics shift. Reddit carried Nicole's Etsy shop to #1 top seller; the warm-up logic and the "study the small accounts" rule are platform-agnostic.

What about copycats?

Confident, not cocky. Nicole studies apps doing far smaller numbers than hers and implements their learnings; she doesn't feel threatened, she feels curious. The response to competition is research, never fear.

How fast will I know if a feature works?

Within the first week for a winner. A dud reveals itself after you've tried variations across a few methods — Glam Up took three to four months of honest attempts before the pivot. The point isn't speed, it's reading the signal without denial.
