Part I

The framing

01

Disruption moves fast, adaptation is slow

"If you get nothing from this video except this one tip, you're going to be much better off."

This is the frame for everything else. When you're early on, you might be on track. But if you're not keeping up with trends, new capabilities, and new approaches to work, you slowly diverge from the proper path.

Disruption curve Your adaptation curve The gap you fall into

This is an extremely common problem for people deeper in their career. "I have 10 years of experience. 15 years. 30 years." But it's not the experience that actually matters today.

Why a senior engineer can lose to an entry-level engineer: if the entry-level engineer focused the majority of their effort on the thing that's disrupting the industry, they have the skill the role is actually hiring for. You may have a higher total skill level. They have the right skill.

What to do about it

  • If you're very experienced: you just need to spend a little extra time to keep up with anything new.
  • If you're brand new: use it as an advantage by focusing your effort on what's currently in demand.
→ You can't control how fast the industry moves. You can control how fast you adapt — and that's the whole game.
02

30 years of the wrong experience

"It's 30 years of the incorrect experience, or just the same experience over and over again."

The trap: "I have 30 years of experience." But that can quietly mean 30 years of the incorrect experience — or worse, the same one year repeated 30 times.

The correction

  • Focus on what's valuable right now.
  • Focus on what companies are looking for.
  • Focus on what you need to be relevant in the industry.
  • Don't get stuck on "we've always done it this way, so this is the correct way."
And the flip side: "innovation is very quick, things adapt fast, but people and systems lag behind. So what's taught in university right now is probably not what you need to know right now to get a job."
→ Years are not a credential. Relevant years are.
03

Don't split fundamentals from AI

"You're asking me what I would do if I was in your situation, and my answer is that I would focus on the fundamentals and AI-enhanced development and learning."

The conversation he keeps having

Someone new to the industry emails: "What can I do to learn as fast as I possibly can, and get as good as I possibly can?"

He answers: "Utilize AI agents to help enhance your progress."

And they say: "Whoa, excuse me — I don't want to be using AI because that's going to slow down my learning. I've just been doing everything solo, learning the old school patterns of work."

The problem isn't AI. It's the approach. The belief that using AI is somehow going to destroy your foundation. That's a belief about how you use it, not a fact about the tool.

Interactive · The three modes of using AI

Use when: you're learning a fundamental, or the logic is critical
You write everything by hand

No agent in the loop. You type the loops, you fight the syntax, you hit the bugs. This is the slowest mode — and the only one that builds muscle memory for the fundamentals.

The pain is the point. If you never struggle through it, you never build the intuition that tells you when the AI's output is subtly wrong.

Use when: you're building something real and the design is settled
You direct, the agent writes

You describe the interface, the behavior, the constraints. The agent produces the implementation. This is where the leverage lives once you actually know what you're doing.

The failure mode here is being a "vibe coder" — accepting what comes out without understanding it. You avoid that by having done mode 1 first.

Use when: you're learning something new or want to go deeper
The agent teaches, you write

The agent doesn't touch your code at all. It explains, quizzes you, generates examples, gives you tests to pass, reviews your notes for flaws, and shows you the space of possible approaches.

This is the mode people forget. It's how you go from "the AI wrote it and I don't know why" to actually understanding the thing.

Why "I won't touch AI until I'm senior" makes zero sense

  • You're forcing yourself into the structure of people who've been in the industry 20+ years.
  • Those people are failing to adapt to the workflows others are using successfully.
  • You're adopting their bottleneck before you've even started.

What to actually do — both

  • Yes, know how to code.
  • Absolutely, understand all the fundamentals — start there.
  • Pair it with AI learning and the use of agents.
  • Use agents to improve the quality of your software and your system design, architecture, bugs, and security.
His own data point: using agents pretty much every day for the last 6 months, the amount of learning he's gone through is comparable to probably a couple of years studying on his own.
→ It'll make your software better, not worse. Don't split these ideas — do both.

Part II

How to learn

04

Find the person. Collapse time.

"I literally would try to find somebody who has more experience than me in whatever it is I'm trying to accomplish on day one."

When he started, he was on his own — wasting time on YouTube, searching the internet. There was no AI back then. Today he uses AI to guide his learning, but for anything important — architectural decisions especially — he checks with people more experienced than him.

Because there's something AI and solo practice cannot replace: the experience that takes years and years to develop.

The mechanism: withdraw from the skills of others

If you're trying to do something beyond your skill set — which is important for learning — the best way is to draw on the skills of others.

If you can't find that person

  • First off, you probably haven't tried very hard.
  • Go network on LinkedIn.
  • Reddit, social media, your existing network.
  • Peer-to-peer communication is still the biggest value you can get for growing your skill, your network, and getting opportunities.
An uncomfortable truth: "I know for some of you it stinks to hear that because you can't just stay behind your computer and just code." But getting experience from other people is going to help a ton.

How to actually reach people

It doesn't have to be complicated. Something he does:

  • Read a book by someone.
  • Message that author on LinkedIn: "Hey, I read your book. Good stuff."
  • A few authors of popular books have responded. Yes, some were romance novelists with nothing to do with code — but the mechanism works.

The pair programming story

At his first job, he started pair programming with a guy who was way better than him. The guy irritated him — every time he tried to do something, "No, rewrite it from scratch. You need to do it this way."

"Dude, I just want to get this app built. Every time I talk to you, you're making me redo stuff and adding all these rules and making it complicated."

Looking back, that was the most helpful time in his journey — because he actually learned why they were doing certain things.
→ You can't reproduce 30 years of experience on your own in a weekend. Go borrow it.
05

Coins vs the treasure chest

"They're too busy distracted by this to go open the treasure chest."

🎁
You spent all that time for 12 cents.

The treasure chest was there the whole time. Two hours with someone experienced beats two hundred hours of tutorial hopping — same total "content," wildly different value.

The coin-collecting trap

Tutorial hopping gives you little coins. You watch a resource, ask an AI tool how to do something, and you stack up little skills over time. That's what you get.

"However, this is going to take you forever to become a millionaire in terms of skill set."

The treasure chest right in front of you

There are much higher-value opportunities for learning and growth that people just choose to ignore. They pick up all the coins, too distracted, and never open the chest.

The honest accounting: back in his early career he spent countless hours watching stuff on YouTube and learned the same amount in terms of value from a couple of things said by someone with more experience. Less total content. Less total findings. Far more value.

Where the real value is now

  • Engineering blogs.
  • Podcasts you're genuinely interested in.
  • Content pieces that talk about applied experiences instead of tutorials.
"The basic tutorial I believe is coming to an end." Most of what we'd once look up on YouTube, we can ask an LLM to explain with examples. Even he acknowledges this might hurt his own channel.

His actual examples

  • Zero Knowledge Podcast (cryptography) — every guest was actually doing something with cryptography. He learned how they thought through things, their discoveries, their problems — and what wasn't being talked about.
  • Discord's article on their database structure and NoSQL approach — he could see why they actually needed this database, which is the biggest challenge when starting out: why choose one approach over another.
The real skill: there are all these options and approaches — why would you choose one over the other? That's the true skill, and it's very hard to learn on your own as a beginner.
→ Applied experience from someone who has been there is not reproducible by AI. It's talking about specific things ChatGPT has never experienced.
06

Learn experience AND adapt fast

"These are kind of like two sides of the same coin."

This seems almost contradictory to lesson 02 — don't get stuck on "we've always done it this way." But they're not in conflict. They're two points of focus working toward the same goal.

The two halves

  • Learn from experience: the 30 years you can't reproduce. Draw on people who've been through it. Learn why things are done. Understand the real-world consequences of decisions.
  • Learn what's new: trends, optimizations, workflows. Keep up with new capabilities so you can be even better than the people who came before.

In practice

  • Learn from experienced people.
  • Then learn what's new and trending to find optimizations and workflows.
  • Don't treat these as conflicting ideas — treat them as a single mindset for learning.
→ Borrow the judgment of the past, and apply the tools of the present.
07

General learning + specialization

"We want to cover a large basis of stuff to get a good broad understanding. And then we build on top of this our skyscraper."

Interactive · Skill shape simulator

There's still unlimited things to focus on — which is why roadmap videos do so well. People need the bigger picture. But regardless of the bigger picture, there are two layers:

  • Layer 1 — General: broad, wide foundation covering many topics.
  • Layer 2 — Specialization: very good at very few things. "Broad" here means something like back-end web development — not some super-specific niche that takes 20 years.
"And I lied. This is actually a top hat." The broad base plus a focused top — not a thin tower on a thin base.

Why spreading thin fails

If you spread yourself thin becoming good at a bunch of different things, you're effectively becoming average at a bunch of stuff and great at nothing. That's a fine approach for the foundational layer — but it limits you when it comes to providing value in a way other people cannot. "You're not good enough at anything to do anything special."

The two problems people hit

  • They don't really know what to specialize in.
  • They topic hop from one thing to the next, never really becoming good at anything.

What to do instead

  • Follow end-to-end flows — point A to point B — and don't get distracted off the path.
  • This is why he's a fan of courses: one lesson after the other, each building on what you just learned.
  • When learning a new topic, find a crash course and watch it end to end.
The React example: he watched a ~4-hour React course in a single sitting. Did he know how to build everything in React after? No. But he had practice going through the lessons, and a video to anchor back to. As a beginner to the topic, it worked — because he could go deeper, stacking knowledge on what he'd just learned.
→ A wide base makes you capable. A deep top makes you valuable.
08

Go deep instead of hopping

"I didn't challenge myself. It felt like learning, but it wasn't really learning because it wasn't painful enough."

What he actually did when starting

  • One day studying jQuery. The next, Angular. Then AppScript automations — something totally off the wall.
  • Basically learned whatever he felt like learning that day.
  • The efficiency of this was trash. He wasted a lot of time.
  • Any topic he started, he'd study briefly and jump to something new.
  • He'd reach the point of stuff he already knew in previous languages — and go no deeper.

What "going deep" actually looks like

Starting at loops, then functions, then generators, then object-oriented programming, then web development, then how async code works — and becoming better as you go deeper.

Where he got stuck: somewhere around web development — then switched to something else and tried again in a different programming language. His skills stayed flat.

Learning is supposed to hurt a little

"Learning is actually painful." If you're learning something and you think "oh, this is hard, I don't really understand" — that's a good thing, unless it's way too difficult.
And the inverse: if you walk away thinking "that was super easy" without any concrete things you learned, it feels like learning, but you're really just exercising your brain on stuff you already know.

For him, deep learning has always been a painful experience: a lot of focus, a lot of cross-referencing, a lot of note-taking. He also has a passive mode — podcasts, entertainment content, experience over time — but deep learning is more painful than fun.

Message to companies and recruiters: if you find a developer who's really good but hasn't worked with your very specific software that nobody cares about — that doesn't matter. If they're a good software developer, they can pick it up.
→ You want to go deep with something so you become better in general — and that general quality translates to other things much more easily.
09

Learn the diff, not the duplicate

"You don't need to know everything twice. You only need to know something once, and then to learn the second thing it's just a diff between these two."

The trap

You learn loops in Python. Then you go to TypeScript and learn loops there. There's a little syntax difference. You spend time on it. It feels productive.

But you didn't learn anything new. You just learned how to translate it from one language to another.

This is a low-value skill. You should be able to pick it up as needed when you pick up a new language — by reading the original implementation, reading docs, or using AI tools to translate.

The better framing

Instead of learning the same thing across two languages, learn how the two languages differ. This encapsulates the skill of translation.

Applied

  • Learn object-oriented programming in Python. Then learn how OOP works in JavaScript. That's the diff.
  • Similar for other languages.
  • Languages that focus on traits or interfaces, or that take a different approach by default — those are the interesting differences.

The school story

He had a Java class and had never used Java — but he had used C, which is basically the same thing from two different companies (an overgeneralization, but close). So he wrote his code in C and then "Javaified" it, making very minor translations.

And it applies to programming languages generally: "I don't really care what you learn. JavaScript or TypeScript. Python. Anything else. Just pick one to start with and learn everything you can, not getting distracted."
→ One deep implementation plus a diff beats two shallow implementations. Every time.

Part III

AI as a learning multiplier

10

Pick one AI tool and go deep

"Yeah, I'm an AI power user — which, by the way, I've never written a Claude Code skill or learned how to create my own sub-agents. Like, basic stuff."

The surface-level trap, applied to AI

The same failure mode as lesson 09, now in a new domain:

  • Some experience with ChatGPT or Codex.
  • Some experience with Claude, Claude Code.
  • Maybe Cursor, Antigravity, any of these tools.
  • You end up with a broad skill set across many platforms — and you never go deep on any one of them.
So you call yourself an AI power user without ever having written a skill, created a sub-agent, or done basic deeper work — because you went surface level across multiple platforms.

What he did instead

"Yo, I'm just going to pick Claude Code and get really good at that."

Why this works: it translates to other tools very easily, because all the other frameworks have similar patterns. You just have to translate and get the diff between the two.

The forcing function he used

  • "I'm going to build an app."
  • "Everything I can do with AI, I'm going to figure out how to do it."
  • But not in a sloppy way — figure out how to produce high-quality results.
  • Continually go deeper with your understanding of agentic AI, LLMs, and the tools.
→ Breadth across AI tools is not a skill. Depth in one is — and it ports to the rest.
11

Build a knowledge vault

"Basically going to be reference for you and the AI."

The very first thing he'd do — for studying or for a project — is create a knowledge vault.

How it works

  • A folder full of markdown files.
  • If it's a project: all the information about that project goes in.
  • If it's studying a topic: your notes go in.
  • Have the AI help you structure your thoughts.
  • Have it correct anything incorrect.
  • Result: you organize and enhance the workflow faster.
It serves double duty. It's reference for you and for the AI. That's the point.
→ Notes you write once, structure with AI, and both of you can read forever.
12

Write your own skills

"What is a skill? It's basically just a prompt you give an LLM, but you can reuse it at any point."

You describe what you want the AI to do, and then you can invoke it like a function.

Examples he'd build

  • Research — a reusable research prompt.
  • Code translations — how might this look in other programming languages? Specifically, how are they different?
  • Design alternatives — the one he calls the most valuable here.

Why the alternatives skill is the most valuable

You craft the skill to find different approaches in design, or different patterns people have used to teach the same thing — conquering the same topic from different angles.

Because the biggest challenge in modern learning is siloed concepts — not understanding how something translates to the real world and to other things that are similar to it.

The OOP example

If you're studying object-oriented programming, you might not even know that:

  • Some languages are functionally organized — functional languages, or functional patterns that can replace OOP design patterns.
  • Some languages are trait-based — no formal classes and instances, instead types that adhere to certain traits or interfaces.
  • Some languages have multiple inheritance while others don't.

You might be thinking "this is the way it's always done" — when in reality other people are doing it a completely different way.

Have the skill challenge the whole category. Do we even need object-oriented programming? Are there other architecture types we could use even in a language that has OOP? Some languages force it (Java); in Python you don't have to follow a strict OOP design pattern.
→ These enhancements would set you apart from other beginners far more than refusing to use AI at all — because those people are failing to see so much of the bigger picture.
13

Make AI prove things, not just state them

"Stop thinking of AI as the enemy to learning and instead think of it as an enhancement that can help you learn more faster."

The flaw he sees in his own learning

"I'll read something and I'll assume it's fact because it's stated as fact."

It's why his videos showcase examples that prove the fact, rather than just stating it. Rounding, for instance — he'll show an example.

What AI changes

  • Have it generate examples to show things that would typically be glossed over.
  • Reading docs that say "this is what happens on an overflow" — you could just trust it and move on.
  • Or you could have AI generate a quick example you can actually run and see in action.
Running an example embeds it deeper. Before, it was faster to just trust and move on — but it doesn't go as deep in your mind.

With AI you can ask:

  • Real-world examples of when you'd choose each approach.
  • Alternative approaches you haven't considered.
  • Code samples that actually produce each behavior.
  • Should we use light threads managed by the runtime? OS-managed threads? async/await?
  • Pros and cons of each.
The point: there are a million ways to do this kind of thing and it can be overwhelming. AI shows you the possible ways, the trade-offs, and not just one isolated solution.

He does this in app development too

Building out a front end, he doesn't just do one thing at a time. He fans out a bunch of different agents to give him different examples, so he can see outside the scope of what he's currently looking at. Introducing more randomness to get new ideas.

Other ways to use AI for learning

  • Give you examples of the actual implementation.
  • Quiz you with questions and answers.
  • Write software tests that you have to implement code to pass.
  • Review your notes and find flaws in your understanding.
→ The isolated solution is what tutorials give you. The space of possible solutions is what actually builds judgment.

Part IV

Build real things

14

Build something real, and keep building it

"Don't let these projects be basic things that are totally useless."

Start building stuff as soon as possible — not just through work, but your own personal projects.

What not to do

  • Maybe start with a calculator app or a to-do list just to get familiar with the language — fine, to start.
  • But then build something that's actually pretty cool, that takes a lot of time, effort, and thought.
  • And keep working on the same apps over time.
His biggest downfall when starting: he'd tinker with something for a day, get bored or not know what to do with it, and move on to something else. It took a long time to start building anything meaningful.

The project that changed it

He decided: "I'm just going to build a cryptocurrency from scratch."

Something ongoing, one thing at a time. He had to figure out every detail:

  • How does a wallet work?
  • How does the encryption work?
  • How can we send a transaction?
  • How could we interact with it through the terminal and build out a CLI?

He built it in Rust — as a way to learn that language, and to get experience in something he thought he might want as a job. Some kind of protocol engineering role.

What actually happened: a company reached out wanting a Rust developer. Not in crypto or blockchain. But he could show his knowledge of the language and that he was able to build something more complex than an afternoon project. It likely helped him get the role — the last software engineering role he had as a senior engineer.

He got the job offer within about a week of starting to build it, dropped the project, and moved on. "It was a very valuable journey for me."

What he'd change: he'd do that much sooner. But he probably wouldn't pick such a challenging topic in a relatively challenging language. He'd pick something with a medium level of difficulty that challenges you every day and helps you discover all the other things you have to concern yourself with when building software.
→ One ongoing, medium-hard project teaches you more than twenty abandoned toys.
15

What building your own software actually gives you

"AI can build out basic CRUD apps … but what it has a hard time doing is learning how to piece all of that stuff together to build a valuable product."

Real, provable experience

The "provable" part is a little less true now that AI can generate apps — but even if you're using agents, you directing the agents to build something helps you build a stronger product mindset.

Product mindset

  • What features do we actually want?
  • What features do we actually need?
  • What is the cost of implementing these features?
  • What things are people actually going to pay for?
You start thinking about these things when you're building your own software versus just learning how to code. You're now learning how to use that code for practical stuff. That's what companies will care about more.

The things AI can't learn for you

  • Real data. GDPR and legal concerns, where data is located for latency, live migrations, protecting data in production while adapting the structure over time.
  • Customers. Actual people with actual needs and actual complaints.
  • Marketing, sales, customer support. All super valuable skills — and becoming a larger part of software engineering over time.
  • The importance of security. Learned firsthand, not as a chapter in a book.

The prototypes-on-your-laptop problem

If your examples all run from your local computer, that introduces a high level of risk to a company — especially if you're ever going to touch production data. You might not be getting opportunities not because you can't code, but because you don't know how to code in the context of what the company is trying to do: building production software.
Live migrations especially: you create a design, then you have to morph it into another design for a software change — which happens all the time. How do you do that with data already in the database? How do you avoid corrupting data? How do you make sure the end user doesn't notice anything changed, while still pushing out updates?
"What I know now is that is 100 times more valuable than being able to build out prototypes on your local computer."
→ Being challenged, and hopefully motivated — that's the real return. Everything else is a byproduct.
16

Ship it like a real company

"I would immediately try to think of some projects I could do that I would push live and treat like a real thing."

If he started again right now, this is what he'd do immediately:

  1. Think of projects you can push live.
  2. Treat them like a real thing — not a subdomain of some random hosting company.
  3. Get a domain.
  4. Get branding.
  5. Try to get some customers.
  6. Do everything a real company would do.
And if you're getting into a specific area — mobile app development, for example: build an app, deploy it on the app store, get reviews, get users, figure out how to monetize it, figure out advertising, marketing, sales. All of these skills are super valuable.
The shift he's pointing at: as time goes on, that's becoming a larger part of software engineering. Can you do more than just write basic code? Can you create features that are actually meaningful for a bigger picture?

And this loops back to people

Building on your own can be a lot. But if you're in a group of people doing the same thing, it's a lot more motivating and encouraging. Another reason to find people with experience and get into forums and groups.

→ A live product with a domain, branding, and one real customer teaches you more than a perfect local prototype ever will.

Part V

Career & life strategy

17

Don't marry a title or a company

"Open your mind a little bit about the company and the job title."

The mindset he's rejecting

Around six years ago there was a big spike of hiring at the FAANG companies, and everybody was trying to get into Google, Amazon, Apple. The implication was that if you didn't, you were a pathetic failure who probably didn't deserve to be in software engineering at all.

The reality: there are thousands and thousands — if not millions — of companies you can apply your skills to and potentially make a lot of money doing so. And there are so many more roles out there than just "software engineer."

What he actually did

  • Developer relations — interfacing with companies and customers in a technical way, helping people integrate with software.
  • Leading technical workshops.
  • Marketing around technical products.
  • Pure software engineering, at times.
  • Technical videos — created from his basement. The offer was around $240K, roughly $180K base.
Those kinds of opportunities are equally as good, if not better, than some of the software engineering opportunities out there.
Where this is heading: pure software engineering roles are becoming less common, and there's going to be a lot of new stuff around AI engineering or variations of software engineering roles.
→ Your personal strengths may not point at the title you've decided you need. Follow the strengths.

Part one: break out

Meet new people, ideally beyond where you are currently and where you want to be. Expose yourself to people doing what you want to do, so that that becomes your new normal.

The co-founder story: he was a co-founder with a bunch of software engineers who all had backgrounds in very high-paying, successful roles. When salary came up, he thought — "holy crap, I didn't even know it was possible to make these kinds of numbers."

That's unheard of in rural Ohio, where his neighbor is a corn field and the best type of job is the factory. Those were eye-opening conversations. They helped him push for higher pay, because he knew what was possible and had seen it in other people.

He'd never have seen it if he hadn't gone outside his network and location. Starting out, he was completely on his own — studying from his room, reading books, watching videos. "I think this was probably the worst thing I could have done."

The real breakthrough

It isn't leaving your comfort zone — it's expanding it, so the numbers stop being wild. "I almost undersold myself. In fact, I probably did undersell myself, because they didn't even blink an eye."

Today, what he did for $1,500 then, he wouldn't even consider doing now — because it wouldn't be worth the effort. $1,500 seems irrationally low. That's because he expanded his mindset and network such that he can see what other people are doing.

Part three: practice discomfort

  • Early career, he was worried about everything — to the point it affected his performance.
  • He almost had this effect: the less he cared, the better he performed. When he cared, he'd overanalyze, have anxiety, and self-sabotage.
  • Now he's built up a tolerance for discomfort and can push himself out of his comfort zone better — just from practicing.
"The secret is I'm always uncomfortable." Sometimes talking price or money is still uncomfortable — and that's fine.

Doing things like sales, negotiating, or pushing back on demands — things he avoided earlier — turned out to be one of the greatest unlocks to get more out of life.

→ The fastest way there isn't more work. It's exposing yourself to different people — and letting that change what "normal" means to you.
19

Go more human in an AI world

"In a world where everything is AI, going up to someone and saying, 'Hey, I would love to have an opportunity to do this with you' — these types of human-to-human interactions are now going to be so much more valuable."

The counterintuitive move

A lot of people believe that adopting AI means making everything artificial. He argues the opposite for certain things: automate the highly automatable work, and go the opposite direction on human-to-human interaction.

Why this is now an advantage

  • Job applications, cover letters, resumes submitted at scale — AI can automate the submission and sift through the pile.
  • Everything has become very inhuman-feeling.
  • So the way you thrive is to go heavy on the human portion, and stand out in a world saturated with AI.
His own practice: he might use AI to help with sales or marketing. But for high-value opportunities, he goes the extra mile to bring his actual human self into those parts of the business and life.

Some people he works with one-on-one, some he provides services to — and that's not something he's giving away to AI. He goes above and beyond to give them the best support possible. That's the differentiator going forward.

The caution for anyone new

Don't go so heavy on artificial intelligence that you lose the human component.

And on mindset, generally

  • Certain beliefs will cap your potential — assuming you're not able to do certain things creates the box you have to live in.
  • The biggest valuable skill is resourcefulness: being able to figure out anything, whether on your own, with AI, or by working with other people.
  • The university system often dims this, because it hands you everything you have to do. "Hey, if you do this stuff, you're going to be successful." That removes the self-preserving drive you have to push through.
  • Formal mentorship works differently: someone gives you guidance, but it's up to you to implement it. That self-initiative is what makes it work.
The beer-drinking story: in a university class, the guy next to him was watching Twitch streams. "Dude, what are you doing? You're paying money to be here so you can watch people play video games while you're in class." That same approach today would hurt him — except he might blame AI or the market instead of his own lack of discipline.
And the closing: even if you're not the most naturally gifted, not a flawless software developer, not perfectly handsome and beautiful — you can still be very successful. Work as if the result 100% depends on you and your input.
→ Use AI to amplify. Use your humanity to differentiate. Have something independent of the job, the degree, and the company — that you build yourself.
