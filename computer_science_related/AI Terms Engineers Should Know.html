1. How an LLM Understands Input

Large language model (LLM): a neural network — layers of simple nodes connected by learnable weights — trained to predict the next term of an input sequence. Feed in all that glitters and it predicts is not — the complete response all that glitters is not gold comes back to the user. Everything else in this lesson is detail around that one job.

Tokenization is how the input gets processed. The model breaks text into discrete tokens — and even the space character is a token (all + space + that + space + glitters). Why not just split on spaces? Because humans don’t talk like that. Suffixes carry meaning: -er (shimmers, murmurs, flickers) says the action is being performed by an object, and -ing (eating, dancing, singing) says an action is in progress. A token like ING is meaningful to the model.

Vectors represent meaning. Tokens tell you what to focus on; vectors say what it means. The model maps words into an n-dimensional space where similar-meaning words sit close together and opposite-meaning words sit far apart — each word becomes a coordinate, and that coordinate is its vector. Vectorization is how the model knows the inherent meaning of the whole vocabulary.

Attention solves ambiguity. Apple can be fruit, company, or a person you’re fond of. The spelling is identical, so the model looks at nearby words for context: tasty apple pushes the vector toward fruit; Apple revenue pushes it toward the company; apple of my eye means affection. This attention operation is what made LLMs famous — the 2017 paper, and by 2022 the quality of responses far exceeded anything seen before.

  1. Tokenize: split input into discrete, meaningful tokens.
  2. Vectorize: map each token to a coordinate in n-dimensional space.
  3. Attend: use nearby words to resolve ambiguous meanings.
  4. Predict: output the next token, completing the sequence.
Obvious“all that glitters” → the model predicts “is not gold.”
Counter-intuitiveTokens aren’t just words split by spaces — suffixes like -ing carry meaning.
Edge case“Apple” means three different things depending on the words around it.

2. Training Without Teachers, Then Specialising

Self-supervised learning means the structure of the data tells the model what to do — no human labelling required. The video’s demo: he blanks part of a video and you guess the missing number (5,4,3,2,1 → 1) or where the AI is looking. There is inherent structure in the input, and the mind fills the blank. A model trains the same way: take real text, truncate it, and predict what was removed. Compare the prediction to the ground truth you already know; if wrong, penalize the model (loss increases) and update the weights. Multiple puzzles run in parallel — what comes after et tu, what comes after Brutus, and so on. No humans needed; you scraped the text, you made the challenges yourself. That architectural decision is what makes LLMs scalable, and it’s spreading — image models now predict missing patches, video models predict motion.

Supervised learning is the standard alternative: a human provides labelled pairs — input all that glitters, expected output is not gold; input et tu, output Brutus. That’s expensive: a human labels every example. Self-supervised flips it — take text that already exists and create the puzzles yourself. When the model guesses wrong, the penalty is called loss: the bigger the mistake, the bigger the loss, and the weights — the learned numbers on the network’s connections — get updated to do better next time. The puzzles run in parallel (what comes after et, after et tu, after et tu Brutus), and part of training is learning when to stop: the correct next token is sometimes a stop token or a comma, not another word.

Transformer is often confused with the LLM itself, but it’s a specific algorithm for predicting the next token. The LLM is the product — think of it as a car; the transformer is the engine. A transformer runs input tokens through an attention block, then a feedforward neural network, producing output vectors — then another attention layer, another feedforward network, stacked. The first attention layer disambiguates terms; deeper layers find sarcasm and implications (a crane hunting a crab: first layer resolves crane = bird, second infers the crab is fearful). Stacks reach 12 layers, sometimes hundreds in recent GPT architectures. The engine could be swapped — state-space models, diffusion models — but today the transformer is the king of architectures.

Fine-tuning specialises a base model. First you train the base model self-supervised; then you run it through question-answer pairs. A medical LLM learns to answer in medical jargon; a financial model thinks in financial terms. Plausible-but-undesirable answers (like “I would like to know that too” to a direct question) get penalized. The same base model can be fine-tuned into many specialised models — a company takes a base model like Llama and tunes it to its customers’ queries.

  1. Collect existing text (scraped from the web).
  2. Truncate it and predict the missing parts — self-supervised.
  3. Penalize wrong guesses (loss); update weights; repeat, many puzzles in parallel.
  4. Learn when to stop — sometimes the right next token is a stop token or comma.
  5. Swap the engine: transformer = attention blocks + feedforward layers.
  6. Fine-tune the base model on Q&A to specialise it.
ObviousTruncate “all that glitters” and train the model to say “is not gold.”
Counter-intuitiveTraining is cheap because the data creates its own tasks — no human labels.
Edge caseTransformer ≠ LLM: the car is the product, the engine is the algorithm.
Step 1: collect a corpus of existing text.
Analogy: Fill-in-the-blank exercises — you hide the ending, the model guesses it, and the answer key is already in your hand. Self-supervised = the data makes its own quiz, and you already know the answers.
What people get wrong

That “transformer” and “LLM” are the same thing. The LLM is the product; the transformer is one algorithm it could use to predict the next token.

3. Adding Context at Inference Time

Few-shot prompting means augmenting the query before it reaches the model — during inference, in production, in real time. Instead of sending a plain vanilla query, you add examples. Your server sends the original query (where is my parcel?) plus example exchanges, and the model takes them into context and responds accordingly. The quality of the response goes up. It’s example prompting: examples in the prompt. That’s it. The key word is inference time — this all happens live, in production, on every request, with zero retraining: the model itself never changes, only the prompt does.

Retrieval augmented generation (RAG) takes the same idea further. A customer hits your API; the server forwards the query to the model along with examples, and also fetches the most relevant documents in real time — policy documents, terms and conditions, whatever matches. The examples teach the format; the documents teach company-specific context; the user query is the direct input. With all of that, the model gives high-quality responses. Some say RAG is already dead, but the idea — retrieve context, augment the query, generate a response — is the pattern.

Vector database is where those documents live, and it’s how the server finds relevant ones. A user says I am upset with your payment system, I expect a refund. No document may contain the word “upset” — but vectors encapsulate semantic meaning, so “upset” sits close to “low rating” and “drop off” in vector space. The database does a similarity search and fetches the nearest documents, which join the query and a system prompt as context. Algorithms like HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) make those searches efficient. To the engineer, it’s a black box: store documents, retrieve the relevant ones fast.

Two supporting ideas complete the picture. The system prompt is the standing instruction sent along with every query — the fixed part of the context (the model’s role, the answer format) — while the retrieved documents supply changing, query-specific knowledge. And where do the documents live? Vendors push different storage: graph databases (Neo4j), vector databases (Neon), or plain in-memory caches. The storage choice matters less than the result — usually a vector database wins, because a similarity search over coordinates is the fastest way to find “relevant” documents.

  1. User query arrives at your server/API.
  2. Few-shot: attach example exchanges to the query.
  3. RAG: fetch the most relevant documents from a vector database.
  4. Send query + examples + documents to the LLM.
  5. Get a response grounded in your context.
Obvious“Where is my parcel?” + examples → a formatted, helpful answer.
Counter-intuitive“Upset” matches documents about “low rating” — by meaning, not keyword.
Edge caseRAG is called dead by some — but retrieve-augment-generate is the pattern everyone builds on.
Analogy: Answering a customer’s question with the company policy handbook open on your desk — RAG hands the model the handbook page. RAG fetches the right documents and adds them to the prompt.
What people get wrong

That documents are matched by keywords. Vector databases match by meaning — “upset” finds documents about “low rating” without sharing a single word.

Mnemonic: “R-A-G” — Retrieve, Augment, Generate
Recall: What three things get sent to the LLM in a RAG setup?
The user query, example exchanges (few-shot), and relevant documents retrieved from the vector database.

Why this matters: few-shot + RAG is how you ground a model in your company’s real knowledge.
Mnemonic: “R-A-G” — retrieve, augment, generate.

4. Connecting the Model to Real Systems

Model context protocol (MCP) is a protocol for transferring context into a model from outside your system. Picture the flow: the user sends a query; an MCP client forwards it to the LLM. The LLM decides it wants external tools or databases — the client connects to external MCP servers, which are wrappers around real systems: one server wraps Indigo’s database, another wraps Air India’s. Flight details come back, get forwarded to the LLM along with the query and any vector-DB context, and the LLM can even take action — book flight Indigo 1020, which triggers another API call on Indigo’s MCP server. The final response goes back through the client to the user. The user no longer has to act on a recipe — the client executes the whole recipe. That makes LLMs far more powerful, and MCP has picked up huge popularity.

Context engineering is the umbrella that encapsulates all of this: few-shot prompting (examples), RAG (relevant documents from a vector database), and MCP (external servers and actions). Engineers face two challenges. One: user preferences. Two: prompt summarization — a sliding window sends the last 100 chats directly and summarises older history into five sentences; documents get summarised (by a cheap small or distilled model) before the expensive LLM sees them. The key distinction: prompt engineering is stateless — one single prompt, the same system prompt every time. Context engineering evolves with the user’s declared preferences and previous chat history. It’s long-term.

Agents are the most long-term thing you can build. An agent is a long-running process — think of a server receiving API calls — with many capabilities: it can query an LLM, query external systems, and query other agents to meet the user’s requirements. A travel agent example: it books flights, books hotels, manages your email while you’re away, and when it sees a window of opportunity — cheap flights — it makes the booking according to your preferences, on its own.

  1. MCP: LLM → client → external servers (wrappers) → real-time data back.
  2. Context engineering: examples + documents + external tools, tuned to preferences and history.
  3. Summarise long history and documents so only useful context reaches the model.
  4. Agents: long-running processes that query LLMs, systems, and other agents.
ObviousMCP server = a wrapper around a company’s database (Indigo, Air India).
Counter-intuitivePrompt engineering is stateless; context engineering evolves with preferences and history.
Edge caseAn agent books your flights while you’re away — it executes the whole recipe, not just answers.
Analogy: A personal assistant who not only reads your inbox but calls the airline, checks prices, and books — that’s an agent. Agents act on the world, not just answer questions.
What people get wrong

That prompt engineering and context engineering are the same. Prompt engineering is a single stateless prompt; context engineering is long-term, evolving with preferences and history.

Mnemonic: “MCP = Model Connects to Providers”
Recall: How does context engineering differ from prompt engineering?
Prompt engineering is stateless — one prompt, the same system prompt each time. Context engineering evolves with user preferences and chat history; it’s long-term.
Check (Module 4): What is an MCP server to a company database?

5. Rewards, Reasoning, and the Fair Coin

Reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) trains models to behave a particular way. Give the model a query; it generates two responses — you’ve seen the “choose the better one” screens in ChatGPT. The chosen response gets +1, the other gets −1. Think of each response as a path through vector space: tokens generated step by step. Good paths get positive scores, bad paths get negative scores, and with enough examples the model learns a landscape — positive regions it wants to reach, negative regions it avoids. It’s hill climbing: optimise the path you take so the end user is happy. It echoes Pavlov’s dog — bell, food, salivation, reinforced behaviour.

But reinforcement learning has a real limit, and the fair-coin example shows it. A coin keeps coming up heads. RL, watching outcomes, gets reinforced for predicting heads and punished for tails. A human, told the coin is fair, says 50/50 — because humans build a mental model of how the coin works, its physics. Reinforcement learning can’t build mental models; it can only read outcomes. We are not crocodiles — we have a deeper understanding of how things work. Still, RL makes models genuinely smarter, and RLHF is an extremely powerful technique.

Chain of thought is simple but powerful: when training, you clearly explain the thought process, so the model learns to break problems down step by step. Newer problems with different parameters still get reasoned through, because the model was trained to reason. The response quality is usually much higher than a direct answer. It resembles few-shot prompting, but the key difference is the step-by-step breakdown — and the model can add steps as it sees fit. Harder problems, more steps; easy problems, fewer (DeepSeek shows exactly this).

Reasoning models (also called LLMs with reasoning) are models that figure out how to solve a problem step by step. Chain of thought is one method; there are others — tree of thought, graph of thought, using tools. Examples include DeepSeek and OpenAI’s o1 and o3 models.

  1. RLHF: generate two responses; human picks the better one (+1 / −1).
  2. Good paths get positive scores, bad paths negative — hill climbing in vector space.
  3. Limit: RL can’t build mental models (fair coin = 50/50 to a human).
  4. Chain of thought: train step-by-step reasoning; model adds steps as needed.
  5. Reasoning models: solve problems step by step (DeepSeek, OpenAI o1/o3).
ObviousChatGPT’s “which response is better?” screen is RLHF in action.
Counter-intuitiveA fair coin that lands heads five times is still 50/50 — RL can’t see that, humans can.
Edge caseDeepSeek uses more reasoning steps for hard problems, fewer for easy ones.
Analogy: Pavlov’s dog — bell, food, salivation. RLHF is the same reinforcement loop, with a human holding the bell. Reward good outputs, punish bad ones, and behaviour shifts.
What people get wrong

That RL alone models human intelligence. It can’t build mental models — a human knows a fair coin is 50/50 even after five heads.

Mnemonic: “+1 good, −1 bad”

6. Images, Video, Small Models, and Shrinking Them

Multimodal models accept and create more than text: images and video. They can analyze an image and tell you the number of apples in it, or modify an image to create a new one; they can work with video the same way. The applications are huge — if you can create video ads with celebrities, the cost of producing video drops. Quality is still improving, but multimodal models already outperform text-only models because training on images of cats alongside the words cat and feline gives a deeper understanding of meaning. Multimodal just means any kind of input mode.

Small language models (SLMs) have fewer parameters than LLMs: roughly 3 million to 300 million, versus 3 to 300 billion for large models. Fewer connections, fewer weights. But they’re useful because they train on less, company-specific, or task-specific data. A bot trained only on customer queries and sales is an expert at sales — it just can’t give you a detailed weather analysis. For most companies that’s fine. NASA, on the other hand, would build a foundation model for weather prediction and not care about sales. Companies train SLMs on their proprietary data for specific use cases, keeping the data close and control high.

Distillation is how small models are usually built. A large LLM acts as the teacher: it gets an input and produces an output. In parallel, the small student model with its limited weights tries to predict the same output. Match — no weight changes. Mismatch — the student’s weights are updated. The goal is to condense the complex teacher network into the most reasonable small representation: acceptable performance, significantly reduced cost. At inference time the small model responds much faster and is easier to host.

Quantization shrinks the model’s numbers. Each weight is a number, say 32-bit; condense it to 8 bits and you expect to save about 75% of memory. It doesn’t map perfectly (attention mechanisms remain, and training cost is unchanged — you train a great model first, then quantize). Quantization is applied after training, so its payoff is reduced inference cost — the cost of running the model in production.

  1. Multimodal: models that accept and create images and video — deeper meaning.
  2. SLM: 3M–300M parameters, trained on company/task-specific data.
  3. Distillation: a small student mimics a large teacher.
  4. Quantization: 32-bit weights → 8-bit, ~75% memory saved, cheaper inference.

Counter-intuitiveA tiny sales bot is a great sales expert and can’t do weather — that’s the point.

FAQ

Is a transformer the same thing as an LLM?

No. The LLM is the product — the car. The transformer is a specific algorithm for predicting the next token — the engine. You could swap the engine (state-space, diffusion) and still have an LLM.

Why not just split input text on spaces?

Because humans don’t talk that way. Meaningful pieces like the -ing suffix carry grammatical information — and even the space character is its own token — so tokenization keeps the pieces the model can actually learn from.

How can a search find documents that don’t contain the query’s words?

Vector databases search by meaning, not keywords. “Upset” sits close to “low rating” and “drop off” in vector space, so the nearest documents get retrieved even without a shared word.

Does quantization make training cheaper?

No. You train a good model first, then quantize the weights. The savings are in inference — memory and the cost of running the model in production.

Can reinforcement learning model human intelligence by itself?

No — the fair coin proves it. RL only reads outcomes (it would chase heads), while a human builds a mental model of the coin’s physics and says 50/50. RL is powerful, but it isn’t the whole story.
