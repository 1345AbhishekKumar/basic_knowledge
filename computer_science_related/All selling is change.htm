All selling is change.

Your prospect is not comparing your product to a competitor's. They are comparing a familiar problem against an unfamiliar solution — and the familiar one usually wins. This lesson is about the eight moves that flip that decision.

Why this matters. If you sell anything — insurance, a business opportunity, a service — the reason you lose is almost never the product. It's that the prospect never felt enough of a gap to justify the risk of moving. Fix the communication and the numbers take care of themselves.

01 / FOUNDATION
Start here

Selling is change. Nothing else.

Every product, every opportunity, every pitch reduces to one act: moving a person from where they are to somewhere they are not.

The two panes of the change decision
The prospect is not weighing your product against a rival. They are weighing motion against stillness.

Strip away the product category, the comp plan, the features, the price. What is left is a person in a current situation, and a proposal that they leave it. That is the whole transaction. Selling is the act of making a change feel like the safer option.

This matters because it relocates the problem. If selling were about product quality, the better product would always win — and it demonstrably does not. Inferior products win every day. Worse policies stay in force for decades. People remain in jobs they openly hate. None of that is a product failure. It is a change failure.

The mechanism is status quo bias: the well-documented tendency for people to prefer the current state of affairs, even when the current state is worse than the available alternative. It is not stupidity and it is not stubbornness. It is how human decision-making is wired. The familiar carries less perceived risk than the foreign, regardless of the actual odds. A mediocre job that you already have feels safer than a better job you might not get.

Which means the prospect's real question is never "is this product good?" It is "is moving safer than not moving?" Those are different questions with different answers, and most sales conversations answer the wrong one.

The four moves in this reframe

  1. Notice what the prospect is currently doing — the status quo, in specific detail.
  2. Notice what the change would cost them, in their terms, not yours.
  3. Notice that the cost of staying is invisible to them until someone makes it visible.
  4. Accept that your job is to change the comparison, not to win an argument about the product.

Worked example: the friend in the bad relationship

Everyone knows someone who complains about a relationship constantly — and stays. From the outside this looks irrational. From the inside it is a straightforward risk calculation: the pain of the current relationship is known, bounded, and survivable, while leaving is unknown, unbounded, and possibly worse. The current pain has a ceiling. The new pain does not.

Your prospect runs the same calculation on your offer. A policy they already own has a known cost and a known (if inadequate) payout. Switching introduces a new company, new paperwork, a new set of unknowns. Unless you make the cost of staying concrete, the scale never moves.

Analogy. You are not pushing someone off a cliff. You are standing on the far side of a gap, making the near side look worse than the jump.
Pitfall

Assuming that a better product changes the decision. It does not. A better product only changes what is available on the far side of the gap — it says nothing about whether the jump feels survivable. Salespeople who lead with superiority ("we're the best", "our comp plan is number one") are describing the far side of the gap more loudly. The prospect is still standing on the near side, and the near side has not moved.

Why it matters: once you stop treating resistance as a product problem, you stop arguing about features — and start working on the only thing that actually moves the sale.

02 / FOUNDATION
The translation

You sell results, not things.

The product is the vehicle. The prospect is buying where the vehicle goes — and they will never buy the vehicle for its own sake.

What you're selling vs. what they're buying
The translation on the arrow is the entire skill. Most salespeople never make it.

There is a gap between the object on the table and the thing the prospect actually wants. The object is a contract, a kit, a subscription. The thing they want is a different life — one where a specific fear no longer applies. The sale happens on the far side of that gap, and the salesperson's job is to build the bridge, out loud, in the prospect's own terms.

Consider life insurance. Nobody wants a policy. Nobody has ever woken up wanting a policy. What they want is the absence of a specific scenario: a spouse who does not have to sell the house, a son who finishes school without inheriting a mortgage, a funeral that does not go on a credit card. The policy is the mechanism. The mechanism is not the offer.

The same logic applies to a business opportunity. Nobody wants a comp plan. What they want is the end of a commute, the ability to be at their kid's school play, or the dignity of not asking a manager for permission to use the bathroom. The comp plan is the vehicle that might get them there.

The practical consequence is a translation habit. Every time you are about to say a feature, stop and finish the sentence: "…which means…" — and make the second half of that sentence about the prospect's life, not about the product.

How to make the translation

  1. Name the feature or component you were about to pitch.
  2. Ask: what does this change about a Tuesday afternoon in their life?
  3. Say that instead. Keep the feature available as an answer, not as the opening.
  4. If you cannot complete the translation, the feature is not the reason they buy. Drop it.

Worked example: the same policy, two pitches

Thing-first: "This is a 20-year level term policy with a living benefit rider and a conversion option." Every clause is accurate and none of them answer the prospect's actual question, which is what happens to their son if they die next year.

Result-first: "If something happens to you, your son stays in the house, stays in his school, and does not have to figure out how to pay for any of it." That sentence contains no product information at all, and it is the sentence that moves the sale. The rider and the conversion option become answers to questions the prospect now wants to ask.

Analogy. A prescription is not the cure. Nobody thanks the pharmacist for the pill — they thank them for the sleep, the cleared infection, the pain that stopped.
Pitfall

Pitching features because you find them impressive. This is the most common failure in the entire field, and it feels like competence from the inside. You know the product deeply; the depth feels like value; so you lead with it. But depth of product knowledge is not the same as depth of prospect understanding, and only one of them closes.

Why it matters: a prospect who cannot picture the result has no reason to accept the risk of the change — no matter how good the thing is.

03 / THE MECHANISM
Before you solve

Be a problem finder, not a product pusher.

Problem solving happens after the sale. Problem finding happens before it — and it is the part almost nobody trains.

Where problem solving actually sits
The lower path is the numbers game. It skips the top of the sequence and hopes the pitch lands anyway.

Here is a claim that sounds like a word game and is not. Problem solving cannot happen before the purchase. If the prospect does not buy, you did not solve their problem — you described a solution they declined. The solving happens after the decision, during delivery. Which means the skill that produces sales is not problem solving at all. It is problem finding.

Most prospects, at the start of a conversation, do not actually know they have a problem. Or rather: they have a vague sense of one — "I'd like to make more money" — but no idea of its shape, size, or cost. They have never sat down and calculated what happens to their family's finances if they die in year three. They have never worked out how many more years of the same commute the current arrangement implies.

A problem that has not been felt does not motivate change. It sits there as an abstraction. The prospect agrees it's a thing and does nothing about it, because doing nothing costs nothing today — and today is the only day they're actually living in.

So the job is to make the abstract problem concrete, in the prospect's own words, using questions rather than assertions. When the prospect says the cost out loud, they believe it in a way they never believe it when you say it. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the question matters more than the answer.

The alternative — leading with product, features, and benefits — is what the source calls hopium: throwing everything at the wall and hoping something sticks. It is not a strategy, it is a lottery ticket. And it converts the sale into a numbers game, because the only way to win a lottery is to buy more tickets.

The four moves of problem finding

  1. Ask about the current situation in specifics — not "how's work" but "how many hours a week, and where is your son while you're there."
  2. Ask what they'd rather have, in their words. Let them describe the far side themselves.
  3. Ask what happens if nothing changes. Let them price the status quo.
  4. Stay quiet while they answer. The silence is where the problem becomes real.

Worked example: the door that didn't open

A rep spends eight weeks selling home security door-to-door. The training was: show your enthusiasm, and they'll be enthusiastic. The rep does exactly that — features, benefits, the number-one ranking, the A+ rating. The result: sixty-plus hours in a single week, zero sales, straight commission, no base.

The pitch was not the problem. The sequence was. Every door opened with the solution before a single question had been asked about the house, the street, the break-ins that had or hadn't happened, the thing the homeowner was actually worried about. The objections — "we don't need it", "too expensive", "I need to think about it" — were not resistance to the product. They were the predictable output of a pitch delivered before a problem existed.

Analogy. A doctor who prescribes before examining is not fast, they're dangerous. The examination is not the delay before the treatment — it is the treatment's first act.
Pitfall

Believing that more objections mean more interest. It sounds reassuring and it is exactly backwards. Every objection is a unit of uncertainty in the prospect's mind, and you created it by pitching into a vacuum. More objections means the pitch went out too early, not that the prospect is warming up. If you find yourself collecting objections as evidence of engagement, you are collecting evidence of a sequencing error.

Why it matters: problem finding is the only part of the sale where you can change the prospect's picture of their own situation — and everything downstream depends on that picture.

04 / THE LADDER
Three eras

Boiler room, consultative, neuro-emotional.

Three approaches to persuasion, ranked by how well they actually work. Most training sits on the bottom two rungs.

The persuasion ladder
The variable is who does the persuading. You, or the prospect.

Persuasion has a history, and the history has a direction. The oldest approach — the boiler room — treats the sale as a contest of wills. You push, you posture, you tell them why they need it, you assume the close and refuse to take no. This is the approach most people picture when they hear the word "salesperson," and it is the least effective of the three. Not marginally — dramatically.

The reason is mechanical. Pressure triggers the same defensive response as any other threat. When you push, the prospect's job becomes resisting the push, and their attention moves off the problem and onto you. You have made yourself the obstacle, which means removing yourself becomes the goal. Fight or flight is not a metaphor here; it is the actual neurochemistry, and it is not a state in which anyone makes a considered purchase.

The second era — consultative selling, arriving in the late seventies and early eighties with Sandler and the SPIN Selling framework — is a genuine improvement. It replaces assertion with questions. Instead of telling the prospect what they need, you ask about their situation and their needs, and let them supply the material. This is far better than pushing, and it is where most professional training now lives.

It still has a ceiling. Consultative questions tend to be logical and surface-level — "what's your current coverage?", "what's your timeline?" — and surface-level questions produce surface-level answers. The prospect stays in their head, in analysis mode, and analysis mode is not where purchasing decisions get made. People buy on emotion and justify with logic. A method that only reaches the logic is reaching the justification, not the decision.

The third era, neuro-emotional persuasion questioning, changes the target. It does not ask the prospect to evaluate your argument. It asks questions that cause the prospect to generate the emotional case for change themselves — and a case you generate yourself is roughly a hundred times more persuasive than a case someone hands you. This is the approach the rest of the lesson unpacks.

How the three eras differ in practice

  1. Boiler room — you talk, they resist. Presentation is most of the conversation.
  2. Consultative — you ask, they answer in their head. Questions are logical and stay on the surface.
  3. Neuro-emotional — you ask, they answer in their chest. Questions move toward consequence.
  4. The measurable difference: presentation time. Boiler room runs 70%+ talking. The target is under 10%.

Worked example: the 15% that doesn't land

Consultative selling says: find the need, match the benefit. Your policy is 15% cheaper for identical coverage, so you lead with "you'll save 15%" — a factually correct, need-matched, logically persuasive opening. The prospect responds: "I mean, it's only like ten dollars a month. Not worth switching."

Nothing was wrong with the number. The question was missing the emotional weight that makes a number mean something. A neuro-emotional version does not lead with the percentage at all — it leads with what the savings would do, in the prospect's own framing, and lets them argue for the value rather than against it.

Analogy. Arguing someone into a purchase is like pushing a car up a hill. Asking questions that make them want it is like rolling it downhill — same car, same distance, completely different amount of work.
Pitfall

Assuming consultative selling is the ceiling. It's a real improvement over pushing, so it's easy to conclude you've arrived. But consultative selling still plays the numbers game, because surface-level logical questions still leave the prospect emotionally neutral — and emotionally neutral prospects don't change. You get a higher hit rate than the boiler room and the same underlying fragility.

Why it matters: knowing which rung you're standing on tells you exactly what to change — and stops you from "fixing" a tone problem with more features.

05 / THE CARRIER
Signal, not decoration

Tone is how they read your intent.

The same eleven words can be a question, an accusation, or an invitation. Tone decides which — before the words are processed.

Five tone registers
Each one changes what the prospect thinks you're actually asking for.

Tone is not how you sound. Tone is how your prospect interprets the intention behind what you say and ask. That definition matters, because it moves tone out of the category of delivery technique and into the category of meaning. Your tone is not decorating the question — it is telling the prospect what the question is for.

Consider: "Do you 100% like what you're doing for a living?" Delivered flatly and neutrally, it's a survey question. The prospect answers "yeah, it's fine" and the conversation stalls. Delivered with a slight lean and a note of genuine doubt, the same eleven words become an invitation to admit something. The prospect hears a different question: is there something you're not saying?

The registers are not interchangeable, and they are not decoration. Each one opens a specific door. Curiosity invites detail — the prospect explains more than they planned to. Confusion invites clarification — the prospect re-examines what they just said. Challenge invites defence — the prospect argues a position, and the position they argue is theirs. Concern invites honesty — the prospect tells you the real thing. Playfulness lowers the guard — the prospect stops bracing against you.

Your facial expression is the remote control for the tone. A slight furrow reads as confusion or concern; a raised brow reads as challenge; a half-smile reads as play. You cannot separate the two — the face sets the register, the voice delivers it.

Matching register to job

  1. Opening — playful. Drop the guard before you ask anything that matters.
  2. Situation — curious. You genuinely don't know yet, and it should show.
  3. Problem awareness — concerned, leaning in. Let them feel you're on their side of the problem.
  4. Gap-building — confused, or mildly challenging. "That doesn't quite add up" pulls them back in.
  5. Consequence — concerned, slow. This is not the moment for lightness.

Worked example: "a fairly decent job"

A nurse manager has been in her role for years. You could say "that's an amazing job" — and she'll agree, and nothing moves. You could say "that job sounds terrible" — and she'll defend it, and you'll be arguing. What the source does instead is say, with a slight tilt of doubt: "I mean, that's a fairly decent job."

Fairly decent is the operative phrase. It's not an insult, so there's nothing to defend. It's not praise, so there's nothing to agree with. It sits in the gap and lets the doubt do its work. Within two follow-up questions she is telling you why the job is not enough — in her words, with her reasons. You never made the argument. You just left a small, tonally-delivered space where the argument could appear.

Analogy. Words are the sheet music. Tone is the performance. The same notation played flat and played with intent are not the same piece of music.
Pitfall

Believing the words carry the question. They don't. This is why scripts fail in the hands of someone who doesn't understand the register: the script's words are correct and the tone is neutral, so the prospect hears a survey. "What's caused you to feel like you might want to start your own business?" is a great question asked with concern and an interrogation asked flatly. Same words, opposite outcomes.

Why it matters: tone is the variable that decides whether a question opens the prospect or closes them — and it operates before they've consciously processed a single word.

06 / THE SPINE
The sequence

The five stages of NEPQ.

Situation, problem awareness, solution awareness, consequence, commitment. In that order. Skipping a stage is the most common way a conversation stalls.

Press play to step through the five stages.

Static version: the same five stages, in the same order, with the same job for each.

The five stages are the spine of the method. Each one has a single job, and the job of each stage is to make the next stage possible. This is why order matters more than content: a brilliant consequence question asked before problem awareness has landed is just a stranger being gloomy at you.

Situation establishes where the prospect actually is. Not the polished version — the real version. How long in the role, who else is in the household, what the hours look like. These are factual questions and they should feel like small talk, because the prospect is not yet ready to be examined. The purpose is not to gather data for your own use; it is to give the prospect a clear picture of their own starting point, which they usually don't have.

Problem awareness is where the gap opens. The prospect already knows something is off — that's why they took the meeting. What they don't have is the size of it. Problem awareness questions don't introduce a problem; they let the prospect measure one they already half-know about. "What's caused you to feel like you might want to start your own business?" is a problem-awareness question, and the answer is the material the rest of the conversation runs on.

Solution awareness is where possibility enters. The prospect has now described what's wrong; they need to be able to picture what right looks like — in their terms. What would the income need to be? What would the schedule need to look like? What would they do with the difference? This stage exists because a problem without a visible solution produces despair, not a purchase.

Consequence is the stage most people skip, and skipping it is why sales stall at the finish. The prospect has a problem and can picture a solution — and still might not move, because not moving still feels safe. Consequence questions make the cost of the status quo concrete: what happens if this is still the situation in five years? Who else absorbs the cost if you do nothing? This is where the weight shifts.

Commitment is the decision, and by this point it should be almost administrative. If the first four stages were done properly, the prospect has already argued themselves into the change. The commitment question is not a close in the boiler-room sense — it's the natural next sentence.

The five stages, one line each

  1. Situation — "Here is where I actually am."
  2. Problem — "Here is what that's costing me."
  3. Solution — "Here is what better would look like."
  4. Consequence — "Here is what happens if I don't move."
  5. Commitment — "Here is the decision."

Worked example: the same conversation, two orders

Wrong order. A prospect mentions she's been nursing for seven years. You immediately move to solution: "Have you thought about a side business? You could double your income." She says "maybe, I'd have to think about it." You have skipped situation and problem entirely, so the solution has nothing to attach to.

Right order. Situation: how long, what shift, who's at home. Problem: "that's a fairly decent job — what's caused you to feel like you might want to start your own?" She describes the ceiling. Solution: what would she want the income to be, what would she do with it — private school, a nanny, peace of mind. Consequence: "what happens if it's still the same in five years?" Then commitment. Same product, same prospect, same facts. The only difference is the sequence.

Analogy. The five stages are a staircase, not a menu. You can't skip from the first step to the fifth — the fourth step is what makes the fifth reachable.
Pitfall

Jumping to solution awareness as soon as you hear a problem. This is the product-pusher reflex wearing better clothes: the prospect says "I hate my hours" and you immediately say "I have something that fixes that." It feels responsive and it is actually a skipped stage. The prospect has named a problem; they have not yet felt its size. A solution offered before the problem is felt becomes another feature to evaluate, and features get evaluated on price.

Why it matters: a stalled conversation is almost always a skipped stage — and the five-stage frame tells you exactly which one to go back to.

07 / THE LEVER
Pull, don't push

Downplay it, and they'll up-play it.

Status is set in the first few seconds. And the value of what you offer is set by who does the emphasising — you or them.

The downplay / up-play seesaw
Whatever you do to the value, the prospect does the opposite.

There are two levers in this module and they run on the same principle: the prospect moves toward whatever space you leave open. If you fill the space with your own emphasis, they have nothing to do but push back. If you leave it empty, they step into it.

Start with the value lever. When you tell a prospect how much they'll save, how much better the product is, how big the opportunity is, you are doing the arguing. And a prospect who is being argued at will find the counter-argument — that's not contrarianism, it's how people protect their autonomy. "You'll save 15%" produces "it's only ten dollars a month." Your emphasis created their minimisation.

Reverse the polarity. Describe the same savings as small and unremarkable — "it's not even a big deal, maybe twelve or fourteen bucks a month" — and the prospect now has a value statement to defend. They will supply the meaning for you: every penny counts, that's a dinner out, over a year that's real money. You have not become more persuasive. You have stopped competing with the prospect for the job of being persuaded.

One caution, and it matters. Downplay is a tonal move, not a literal one. You are making the pitch small, not the product. If you downplay all the way to "honestly this probably isn't worth it," you have not created space — you have given an accurate negative review. The register is light and slightly dismissive of your own salesmanship, never dismissive of the prospect's benefit.

The second lever is status. Society does not hold salespeople or network marketers in high regard — the audience knows this, and the source says it directly. That means your opening lines are doing status work whether you intend them to or not. "I've got a business where I help people print cash" or "I help people fire their boss" reads as low-status to a prospect, who hears one thing: salesperson, run.

The fix is mismatching: downplay your own position, put the focus on them immediately, and let them carry the conversation's importance. "You'd probably get bored if I told you — I'm sure what you do is way more interesting" is not self-deprecation for its own sake. It moves the spotlight off you, which raises your status by lowering your self-importance. The prospect who feels important is not braced against you.

Using the lever

  1. Never open with what you do in a way that sounds like a pitch. Downplay, then hand them the floor.
  2. When you name the value, name it smaller than you want to. Let them enlarge it.
  3. When they enlarge it, don't pounce. Let the enlargement sit for a beat — it's now theirs.
  4. Check yourself: are you downplaying the pitch, or the product? Only the pitch.

Worked example: the $12 a month

Your policy is 15% cheaper for identical coverage. The prospect currently pays around $80 a month, so the saving is roughly $12.

Up-play version: "You'd save 15% for the same coverage." Prospect: "That's like ten bucks. Not even worth switching." You have made the decision about whether $120 a year is worth paperwork, and the prospect has decided it isn't.

Downplay version: "For someone in your situation it's not even a big deal — you're only going to save twelve, thirteen, fourteen bucks a month." Prospect: "Are you kidding? My mom always said every penny saved is a penny earned — I could take my wife out to a nice dinner with that." Same $12. Now it's a dinner, a principle, and a decision the prospect made themselves.

Analogy. A door held open is an invitation. A door pushed open is an intrusion. Same door, same opening — different amount of force, completely different reception.
Pitfall

Confusing downplay with negativity. The move is to make your salesmanship small, not the prospect's benefit. "It's only twelve bucks" works because it's true and slightly self-effacing. "Honestly this policy is pretty mediocre" is a different sentence with a different outcome. If a prospect could reasonably hear your downplay as a reason not to buy, you've gone past the mark.

Why it matters: the strongest version of your case is the version the prospect says out loud — and they can only say it if you leave the sentence unfinished.

08 / THE DELIVERY
Timing

Pace the question. Then loop back.

The single most common reason prospects give vague answers is that you asked too fast. The second most common is that you didn't come back around.

The loop-back
A question asked once gets a surface answer. The same question asked again with a new word gets the real one.

There is a delivery failure that looks like a content failure. You ask a good question, you ask it in the right tone, and the prospect answers with something vague — "yeah, I like it," "things are fine," "I'd have to think about it." It's tempting to conclude the question was wrong. Usually the pacing was wrong.

Verbal pacing means leaving space after a question. When you fire the next question half a second after the previous answer, you deny the prospect the time to internalise what they just heard themselves say. And the internalisation is the whole point — the moment where the answer stops being a reply to you and becomes a thought of theirs. Asked too fast, a question is just information you want. Asked with a pause, it's an invitation to think.

The second technique is the loop-back. You ask a question, get a surface answer, and rather than moving on, you emphasise a word from their answer and come back around to the question with a new qualifier attached. "Can I ask how come this is so important to you?" — pause — they answer — then, later: "why is it so important to you now though?"

The word "now" is doing real work. The first question sent their mind to the general importance of the thing. The second sends it to the specific importance at this moment in their life — which is where the urgency lives, and urgency is what converts a preference into a decision.

The third technique in this family is the qualified question — the one that makes a surface answer almost impossible. "Do you 100% like what you're doing for a living?" Nine out of ten people cannot answer yes. The qualifier 100% creates a hairline crack, and when the prospect says "well, not 100%," they have just admitted a problem in their own voice.

There's a further move available when they don't take the bait. If the prospect says "yeah, I love it," you don't argue — you push them away. "Sounds like things are 100% perfect for you." Now they have to pull you back: "no, not a hundred percent." The position they just defended is the position they now have to retreat from, and the retreat is theirs, not yours.

The three delivery techniques

  1. Pace — pause after each question. Let the answer become a thought before you ask the next thing.
  2. Loop back — return to an answered question with a new qualifier. "Why is that important to you now?"
  3. Qualify — attach "100%" or "always" or "everything" so a yes becomes almost impossible to give honestly.

Worked example: "do you 100% like it?"

Unqualified: "Do you like what you do for a living?" — "Yeah, I like it." The conversation is now closed, and you have no material.

Qualified: "Do you 100% like what you're doing?" — "I mean… I definitely love being a nurse, but not a hundred percent, no." The prospect has just placed their own foot on the crack. Follow up: "What do you mean?" — "They keep adding responsibilities without adding pay." You now have a priced problem, in their words, that you did not have to assert.

And if they do say "yes, a hundred percent"? You don't argue. You say, warmly: "Sounds like things are a hundred percent perfect for you." Pause. Most people cannot let that stand, and they'll correct themselves. Either way, you win by not pushing.

Analogy. A question asked once is a knock at the door. A question asked again with a new word is being let in. Same house, different room.
Pitfall

Treating the loop-back as repetition. Asking the same question twice in the same words is an interrogation — the prospect hears "you didn't answer properly, try again" and closes. The loop-back works because a single word changes the question's target: now, really, for you, at this point. The question sounds familiar and points somewhere new. Without the new pointer, it's just pressure.

Why it matters: pacing and looping are the difference between collecting answers and creating them — and created answers are the ones the prospect acts on.

11 / PRACTICE
Next steps

Two drills and a place to test them.

Reading this changes nothing. Two of these exercises, done this week, change a lot.

Drill 1 — Translate a feature

Take your single most-pitched feature. Write it down. Then write the sentence a prospect would say to their spouse about the result. If you can't write that sentence, the feature isn't your opening — find the one that is.

Drill 2 — Run the five stages on a real person

Pick one live conversation this week. Before it, write S-P-S-C-C on a card. After it, mark where you skipped. Do this three times and the sequence becomes automatic — which is the point.

Where to test it

Take a recorded practice call to a practitioner community and ask for a specific critique: "where did I skip a stage?" The sales subreddits and the NEPQ practitioner communities both do this well. Ask for the sequencing note, not general feedback.
