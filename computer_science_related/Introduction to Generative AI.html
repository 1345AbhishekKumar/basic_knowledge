Experiment 1 · Source 06:04–09:00

1. Why learn generative AI now

Corey has been at Microsoft about two years. The first six months were warming up; then ChatGPT and the OpenAI models made everything move fast. A year later the questions changed. People no longer ask only what the new thing is. They ask where it already sits in their work, what new inputs it accepts, and how they keep control while using it. That shift is the reason this session frames the topic around three pressures you already feel, not around model trivia. If you felt star-eyed, confused, or behind in the opening poll, this is the remedy: a shared map before any tool talk.

First, ubiquity. Copilot-style help now sits inside almost every product surface: word processors, spreadsheets, meeting apps, code editors. You ask about a document, draft from it, or process rows without leaving the app. Second, multimodality. GPT-4 class models accept more than typed text. You show an image and ask what it holds. You speak and get text back, or send text and hear speech. You attach data and ask for a reading of it. Third, autonomy. This is the human part. When a tool drafts, summarizes, and suggests, how do you keep your own aims, standards, and daily rhythm? The session returns to this in responsibility and security later: productivity should rise while your judgment stays in the loop. Together the three explain timing. The tools are near, the inputs are wide, and the open question is how you work with them well.

Key definitions.
Generative AI
Models that produce new text, images, code, or speech from your instruction.
Ubiquity
Help built inside everyday apps, where the work already happens.
Multimodality
One model reading text plus image, voice, or attached data.
Autonomy
You keep the aims, standards, and final say while assisted.
Procedure
  1. Point to one app you already use that now offers built-in drafting or summary.
  2. Name one non-text input you could try this week: image, voice, or a small file.
  3. Write one line stating where you keep the final say.
Obvious example. Meeting notes in Teams: Copilot lists decisions and your action items while you stay in the call.
Counter-intuitive example. A laggy stream and kids heard in the background: the session still works because the value is in the method, not perfect delivery.
Edge case. A global audience on a London group call — Texas, Virginia, Sweden — learning together because the stream travels but the tasks stay local.
Analogy card. Think of a kitchen that gained running water, a blender, and a clear head cook. Pipes everywhere is ubiquity. A blender that takes fruit, ice, or nuts is multimodality. The cook who tastes before serving is autonomy.
What people get wrongThat newness alone is the reason to learn. The session argues timing comes from placement, inputs, and control — not novelty.
Why this matters. It turns vague pressure into three checks you can apply to any tool this week.
Leads to Experiment 2: once you see where tools sit, you can name the four jobs worth giving them.
Memory: UMA — Ubiquity, Multimodality, Autonomy

2. Four jobs where models help most

When people ask what generative AI can do for me, the session refuses generic hype and names four lanes with customer evidence. A large language model, or LLM, means a model trained on large text collections that predicts likely continuations and can be steered by your prompt — the instruction or question you send. Content generation covers call-center replies, customer-service chats with a more conversational and adaptive tone than older bots, plus blog drafts and search-grounded copy shaped to your readers. Code generation covers GitHub Copilot in the editor and, just as important, natural-language routes for non-developers: describe the rows you need and get SQL, even over proprietary data models where standard docs fall short. Semantic search means asking across messy holdings — structured tables plus unstructured notes, tickets, scans — without first rebuilding everything into neat schemas. Summarization means compressing logs, reports, and analytics into insights and next actions for finance, service, or government teams.

You also learn what changed in apps. Older apps constrain you to their boxes and buttons over fixed datasets. Intelligent apps accept natural-language goals, personalize as data shifts instead of freezing one dataset, and return fresh reads quickly. Picture the insurance case: an adjuster points a vision-enabled model at claim photos and scribbled notes, the model reads them, and the result lands in structured form another system can use. Or your own week: long threads become a short brief with owners. The practical move is matching: take one repeating task you own and place it in exactly one lane. If it fits none, do not force it. That discipline keeps pilots small and honest, and it sets up the value chart next.

Key definitions.
LLM (large language model)
A model trained on large text collections and steered by your prompt.
Intelligent app
An app accepting natural-language goals and personalizing as data shifts.
Semantic search
Asking across messy structured and unstructured holdings without rebuilding them.
Summarization
Compressing logs, reports, and threads into insights and next actions.
Procedure
  1. List three repeating tasks from your last two weeks.
  2. Assign each to content, code, search, or summary — or park it.
  3. Pick the one with the clearest input and the clearest reader.
Obvious example. Support inbox: draft adaptive replies to common queries, agent edits before sending.
Counter-intuitive example. A non-developer writing SQL by describing the table in plain words over an internal model.
Edge case. Handwritten site notes photographed and returned as structured rows for a claims system.
Analogy card. Four bench tools: a press for content, a jig for code, a sieve for search, a still for summary. Same workshop, different job.
What people get wrongThat chatbots are new. The session notes bots existed for years; LLMs changed tone and adaptability, not the existence of chat.
Why this matters. Four lanes stop you from asking one tool to do everything badly.
Leads to Experiment 3: lanes tell you what; the value chart tells you what first.

3. Start by value and read the stack

This is the planning bench. The session draws value against complexity so an analytics audience can choose a start. Summarization and question answering sit low on complexity and high enough on value to begin: briefs, answers over your docs, meeting recaps. Data-driven decisioning costs more because analysis is involved, yet it pays on unstructured holdings you could never tabulate by hand. Personalization follows: recommendations and tuned interactions for distinct users. Automation sits top-right — most value, most complexity — where repetitive work you must complete but dislike doing gets handled end to end. The advice is plain: begin with summary and Q&A, prove care and handling, then climb only when the win justifies the build.

Under that chart sits the real stack, and the session pulls the cover off. Prebuilt capability includes Azure OpenAI for direct work with OpenAI models, plus Microsoft Fabric and Power Platform for faster deploy and build. Orchestration includes Prompt Flow, an open-source Microsoft tool that shows the path from user prompt to response and helps evaluation, alongside Semantic Kernel and LangChain, which connect to stores like GitHub or document libraries and decide next steps after each action. Then models, then APIs, deployment, and steady data access. Two live questions matter here. Hugo Wing asks if Visual Studio Code is enough — enough to start coding, yes, but not for hosting, data plumbing, and monitoring. Andy asks where the model begins — the answer is that the stack is not a ladder. You move up and down: start on OpenAI models with an orchestration helper, then drop to an open model to control cost once the use case is firm. Knowing the layers keeps you from mistaking a demo for a system.

Key definitions.
Value versus complexity
A chart placing summary and Q&A first and full automation last.
Azure OpenAI
Managed access for working directly with OpenAI models.
Orchestration
Helpers routing prompts, data, and next steps between parts of a build.
Prompt Flow
An open-source Microsoft tool showing the path from prompt to response.
Procedure
  1. Place your candidate task on the value chart; require a summary or Q&A start.
  2. Circle the stack layers you need: model, data access, orchestration, deploy.
  3. Note one cost or control reason you might swap a layer later.
Obvious example. Weekly report brief: low build cost, clear readers, fast proof of care.
Counter-intuitive example. Starting on a strong model then moving down to a cheaper one once prompts settle.
Edge case. A team that skips to full automation first and pays in monitoring debt.
Analogy card. A lab bench with drawers you open as needed. You do not buy every drawer to run one test.
What people get wrongThat the stack runs top to bottom once. The session stresses travel in both directions as cost and fit become clear.
Why this matters. It protects beginners from building the hardest system first.
Leads to Experiment 4: the model drawer deserves its own careful look.

Experiment 4 · Source 25:28–32:43

4. Choosing and combining models

Dan asks the question everyone carries: with so many models, how do I choose? The session splits the world in two. Proprietary models, here the OpenAI family — GPT-4, GPT-4 Turbo, Whisper for speech, older Davinci and Babbage — tend to lead on several tasks but carry company ownership and limited licenses. Open-source models — Llama 3, the Mistral series, Cohere Command R, plus the Hugging Face catalog that hosts community models rather than making them — allow freer use and faster task-specific fine-tuning, and often win on cost for each unit of performance because architecture and hosting differ, not because they are simply worse. Community fine-tunes matter: someone trains a base model narrowly, for example for a language or for math-heavy work, and you reuse that focus instead of paying a giant model to be average at everything.

In practice teams use both, assembled like parts. The Stockholm example makes it concrete. A Swedish site reads well under GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 in Swedish, but Finnish lags. The fix chains three parts: a Helsinki Swedish-to-Finnish translation model, a Finnish-tuned generation model for fluent answers, and a Whisper Large fine-tune for speech to text — together a Finnish customer helper matching the Swedish experience. Note the cautions the session adds. A Llama 3 release does not retire Llama 2; older models keep value for fitting tasks. Sami names TimeGPT for time series, and the session points to Hugging Face for synthetic series, forecasting helpers, and math-tuned options, with Mistral-family models rating well on some math evaluations. Choose by use case first, then latency (how fast answers return), expected response type and length (some models run long, some terse), and context window (how much prompt plus supplied data fits before the answer). Cost follows fit; never lead with the newest name.

Key definitions.
Proprietary model
A company-owned model with a limited license, such as GPT-4.
Open-source model
A freely reusable and tunable model, such as Llama 3.
Fine-tuning
Narrow extra training that focuses a model on one task or language.
Context window
How much prompt plus supplied data fits before the answer.
Latency
How fast the model returns answers to queries.
Procedure
  1. Write the task, the reader, and the longest input you must accept.
  2. Shortlist one proprietary and one open option; compare license and cost.
  3. Test tone and length side by side before committing.
Obvious example. Drafting help in English on a proprietary model with a wide window.
Counter-intuitive example. Keeping Llama 2 for a narrow job after Llama 3 ships, because fit beats newness.
Edge case. Chaining translation plus Finnish-tuned generation plus speech-to-text for one audience.
Analogy card. Model choice is like choosing pans: one large stockpot plus two small saucepans often beats three stockpots.
What people get wrongThat cheapest means weakest. The session ties lower cost to different build and hosting, plus narrow tuning.
Why this matters. Fit-first choice controls spend and avoids license surprises later.
Leads to Experiment 5: even the right model fails on a vague prompt.
Memory: L-L-C — Latency, Length, Context window
Recall prompt. List the three selection checks after use case, then reveal.
Latency, expected response type and length, context window.

Experiment 5 · Source 32:43–36:50

5. A prompt method that holds up

Prompt engineering, in this session, means shaping what you send so the model can complete the task with less guesswork. A prompt is simply your instruction, question, or request plus any material you attach. The skill belongs to everyone, not only developers, because every Copilot, chat box, and search helper reads prompts. The session organizes good practice into two handles: context and constraint. Context tells the model what world it works in. Constraint tells it what shape the answer must take and what to do when unsure. Short, explicit prompts beat long, hopeful ones, and the transcript is thin on exact wording tricks — it teaches the handles rather than a phrase list, which is worth stating plainly.

Context has four moves. Give a role: helpful assistant, senior business analyst, developer advocate — each sets aims and tone. Be specific about the material, as if briefing a skilled newcomer to your team who knows the craft but not your case. State the end goal openly: with Copilot, ask for the array because you will loop over it later, not just the array alone. Add supporting content: examples of the outcome you want, called zero, one, or multi-shot depending on how many samples you show. Constraints have four moves as well. Ask for format: bullets, table, ordered steps. Set length: one paragraph, five rows, under eighty words. Set priority or style: favor brevity, favor humor, favor caution. Finally, give the model an out: say what to do without an answer — state uncertainty, decline, or flag illogic — so it does not invent with confidence. Together these eight moves turn a vague wish into a testable request you can reuse.

Key definitions.
Prompt
Your instruction plus any attached material sent to the model.
Context (prompting)
Role, material, end goal, and supporting examples.
Constraint (prompting)
Format, length, priority, and the out-clause.
Zero / one / multi-shot
How many outcome examples you show the model.
Out-clause
An instruction to state uncertainty instead of guessing.
Procedure
  1. Draft role, material, goal, and one example.
  2. Add format, length, priority, and the out-clause.
  3. Run, keep the winner, and reuse it as a template.
Obvious example. Role plus table: as a senior analyst, return three risks in a three-column table.
Counter-intuitive example. Stating the later use — because a loop follows — improves the immediate code.
Edge case. Explicit out-clause: if the notes conflict, list the conflict instead of choosing.
Analogy card. A lab order slip: sample, test, and one example of a good result, plus tube size and what to do if the sample is short.
What people get wrongThat prompt skill is developer-only. The session places it with every user of every generative tool.
Why this matters. Reusable prompts make quality repeatable instead of lucky.
Leads to Experiment 6: clear prompts still need harm checks and guards.
Memory: R-G-E plus F-L-P-O — Role, Goal, Example; Format, Length, Priority, Out
Recall prompt. Recite the two handles and one move from each, then reveal.
Handles: context and constraint. Sample moves: role (context), format (constraint).

6. Responsible use and secure builds

The session places responsibility early on purpose, with a driving image: you do not teach steering first and care for others later. A human-centric approach means the user's best interest equals the best result; one bad direction can erase prior value. Impact requires monitoring of interactions, and intent is not enough — nobody plans the most careless build, yet harm still arrives without principles and practice. Three harm families follow. Hallucinations and fabrications: fluent nonsense, confident factual errors, contradictions, irrelevant filler. Harmful content: encouragement of self-harm, guidance for violent attacks, illegal instruction. Lack of fairness: exclusionary views and bias against groups. These extend the broader Microsoft responsible-AI principles into generative specifics.

Measurement comes before fixes. Prompt testing borrows from software testing: try diverse prompts, including ones that should go wrong, start by hand with samples, then automate at scale. Four mitigation layers sit above that base. Pick the right model — not always the strongest; a smaller task-fit model often behaves more safely. Add a safety system such as Azure AI content filtering with scores and metrics, observed through Prompt Flow rather than eyeballing single replies. Write a meta prompt: standing rules and grounding that bound behavior to the use case. Shape the experience: tell users they face a model and validate inputs before anything reaches it. Security then gets its OWASP Top 10 frame with three details. Prompt injection crafts inputs to force unintended behavior, bias, or hateful text — counter with validation, logging of inputs and replies, and watchlists. Supply-chain weakness hides in fast-moving packages, plugins touching confidential stores, and poisoned training data — counter by verifying or building plugins, staying current, and red-teaming with adversarial tests. Overreliance means trusting fluent output too far — counter with user education and skepticism, output verification and monitoring, and use-case testing before launch.

Key definitions.
Hallucination
Fluent but false, nonsensical, or irrelevant model output.
Prompt testing
Diverse trial prompts, starting by hand, then automated at scale.
Meta prompt
Standing rules and grounding that bound a build to its use case.
Prompt injection
Crafted input forcing behavior the builder did not intend.
Red-teaming
Adversarial testing of a build before users arrive.
Procedure
  1. List one harm per family for your pilot task.
  2. Run ten diverse prompt tests by hand; keep the failures.
  3. Apply one fix per layer, then name your OWASP watch item.
Obvious example. Filter blocking instructions that enable wrongdoing before they reach readers.
Counter-intuitive example. A smaller model chosen for safety beating a giant on a narrow brief.
Edge case. A trusted plugin with stale credentials leaking a confidential table through a helper.
Analogy card. A lab safety stack: right vessel, fume hood, written protocol, and clear labels for everyone at the bench.
What people get wrongThat good intent suffices. The session insists on testing, layers, and monitoring as the actual proof.
Why this matters. Trust is the product; one unhandled failure costs more than ten good drafts earn.
Leads to Experiment 7: grounding answers in your data is itself a safety move.
Memory: M-S-M-U — Model, Safety, Meta-prompt, UX
Recall prompt. Name the four mitigation layers in order, then reveal.
Right model, safety system, meta prompt, user-experience guards.

7. Grounded search with RAG

The session calls this the hello world of building: RAG, or retrieval augmented generation. A model trained months ago cannot know last week's release, and its cutoff date bounds it. RAG works around that without retraining. Retrieval pulls related material for this user and this question — a knowledge base, a document, a code repository, a spreadsheet. Augmentation places that material into the prompt, which is the full package you send the model, with an instruction such as answer only from the supplied passages. Generation is the model reading the question against the passages and drafting a bounded reply. Delivery returns that reply to the user. Because the bounds are explicit, the experience feels personal without pretending the model memorized your holdings.

The telecom example carries the pattern. A customer asks for iPhone help. A search service sits between the chat and the sources: files, databases, the product catalog, customer and purchase records. The model sees that the latest purchase was an iPhone 15, checks current catalog entries, and adds dynamic facts about that account — not static training memory but live, relevant rows. A sharp audience question follows: does the model retain prompt data? No. Across sessions it keeps nothing by itself; in cloud setups the data rests in your store and you re-supply the relevant slice each turn through search. That is why RAG repeats per request instead of teaching once. For your pilot, the move is narrow: one source set, one search path, one instruction to stay inside the passages, and one log so you can see what was retrieved when an answer disappoints.

Key definitions.
RAG
Retrieval augmented generation: answers bounded to retrieved passages.
Retrieval
Pulling the relevant slice of your sources per user and question.
Augmentation
Placing passages into the prompt with a stay-inside instruction.
Cutoff date
The training-data limit that grounding with fresh sources works around.
Procedure
  1. Choose one source set and one test question set.
  2. Retrieve top passages, then augment with a stay-inside instruction.
  3. Generate, deliver, and log passages with each answer.
Obvious example. Policy helper answering only from the current handbook version.
Counter-intuitive example. Telling a smart model to use less knowledge — only the passages — to raise trust.
Edge case. A just-shipped product the base model never saw, answered correctly from catalog rows.
Analogy card. An open-book test with a strict librarian: the librarian fetches pages, you may cite only those pages.
What people get wrongThat pasted data teaches the model permanently. It lasts for that request only; retention needs your store plus repeat retrieval.
Why this matters. Grounding is the fastest way to cut confident errors on your own facts.
Leads to Experiment 8: once answers cite your data, you can grant careful actions.
Memory: R-A-G-D — Retrieve, Augment, Generate, Deliver
Recall prompt. Expand RAG and state what the R retrieves, then reveal.
Retrieval augmented generation. R retrieves passages for this user and question.

Experiment 8 · Source 51:36–55:57

8. Agents that act with tools

An agent, as the session defines it, is a model plus permissioned access to tasks and tools so it can act, not only answer. The contrast opens with Eliza, the 1960s chat program shown in a 2005 implementation: you type, it replies. Modern chat looks similar on the surface. Agency changes what happens between the lines. Asked to schedule a meeting, a chat-only model returns an agenda; an agent can check the calendar in Teams, propose times, and — with your approval setting — book the slot. Orchestration helpers such as Semantic Kernel and LangChain supply this pattern: hand the model a tool such as web search, take the tool result back into the model, and report to the user. Approval may sit with you each time, or you may grant bounded autonomy with visible results.

Two research directions round out the picture, and the session marks their maturity honestly. Self-improvement, or re-prompting, asks the model to grade its own draft and improve it; work continues on how far this helps, and the session calls the framework side early. Multi-model teamwork assigns roles or purposes: the Autogen framework from Microsoft Research might set one model as product manager and another as developer to debate a feature list, often beating a single model told to play both parts, or it may let an orchestrator divide work among purpose-tuned specialists. The closing counsel fits the whole hour: agents grow more useful as frameworks mature, so explore with small permissions, visible logs, and human approval where stakes are real. Your first agent need not be grand — one calendar write or one search-then-draft loop, guarded like any pilot.

Key definitions.
Agent
A model plus approved tools that can act, with a visible trace.
Tool
A permissioned capability, such as calendar or search, the model may call.
Re-prompting
The model grading and improving its own draft.
Autogen
A research framework for role-based teamwork between models.
Procedure
  1. Grant one tool to one narrow task with approval on.
  2. Log the model call, the tool call, and the final reply.
  3. Widen permission only after ten clean runs.
Obvious example. Meeting request becomes a booked slot after calendar check and your confirm.
Counter-intuitive example. Two narrow models debating outperform one large model wearing two hats.
Edge case. A smoke alarm sounding mid-demo while the speaker keeps going — autonomy under noise.
Analogy card. A junior colleague with one key, a sign-out sheet, and a rule to knock before entering.
What people get wrongThat chat equals agency. Chat answers; agency books, searches, and files — with permission and trace.
Why this matters. Actions carry stakes that words alone do not; design approval first.
Connects back to Experiment 1: autonomy was the third pressure — agents are where you practice it.
Memory: T-S-M — Tools, Self-grade, Multi-role

FAQ

Questions the chat asked

Is Visual Studio Code enough?

Enough to start coding, yes. Not enough for data plumbing, hosting, evaluation, and monitoring — those need the wider stack from Experiment 3.

Where does the model sit in the stack?

Nowhere fixed. Teams travel up and down: start on a strong model with an orchestration helper, then swap layers for cost or fit. See Experiment 3.

Does pasted data stay in the model?

No. Each request carries its own passages; across sessions the model keeps nothing itself. Your store plus repeat retrieval does the remembering. See Experiment 7.

Which model for math like cubic roots?

The session declines one name and points to Hugging Face fine-tunes for narrow math, noting Mistral-family evaluations read well. Compare narrow tunings rather than defaulting large. See Experiment 4.

Must I always use the newest model?

No. Older models keep value on fitting tasks, and smaller task-fit models can behave more safely. Newness never leads the choice. See Experiments 4 and 6.
