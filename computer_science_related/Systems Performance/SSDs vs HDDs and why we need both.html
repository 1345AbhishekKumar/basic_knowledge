SSDs vs HDDs and why we need both.

Your code never touches the platter or the flash cell directly — yet the way you lay out reads and writes decides whether storage answers in microseconds or milliseconds. This lesson rebuilds both devices from their hardware characteristics, then follows them into queues, prices, caches, and the cloud.

01 / FOUNDATION
Why software people study spinning metal

Your code runs on the CPU. Its speed lives in the storage.

Bringing software closer to the hardware is the theme of Systems Performance by Brendan Gregg — an 880-page book this stream draws from. Four contrasts decide almost every storage decision.

Just-in-time terms: latency is how long one request waits before its first byte arrives. Throughput is how many bytes arrive per second once delivery starts. Random performance means fetching scattered pieces; sequential performance means reading one long run. Read vs write matters because the two directions take different paths and carry different costs on each device.

You control only the code the CPU (Central Processing Unit) executes. You do not control the platter spinning below. But the order in which your code issues storage characteristics — small scattered reads, one large sequential write, a burst of mixed requests — decides how much mechanical waiting the hardware must do. To understand the performance implications, consider what each component represents: a distinct physical structure with its own movement — spin plus swing on one side, electronic fetch on the other. Regardless of which device you use, that original structural difference is the inspiration for every latency visual in this lesson. High-performance programs in databases, queues, compilers, and operating systems win by matching their access pattern to the device characteristics underneath.

Contrast 01

Latency vs throughput

Latency is time-to-first-byte. Throughput is bytes-per-second after that. An HDD can hide slow starts behind strong streaming once its head arrives.

Contrast 02

Random vs sequential

Sequential reads one neighborhood. Random jumps between neighborhoods and pays positioning cost every jump — the gap that punishes HDDs most.

Contrast 03

Read vs write

Reads fetch what exists. Writes must place, and sometimes relocate, data. On SSDs a write can trigger erase-unit housekeeping reads never need.

Where this contrast is needed: opening a photo folder

You double-click a folder of 400 photos. They live on one drive. If the files were written back-to-back, one positioning motion serves all 400. If each photo landed wherever space was free, every photo pays its own positioning bill. Same bytes, wildly different wait — which is why layout, not just byte count, is a performance control.

02 / MECHANISM
Open the spinning drive

HDD: a magnetic platter, an ARM, and a controller that maps.

HDD stands for hard disk drive. Inside is real motion: billion-bit magnetic storage plus a swinging reader. Learn the parts and the latency that follows becomes obvious.

An HDD (hard disk drive) stores bits as magnetic polarity on one or several large spinning discs. Billions of tiny spots each hold what the system treats as a one or a zero, and long runs of them encode files, text, and video. The disc surface is divided into tracks — concentric rings, thousands or tens of thousands per device — and each track is divided into sectors, the small slots a single read or write addresses. Sector is the HDD-flavored word; SSD people usually say page for the same idea of a small fixed-size unit.

Beside the platter sits the controller: firmware plus an I/O interface that plugs into the motherboard I/O bus, a small NAND cache that answers repeats quickly, and the built-in queue plus scheduling logic. The moving reader is the ARM with a head at its tip. To read one spot, the platter must spin that spot under the head and the ARM must rotate so the head sits over the right track. The head then senses polarity for a read or flips it for a write, and the controller translates your file offset into that physical address and sends the bytes back over the bus.

ProblemThe wanted bytes sit near the rim. The head currently rests near the hub. What must happen?
KnowsPosition needs two motions: rotation of the ARM plus rotation of the platter.
ApproachWalk the request from file offset to motion to bytes.
StepsController maps the file to track plus sector. ARM swings outward to that track. Platter spins the sector under the head. Head senses polarity; controller sends bytes over the I/O bus.

WhyThe ARM is quick to watch yet slow beside a CPU: each swing is an eternity in cycles, so placement dominates cost.
PitfallForgetting the controller. Programs address files; the controller owns the map from file to track and sector, plus its NAND cache and queue. Two identical file reads can take different physical paths because the head started elsewhere.
MisconceptionMyth: "HDD reads are slow because magnets are slow." Correction: sensing is quick; getting there is slow — spinning the right sector around and swinging the ARM to the right track. Why the confusion happens: people picture the storage medium, not the two positioning motions in front of it.
03 / PERFORMANCE
Why the same file has two speeds

Sequential streams. Scattered seeks. Queues reorder both.

Once the head is parked and the sector is underneath, an HDD streams well. Getting there is the tax — and the on-device queue exists to pay less of it.

Path recap: software hands the request to the operating system, which may do its own queueing or buffering, then the request crosses the I/O bus — a trip measured in microseconds — and lands in the drive queue. If the queue is empty it goes straight to the controller. If the head already sits where it needs to be, service can finish in microseconds. If the platter must spin and the ARM must swing, service stretches into multiple milliseconds. That gap is why one file can show about 1 millisecond of latency today and about 10 milliseconds tomorrow: head position, device busyness, and queue contents all moved overnight.

Bursts make queues real. An HDD reads and writes only a few things at a time because physics caps parallelism, so a burst of five requests — call them A, B, C, D, E — piles up with one in service and four waiting. The drive can then run an elevator algorithm: like an elevator serving ten busy floors, it reorders stops to shorten total travel. If B and E sit side by side on one track, serving them together costs barely more than serving one, so E jumps ahead of C and D without penalizing them much. If A sits far away while B and E sit one track over from the current head, the scheduler may even deprioritize first-arrived A to finish the nearby pair first. Reordering breaks strict arrival order to buy throughput. For hands-on play with these tradeoffs, Sam Rose's interactive Encore article on queuing lets you run different queue policies and watch the backlog behave.

ProblemRead the first 100 bytes of file A today: 10 ms. Repeat tomorrow: 1 ms. Nothing in your program changed.
KnowsLatency includes head start position plus queue depth plus bus travel.

StepsDay one: head far away, four requests queued, long spin plus swing. Day two: head already parked nearby, queue empty, straight to controller and back.

04 / ECONOMICS
Why spinning rust refuses to die

Cost per gigabyte keeps HDDs everywhere.

Slower rarely wins — unless it is three to four times cheaper and the wait is acceptable. Track the price lines and the HDD stronghold explains itself.

Cost per gigabyte is total drive price divided by capacity. On that metric spinning drives still beat SSD (solid-state drive) devices by a wide margin, which is why object platforms such as Amazon S3, Backblaze-style stores, and household NAS (network-attached storage) arrays keep buying them: many terabytes, petabytes, even exabytes of data where waiting 10 milliseconds, 100 milliseconds, or a full second to start is fine.

The stream priced this on PCPartPicker, whose history graph spans March 2025 to July 2026. A small 2.5-inch 256 GB SATA SSD cost about $60 around Christmas and about $80 months later. A 1 TB SSD hovered just under $100 last July to August, spiked to almost $300 a month or two before the stream, then started easing down. A common 4 TB HDD moved from about $130 a year earlier to about $220. Even inflated, 4 TB of spinning storage at roughly $225–$230 faces a 4 TB NVMe SSD at roughly $800–$900 — about one third to one fourth the cost per gigabyte. The surge tracks the AI boom: GPUs, VRAM, RAM, and flash-adjacent supply all tightened, with RAM arguably hit hardest, in some cases doubling or tripling year over year before supply began catching up. The surrounding infrastructure story is primarily about manufacturing capacity: relatively fixed fabrication output met surging demand, so prices climbed across RAM, VRAM, flash, and spinning media alike.

At 4 TB the SSD costs roughly four times the HDD — multiply by petabytes and the choice is made.

Coldest corner: tape

Tape still survives for huge, rarely touched, mostly linear archives — petabytes to exabytes of cold storage where cost per dollar beats everything and sequential access is the plan. Worked example — price the 4 TB decision

ProblemKeep 4 TB online. HDD asks ~$230; NVMe SSD asks ~$850. Which premium buys what?
KnowsHDD cost per gigabyte ≈ one third to one fourth of SSD; SSD answers random reads far faster.
ApproachDivide price by gigabytes, then weigh the wait.
Steps230 ÷ 4000 ≈ $0.06 per GB for HDD. 850 ÷ 4000 ≈ $0.21 per GB for SSD. Ratio ≈ 3.7x. If the workload tolerates 10–100 ms starts, the cheaper tier holds; if phones and laptops need instant random reads, pay the premium.
ResultSame 4 TB, ~$620 apart, with the gap compounding at NAS and cloud scale.
WhyAt household terabytes the gap is visible; at organizational petabytes it decides the architecture.
PitfallPricing the purchase but not the years. Bits do not last forever on either medium: SSDs carry wear cycles, and cost per gigabyte per year — how soon a device must be replaced — can widen the SSD premium further. Add replacement cadence to the bill.
MisconceptionMyth: "For unplug-it-and-closet-it 10-year keeps, one answer is always best." Correction: powered versus unpowered aging, rewrite wear, and continuous-power advantages differ by medium, and the stream declines to crown a winner — the engineering runs deeper than price tags. Why the confusion happens: shelf life sounds like one property; it is several interacting ones.
05 / MECHANISM
Open the silent drive

SSD: no spin, many targets, parallelism on purpose.

The computer you are reading this on almost certainly boots from one. Same controller-and-queue idea as an HDD — with the mechanics deleted and parallelism added.

An SSD keeps bits in NAND flash — often 3D NAND flash stacked in layers — instead of on a magnet. Around the flash sits a controller that queues, schedules, consolidates I/O requests, and returns them over the bus. The capacity is split into separate targets (arrays or matrices of flash): the stream sketches eight across two rows, each able to hold on the order of 10 GB, 100 GB, or even a terabyte depending on cell density. Thin on-chip buses join every target to the controller, and in the simplified model the controller issues about one request per target at a time, waiting only microseconds — sometimes nanoseconds — when a target is briefly busy with another read or write.

Reads and writes move in pages of roughly 4 KB, 8 KB, or 16 KB at a time: ask for a page, get a page. A one-page file occupies one square; a three-page file occupies three neighbors; a two-page file and a single pink page fill other slots. Nothing spins, so latency collapses versus HDD — yet layout still matters. Packing a 100 GB video onto one target forces its pages to queue through one door. Striping the same file across three targets lets the controller fire three fetches at once and return them together: parallelism instead of sequence. For deeper physics the stream recommends the Branch Education video series on 3D NAND rather than compressing it here.

Garbage collection: the SSD version of tidying

HDDs fragment and later need defragmentation. SSDs cannot delete a lone page: pages group into an eraser unit (erase block) spanning many pages, so deleting means reading the survivors out, parking them safely elsewhere, then erasing the whole unit. Placement policy therefore also optimizes future garbage collection — fewer relocations later. For most application code the OS kernel plus drive firmware absorbs all of this; builders of very high-performance database systems are the ones who tune around it.

Worked example — the 100 GB video, two placements

ProblemA 100 GB video must read back quickly. Place it on one NAND target, or stripe it across three?
KnowsOne target serves about one request at a time; three free targets can serve together.
ApproachCompare serialization against parallelism, including queue waits.
StepsSingle-target: every page queues through one door and streams in turn. Striped: controller issues page 1 to A, page 2 to B, page 3 to C at once, then merges the returns; a busy target costs only a microsecond-scale wait.
ResultStriped reads overlap; single-target reads serialize. Minutes can separate the two at video scale.
WhyThroughput grows with open doors, not just faster doors.
PitfallDeleting one SSD page in your head like one file row. The erase unit is bigger than the write unit, so a tiny delete can move data first. Design delete-heavy flows with that asymmetry in mind.
MisconceptionMyth: "SSDs made layout irrelevant." Correction: they removed spin and swing, but target busyness, stripe width, and garbage-collection placement still reward good layout. Why the confusion happens: the biggest cost vanished, so the remaining smaller costs look like zero.
06 / SYSTEMS
Zoom out to the whole machine

Registers to S3: one machine, many cache layers.

Volatile or not, every store the CPU touches behaves like another cache level — faster and smaller near the core, slower and larger farther out.

Follow a byte the CPU wants. Closest sit CPU registers, the fastest perches inside the core. Next come the L1 cache, L2 cache, and L3 cache — small, very fast CPU caches that physically live on the processor so signals travel almost nowhere. A miss steps out to RAM (Random Access Memory), a separate larger store. Overflow further and an NVMe SSD acts as another layer: virtual memory spills RAM contents onto flash. Need more than the roughly 1 TB NVMe tier? Add a ~10 TB HDD tier. Still short? Add network storage — a 30 TB local array or effectively infinite cloud object storage such as Amazon S3 — as the outermost ring. Caching here means file-level or code-level reuse at every rung, which is why the whole volatile-plus-nonvolatile stack reads as one giant layered cache rather than separate worlds.

Modern packaging blurs the lines without erasing them. Apple-style system on a chip designs solder RAM near the processor on one board, so the diagram looks fused — yet the L1, L2, and L3 caches still sit on the CPU die itself because only that tight integration delivers their ultra-low latency. The reason everything cannot be L1 is transistor real estate: L1 occupies precious die area beside the cores, and dies are finite. If caches lived elsewhere on the motherboard, the wire delay alone would demote them.

ProblemThe core needs a byte that is in none of registers, L1, L2, or L3. Where next, and what does each step cost?
KnowsEach rung outward trades latency for capacity and cheaper gigabytes.
ApproachWalk outward until the hit.
StepsCheck RAM. On miss, check the NVMe tier where virtual memory spilled it. On miss, check the 10 TB HDD tier. On miss, fetch over the network from the NAS array or S3 object storage.

PitfallCalling only RAM a cache. The NVMe drive, the HDD, even S3 act as cache layers for hotter tiers above them. Size the whole ladder, not one rung.
MisconceptionMyth: "Nonvolatile means not-a-cache." Correction: persistence and caching are independent roles — an SSD can durably hold files and serve as overflow for RAM. Why the confusion happens: textbooks introduce caches with CPU diagrams, so larger stores feel like a different species.
07 / ARCHITECTURE
Use both at once

Tiered storage: fast little front, cheap giant back.

Keep the hot sliver in RAM and local flash; park the cold ocean behind them. Whether that wins depends on one workload signal: skew.

The hybrid question from the stream: should systems layer data between SSD and HDD intelligently, what signals choose the backend, and where does eager reshuffling belong? The working answer is tiered storage. Take TurboPuffer-style vector database search — nearest-similar-vector lookup standing in for any hot-cold database. Requests first probe RAM on one or several server compute instances: a hit returns immediately. On a miss they probe the larger local NVMe tier holding most-recently-used vectors. Only a miss there travels over the network to object storage such as Amazon S3 or Google Cloud Storage (GCS), where tens to hundreds of terabytes rest largely on spinning HDDs behind the scenes. The illustrated split: perhaps 80% served from RAM, then 80–90% of the remaining 20% served from local NVMe, leaving roughly one request in a hundred to pay the slow red path — and the returned bytes can then be promoted upward so the next identical lookup hits higher.

The signal that decides is request skew: when 90–99% of lookups touch a small slice that fits in RAM plus NVMe, tiers multiply value. When 100 TB of data is requested uniformly — any byte equally likely next — the miss rate stays punishing, the slow path fires constantly, and the extra machinery earns little. Eager reshuffling (promoting hot objects, demoting cold ones) belongs where the skew signal is measured: track recency and frequency at the top, move in the background, and never let migration block the read path.

Worked example — the 100 TB spread test

Problem100 TB total. Case A: 99% of reads hit 1 TB. Case B: reads spray evenly. Does tiering pay in each?
KnowsTiers win when the hot set fits above the slow store.
ApproachMultiply hit rates down the ladder.
StepsCase A: 80% RAM hit; 80–90% of the leftover 20% hits NVMe; ~1% reaches S3. Case B: most reads miss both upper tiers and pay network plus HDD latency every time.
ResultCase A flies; case B drags the whole ladder along for nearly every read.
WhyTiering is a bet on skew. No skew, no prize.
PitfallTiering without measuring. Add tiers only after recency and frequency counters prove a hot sliver exists; otherwise the promotion traffic itself becomes the load.
MisconceptionMyth: "More cache layers always help." Correction: each layer adds lookup, bookkeeping, and migration cost — uniform workloads pay all of it and collect almost none of it. Why the confusion happens: skewed demos look miraculous, and people generalize the miracle.
08 / CLOUD
Rent the same physics

Local NVMe, EBS, S3: speed, safety, and the bill.

Cloud vendors sell the same two media with different promises. Pick by answering: how fast, how safe, how big, how much?

On Amazon, EC2 instances supply compute; some instance types include a local NVMe disc — the fastest path, and also ephemeral: the instance can vanish at any moment from hardware failure or power loss, and unlike a desk machine you cannot walk up and pull the drive, so the data simply goes poof. Sizes are fixed and cannot grow arbitrarily. PlanetScale Metal keeps the speed while buying safety by running typically three copies — a primary plus replicas in different availability zones (separate buildings) — so every write fans out to several local NVMe drives and a dead server is replaced without data loss.

EBS (Elastic Block Store) is the network-attached volume service: create 100 GB, 200 GB, or 3 TB volumes through the API, attach one (say 1 TB) to an instance, and grow it later — shrinking is not offered. Older generations mixed media, but modern EBS is mostly SSD-backed, which reads as mostly SSD in the stream. Because every byte still crosses the network, typical latency lands sub-millisecond yet sometimes surfaces at millisecond or multi-millisecond scale, and throughput is gated by IOPS (I/O operations per second) limits that money unlocks as bandwidth. S3 object storage sits one rung out: single files to millions of objects, reachable from anywhere, replicated many ways for high durability — powered substantially by tens of millions of spinning HDDs with SSD caching in front, holding exabytes for thousands to millions of customers, slower yet very reliable and far cheaper per gigabyte. The stream ballparks 1 TB in S3 at a couple of dollars a month against about $100 a month for a 1 TB EBS volume — roughly an order of magnitude apart. Google Colossus shows the hybrid endgame: mix slower and faster devices under a predictive placement algorithm that parks expected-hot bytes on fast media and the rest on slow media. S3 very likely hides a similar SSD front for hot objects, though vendors keep those internals private. On AWS, the requester talks to APIs that dynamically provision capacity behind one call, which hides real complexity — including the distributed replication described in the All Things Distributed write-up on S3 — but never removes its latency or its bill.

  7. Cost per gigabyte — price divided by capacity; spinning storage runs about one third to one fourth of flash. The 4 TB math — ~$230 versus ~$850 — is why S3, Backblaze patterns, and NAS stay magnetic.Click to reveal
  8. SSD — a solid-state drive: NAND targets behind a controller, no spin or swing. Electronic fetch collapses latency; layout still decides parallelism and cleanup.Click to reveal
  9. Page — the SSD read/write unit of roughly 4, 8, or 16 KB. Files are built from pages; videos from thousands of them.Click to reveal
  10. Striping — spreading one file across several NAND targets so fetches fly in parallel. Three doors open at once beats one fast door opened three times.Click to reveal
  11. Eraser unit — the multi-page block an SSD must erase whole, forcing relocate-then-erase deletes. Small deletes can move data first; that asymmetry shapes write-heavy design.Click to reveal
  12. RAM — Random Access Memory, the large fast tier outside the CPU that catches L1–L3 misses. It is the first rung of the outer ladder, spilling to NVMe through virtual memory.Click to reveal
  13. Tiered storage — hot RAM plus local NVMe fronting a cold HDD or object back, promoted by recency. It pays only under skew: ~99% of hits absorbed above, ~1 in 100 paying the slow path.Click to reveal
  14. EBS — Elastic Block Store: growable network SSD volumes with IOPS limits. SSD speed minus a network hop, metered — sub-millisecond typical, milliseconds possible.Click to reveal
  15. S3 — object storage: a replicated, mostly HDD-backed ocean with SSD caching, holding exabytes. Roughly an order of magnitude cheaper per gigabyte than EBS, and slower to first byte.Click to reveal

Still confused?

FAQ — edge cases learners keep asking about

Where does the queue actually live?

In several places at once. The operating system keeps its own queues and buffers, and the drive keeps an on-device queue with its own scheduler. A request can be reordered at either layer, so end-to-end latency reflects both. Remember: two layers of waiting, two chances to batch.

Why can the same read take 1 ms once and 10 ms the next day?

Because the start state moved. Head parked nearby plus an empty queue answers near the microsecond floor; head far away plus a burst of A–E queued ahead forces extra spins, swings, and waits. The takeaway: report storage latency as a range over states, never as one heroic number.

What actually happens when I delete from an SSD?

Not a single-page erase. The controller must protect the surviving pages in that eraser unit: read them out, park them elsewhere, then erase the whole block. That extra motion is garbage collection, and placement policy exists largely to keep it cheap. Remember: writes are page-sized, erases are block-sized.

Are L1, L2, and L3 separate chips I can upgrade?

No. They are CPU caches built onto the processor die for ultra-low latency, with registers even closer. RAM is the separate upgradable tier; system-on-a-chip boards solder it nearby but the cache layers stay on-die. Remember: proximity is the performance, so caches live where the wires are shortest.

Which signals say tiering will pay?

Measured skew: recency plus frequency counters showing 90–99% of reads landing on a slice that fits in RAM plus local NVMe. Uniform 100 TB spray is the red flag — misses dominate and migration traffic becomes pure overhead. Remember: measure hot-set fit first, add tiers second.

Why not always use local NVMe in the cloud?

Because ephemeral means losable: a dead host takes unreplicated bytes with it, and sizes are fixed. Teams that live on local flash replicate — typically three copies across availability zones — or they accept EBS network latency and S3 durability economics instead. Remember: speed without copies is a gamble, not an architecture.
