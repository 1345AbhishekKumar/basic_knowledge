Module 01

The Age of Taste

Why taste becomes the scarce resource when building is nearly free — and why Mosseri is “pretty long on designers.”

Ask Mosseri what survives as AI eats the product development lifecycle and he answers in one word: taste. “In a world where it's easier to build things, it's more important to make sure that your time is spent figuring out what you should be building in the first place.” Anyone can ship now; almost no one can reliably choose. That asymmetry is the whole argument of the episode.

It also explains a counterintuitive bet: while designers are anxious about being replaced by generalists doing design, Mosseri says he's “pretty long on designers” — because designers tend to have taste, and taste is “much more difficult to imagine being automated away.” The catch: the designers who thrive won't stay in traditional roles. They'll have strong opinions on product strategy, business, and go-to-market — becoming the strongest “product staff” of all.

You can already see the fingerprint of taste in AI output. The episode's hosts riff on “vibe coding” — you can look at an app and say “oh, that's a Cursor app” or “that's a Claude app,” the way you can identify a designer's hand. When every tool produces a recognizable aesthetic, taste is what lets you notice the sameness — and push past it. That's why the best product leaders, Mosseri argues, are less prolific idea machines and more curators: curators of people, of ideas, of technologies, of strategies. “It's hard to be a great curator if you don't have some of your own ideas,” but the great ones create environments where good ideas bubble up — and then choose.

The episode's darkest frame is the reverse centaur (a concept Mosseri credits to writer Cory Doctorow): a centaur is a human in charge riding a powerful body; a reverse centaur is the horse's head on a human body — the AI deciding, the human doing its bidding. Mosseri's warning: don't assume that because you can outsource a workflow to AI, you should. Some are win-wins; some are risks that outweigh the benefits.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Building gets cheap. AI collapses the cost of making; anyone can ship a product in a weekend.
  2. Taste becomes the bottleneck. The scarce skill is deciding what deserves to exist at all.
  3. Recognize the sameness. “Vibe coding” makes AI output identifiable — and generic. Taste notices.
  4. Leaders become curators. Great product leaders stop generating everything and start choosing the best of what bubbles up.
  5. Watch the centaur's head. Keep the human in charge; avoid the reverse centaur where AI steers and humans obey.

  • Edge case —Uber and DoorDash drivers, in Mosseri's framing, already live in a reverse centaur: the algorithm decides the route, the work, the pay — and the human executes.

If every kitchen could cook anything for free, the bottleneck isn't cooking — it's knowing what's worth serving.

Taste is the critic's skill: discriminating among infinite possibilities. AI gives everyone infinite ovens; it can't give them the critic's palate. That's the whole bet of the episode.

“AI makes taste obsolete because AI can generate anything.” The opposite: when anyone can generate anything, the value moves upstream to selection. Mosseri's exact words: “taste matters a ton.”

Module 02

Pods & Product Staff

From a baker's dozen of specialists to a six-person pod — and the new role that blends PM, design, data, and research.

For years, the canonical team at a big company looked like this: two or three Android engineers, two or three iOS engineers, two or three server engineers, maybe a generalist, a PM, a designer, a data scientist, a researcher — “on the order of a baker's dozen.” The logic was coverage: you needed code reviewers, specialists, and representatives of every function. This year, Mosseri says, that changed.

Instagram adopted pods: mini teams of four to six engineers who are “a bit more generalist,” plus one product staff — “an evolution of the PM”: someone who can do some of what a designer does, some of what a data scientist does, and some of what a researcher does, by leveraging the latest AI tools. Specialists are pulled in only when the work demands them: pricing work needs a senior data scientist; a genuinely novel experience needs a very senior product designer. The core ends up “six or seven usually.”

Why does it work? Fewer people to coordinate means faster decisions and less design by committee. It's not only AI productivity — “small teams often are just more effective.” And the functions are bleeding into each other. A classic waterfall analysis of, say, reel creation — where people drop off at each step — used to require bespoke data-scientist work; now internal tools can pull it automatically, so a product staff can do what they couldn't a year ago.

Hiring shifts accordingly. The baseline never changes: grit (“fire in your belly”), being a quick learner, and being very self-aware so you can take feedback. If you have those three, “you can kind of get good at anything eventually.” The premiums for the next decade: curiosity and willingness to put yourself out there — Mosseri compares it to learning a language: you have to be willing to sound like an idiot, get corrected, and improve. Meanwhile, roles managing thousands of people get rarer; the future is smaller teams.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Old model: the baker's dozen. A team sized for coverage — every function represented, code reviewers for every writer.
  2. New model: pods. Four to six generalist engineers + one product staff; specialists on demand.
  3. Product staff replaces PM. A generalist who can touch design, data, and research using AI tools.
  4. Smaller core, faster decisions. Six or seven people; less coordination, less design by committee.
  5. Hire for the new shape. Grit + quick learning + self-awareness; then curiosity and the willingness to try things publicly.
Idle

Three Examples

  • Obvious —Waterfall analysis: a classic funnel question about reel creation now runs automatically, so a product staff can do it without a data scientist.
  • Counter-intuitive —AI is resetting who performs: engineering used to be 40–60% writing code; now it's mostly planning and reviewing — so people who loved writing code (or were slow at it) flip outcomes.
  • Edge case —Mosseri himself: a designer who had to pass a technical loop at Facebook, then didn't program for a decade — and now “can write code responsibly” again because the tools changed the job.
Analogy · A band instead of an orchestra

An orchestra needs one specialist per instrument. A band's members all sing, play, and write a bit — and they improvise.

Pods are bands: smaller, faster, and better at improvising. You bring in a session musician (senior specialist) only when a song genuinely needs a virtuoso.
What People Get Wrong (expand)
“AI is replacing specialists with juniors.” Mosseri's actual take: functions survive, but shaped differently — mostly senior people, with a pipeline mentoring tomorrow's seniors. Those who aren't on the senior track move into generalist roles: “a reasonable soft landing.”

Module 03

The Algorithm: What Instagram Actually Knows

Embeddings are inscrutable numbers, not semantic understanding — and LLMs are finally making them legible.

Asked what the Instagram algorithm knows about human behavior that people don't realize, Mosseri flips the assumption: people assume the algorithm has a detailed semantic understanding of their interests. It mostly doesn't. The progress in recommenders over the last decade came from large embedding models — giant vectors of numbers that “cannot be read by people.” “It knows I like surfing… it doesn't. It just has this big-ass number that happens to correlate with surfing.”

What changed recently is that LLMs can finally describe those illegible artifacts. Think of embedding space as a map: videos that are similar sit close together. Now you can ask an LLM to look at a region of the map and it says, “oh, that's deep pourover coffee snobbery.” Instagram's new “your algorithm” feature does exactly this — it shows you the topics the system thinks you're interested in, lets you add and remove them, and gives people “some agency back” in a world where social feeds are run by recommendations. It's still early: you can't yet say “more fun content” or “fewer photos of my high-school friends' kids,” but that's the direction.

Why not just give people a chronological feed? Two problems. First, incentives: with pure chronology, the winning strategy is to post as much as possible, so feeds get overwhelmed by high-volume professional publishers — the New York Times can pump out fifty posts a day while your best friend posts once a week. Designing these systems is “almost like designing a city”: you must think about the mechanics, the incentives that arise, and what people do within them. Second, recency isn't everything: “recency is an important input into relevance, but it's not the only one.” Mosseri would rather see his sister's engagement than his brother's poboy sandwich — even if the sandwich is newer. When Instagram has run chronological-by-default experiments, usage and long-run satisfaction both go down.

Finally, ranking is a trade-off machine. Exploitation shows you more of what you already like; it's easy and predictable. Exploration tests content you might like but don't know yet — harder, but it's how small and niche creators break out. TikTok's recommender is famous for the latter, and Mosseri credits it as inspiration for Instagram's push to increase originality and breakout content.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Interests become vectors. Embedding models turn behavior into giant, unreadable numbers.
  2. LLMs make the map legible. Ask a model to describe a region of embedding space and it names the cluster: “deep pourover coffee snobbery.”
  3. Give people agency. “Your algorithm” shows and edits the inferred topics — a first step back from pure recommendation.
  4. Chronology fails at scale. Post-everything incentives flood the feed; recency beats relevance for publishers but not people.
  5. Balance exploitation and exploration. Exploitation grows engagement; exploration breaks new creators — and they trade off.

Module 04

AI Content: Tailwind, with a Challenge

More synthetic content, more authenticity — why Mosseri thinks AI is a tailwind, and how he'd label it.

Is the rise of AI content a headwind or a tailwind for Instagram? Mosseri's answer: a tailwind, but a challenge. More content means more attention — Instagram is an attention business — but “we're not very good at ranking AI content yet.” There's great AI content and crap AI content, and the ranking problem is separating them.

The deeper bet is about power. For years, Mosseri says, power has been shifting from institutions to individuals — the easiest example is sports, where players are now more relevant than teams. In a world of abundant synthetic content, “people are going to seek out creativity and authenticity and people more, not less.” That favors the largest creator platform — which Instagram is. “Instagram was never just about the content. It was always… the person behind the content, the point of view.”

His policy stance is deliberately anti-censorship: “I don't think we should judge content based on the tool that made it.” You shouldn't filter AI content out; you should let people know it's AI. That's harder than it sounds — detection may work today, but as models improve “we might lose the ability to detect that.” So the honest move is to report confidence: definitely AI, probably AI, not sure. Mosseri even suggests it may be more practical long-term to label camera-captured content (non-AI) rather than AI content, and to label at the account level too — because the real abuse vector is fake accounts: the “AI monk” selling bogus supplements, impersonating a vibe to scam people. You should know whether an account is a real person, and how old it is, so you can decide whom to trust.

And what does the head of Instagram actually enjoy? “Plastic Dream Sequence” — AI videos of doll-like figures singing, weird and clearly AI, with “a very clear creative and aesthetic point of view.” And “If Only AI,” a Paris-based artist who chains models together to build dreamscapes. His test for good AI content: does it have a point of view?

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Accept the flood. More content = more attention; the challenge is ranking it, not blocking it.
  2. Bet on authenticity. Power moves from institutions to individuals; people seek creators, viewpoints, and people.
  3. Judge the content, not the tool. Don't filter AI; label it — with honest confidence levels.
  4. Consider labeling “camera-captured.” As detection degrades, it may be easier to mark non-AI than AI.
  5. Police the accounts, not the aesthetics. The real threat is fake accounts and spam — “AI monks” selling supplements — so give people identity signals.
Idle

Three Examples

  • Obvious —Labeling: “Is this AI?” should be answerable — “we think it probably is, or we're not sure, or it definitely is.”
  • Counter-intuitive —The protectionist-sounding move is actually the opposite: don't filter AI content — filter the deception, by making camera-captured content the labeled default.
  • Edge case —The “AI monk” spam account: an AI-generated persona pushing bogus supplements. The threat isn't the tool — it's identity fraud, which is why account-level signals (age, history) matter.
Analogy · Labeling ingredients, not banning recipes

You don't ban foods made with a certain machine — you put the ingredients on the label and let people decide.

Same principle: label what content is, be honest about confidence, and make the person behind it visible. Informed people, not filters, are the defense.
What People Get Wrong (expand)
“The answer to AI content is to filter it out.” Mosseri disagrees on principle: judging content by its tool is a value judgment he won't make. The answer is transparency — and betting that authenticity becomes more valuable, not less.

Module 05

Strategy & Judgment: Where Humans Stay Valuable

Vision as a destination, strategy as a controversial path, and why AI strategy needs aggressive steering.

As AI eats more of the product lifecycle, Mosseri's answer to where human brains stay valuable is taste and judgment — particularly around strategy. He defines the terms sharply. Vision is “an articulation of the world or the state of the product you want to get to.” Strategy is “an opinionated path to achieve that vision.” And a strategy that can't be argued with isn't a strategy: “it has to be controversial — a reasonable person should be able to disagree with it — because otherwise you're probably just trying to compete on raw execution.”

What about asking AI for strategy? Mosseri is skeptical of the lazy version. You might think: give the model the market, competitors, metrics, and growth, and let it win. “I think it could be. I have found it's not unless you steer it pretty aggressively.” Strategy requires weighing the state of the technology, the people on the team and what motivates them, the talent magnet of an idea on the bubble, the competitive and regulatory landscape, the brand's identity. An unsteered AI returns “something pretty predictable that probably the competition would expect you to do.” The effective version is a conversation: enumerate the inputs, make the model consider them, and tell it to be critical. And pick a model that pushes back — “I don't want the pleaser.”

Budgeting is its own judgment problem. Meta once had a “leaderboard of token spend” — Mosseri: “It's a terrible idea.” They've since cut the “token incinerators,” and there are no token limits today. But he expects that to change: “the burn rate of a strong engineer might be the same as their salary,” and caps will probably come — proportional to trust in someone's ability to use tokens in an ROI-positive way. Prices will roller-coaster: usage goes up, then frontier models enter a pricing war. And remember Kevin Weil's line: “This is the worst the model will ever be.”

The through-line: humans should set goals, define constraints, and give feedback — “that is its own craft.” Too prescriptive and you stifle ideas; too open-ended and teams waste time. The same autonomy question will apply to agents.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Separate vision from strategy. Vision is the destination; strategy is an opinionated, arguable path there.
  2. Steer the AI hard. Feed it the real inputs — team, talent, regulation, brand — or you get the predictable answer your competitors would expect.
  3. Make it a conversation. Back-and-forth beats one-shot; demand a model that pushes back.
  4. Budget like any resource. No leaderboards; watch for token incinerators; expect caps proportional to trust when burn ≈ salary.
  5. Keep the human loop. Set goals, constraints, and feedback — the craft of giving just enough autonomy.

  • Edge case —The “token incinerator”: an engineering project that burns compute and creates no value. The discipline isn't capping tokens; it's killing incinerators and, later, capping by trust.
Analogy · A GPS with opinions

Give a GPS the destination and it proposes a route. The predictable route everyone takes is the one traffic knows to avoid.

The AI is the GPS: fast at routing, blind to the messy inputs a human sees (who's on the team, what's on the bubble, what regulators will tolerate). Judgment is choosing the route that's defensible precisely because it's debatable.
What People Get Wrong (expand)
“AI should be amazing at strategy because it has all the data.” Mosseri tried it: without aggressive steering, you get the predictable move the competition expects. Strategy is a conversation with constraints, not a prompt.

Module 06

Leading Through the Fire

The public face of Instagram's hardest moments — from the 2009 News Feed redesign to the 2022 backlash — and the lessons inside the failures.

Mosseri didn't ask to be the face of controversy; he walked into it. It started with the 2009 News Feed redesign at Facebook, when he was an entry-level designer. The very first comment was vicious — homophobic, antisemitic, aimed at the anonymous designer who'd “rearranged” everyone's digital home. Devastated at 25, he found his way to empathy: if you'd spent years at your desk, organized your photos and letters there, and someone rearranged it without warning or explanation, “you would be pissed, and that would be reasonable.” That perspective — millions of people, all reasonable in their frustration — became his operating frame.

He chose to show up where the critics were: Twitter, where journalists lived, “to show some humility on their turf.” It was “the darkest place in my life” — he followed all his biggest critics — but he kept at it, because “people are fundamentally more afraid of things they don't understand.” His debate style follows: he's not trying to convert anyone; he's enumerating the trade-offs for everyone watching.

The 2022 feed backlash taught the scale lesson. Four things got conflated — a video-first test shown to 4% of iOS users that was never going to launch, a push into reels, more recommendations, and creators angry about reach — and the echo chamber between creators and press multiplied it. His takeaway wasn't “don't test.” It was: don't push too fast, and prepare the story in advance. “Not if it leaks — when it leaks.” At three billion users, every test is public.

Fail corner is where he's most candid. Facebook Home — a fork of Android with an HTC handset — was “a spectacular failure,” yet he learned more in that year than any other, and sometimes the best thing you can do is “execute an idea that doesn't have market fit well, just to decide whether the idea was a good idea.” And the first version of Reels, built on Stories in 2019, was the wrong foundation — most Stories go unseen, so most Reels were never seen. If the 2020 version had shipped in 2019, he believes TikTok wouldn't be what it is. “We created a lot of economic opportunity in the world allowing TikTok to grow.”

And at home: his kids are 10, 8, and 6, too young for social media. They have iPads, and they earn their time — three half-hour homework sessions buys 90 weekend minutes. He approves every app. He wants them “digitally literate, AI literate” — his 10-year-old is already vibe-coding with Cloud Code. Boundaries, earned access, and literacy: the same values he asks of the platform, applied to his own kitchen table.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Get hit early. The 2009 redesign's first comment taught the desk-rearrangement lesson: understand the user's reasonable anger.
  2. Show up on their turf. Engage critics where they live; people fear what they don't understand.
  3. Prepare for the leak. “Not if it leaks, when it leaks” — have the story ready before the test ships.
  4. Own the failures. Facebook Home and Reels-on-Stories: execute bad ideas to learn, and fix the foundation before scaling.
  5. Apply it at home. Earned screen time, approved apps, and AI literacy for his own kids.
Idle

Three Examples

  • Counter-intuitive —Facebook Home was a spectacular failure that he's glad he ran — executing a bad idea is how you learn whether it was a good idea.
  • Edge case —The 2022 backlash was four conflated things amplified by a creator-press echo chamber; the real lesson wasn't “don't test” but “don't push too fast, and prepare for the leak.”
Analogy · A lighthouse keeper in a storm

You can't stop the waves; you can keep the light on and the charts honest.

Mosseri's playbook: keep showing up, explain the trade-offs, prepare the story before it leaks, and own the failures. The storm is part of the job at three billion users.
What People Get Wrong (expand)
“Controversy means you did something wrong.” Sometimes yes, sometimes no — Mosseri insists he's been criticized fairly and unfairly. His standard isn't avoiding backlash; it's being able to say what you did, why, and what the trade-offs were, while remembering “we are people here trying to make these decisions.”

08Frequently Asked Questions

Is AI really going to make taste more important, not less?
That's Mosseri's core argument: as the cost of building collapses, the bottleneck shifts to deciding what to build. He's “pretty long on designers” precisely because taste — the ability to discriminate and choose — is the hardest thing to automate, even as the mechanical parts of every role get easier.
What exactly is a “product staff” role?
An evolution of the product manager: a generalist who can do some design, some data science, and some research using the latest AI tools, paired with a pod of 4–6 generalist engineers. Deep specialists get drafted in only when the work genuinely needs them — e.g., a senior data scientist for pricing.
Does Instagram's algorithm really understand what I like?
Not semantically — it runs on embedding vectors that correlate with your behavior but can't be read directly. What's new is that LLMs can now describe regions of that embedding space in words, which is what powers the “your algorithm” feature that lets you see and edit inferred topics.
Why not just give everyone a chronological feed?
At Instagram's scale, pure chronology rewards posting volume — the New York Times posts 50 times a day and buries your friends — and recency isn't the same as relevance (sister's engagement beats a newer poboy sandwich). In experiments, chronological-by-default drove usage and long-run satisfaction down.
Should AI content be banned from the feed?
Mosseri says no: don't judge content by the tool that made it. Label it (with honest confidence), consider labeling camera-captured content as the default, and police deceptive accounts instead. The real threats are fake accounts and spam — the “AI monk selling supplements” — not AI art with a point of view.

09Next Steps

Three ways to apply what you just learned.

  1. Run the taste test on your own work. Pick a project you're building or planning. List what's now easy (execution, generation) and what's still hard (selection, strategy). Write your strategy down — then check: could a reasonable person disagree with it? If not, it's not a strategy.
  2. Try the steering experiment. Take a real decision and ask an AI for a strategy with no constraints. Then redo it with explicit inputs — team, constraints, audience, risks — and ask it to argue with you. Notice the difference between the predictable answer and the steered one.
  3. Audit your own feed dial. For the next week, notice the trade-offs in your own algorithm: what exploitation keeps showing you, and what exploration has surprised you with. Ask what “your algorithm” thinks you're into — and whether you agree.
