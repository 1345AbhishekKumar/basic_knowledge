What Is Generative AI

Your goal: understand how generative AI actually works — not as magic, not as a monster, but as a machine that predicts the next word. This lecture walks the past (Google Translate, Siri), the present (GPT-4, language models, fine-tuning), and the future (scale, alignment, risks). This lesson turns it into six ideas you can hold: what it is, why it exploded, the prediction engine, the training recipe, scale and alignment, and the honest risk picture.

Issue 01Generative AI Is Not New

From transcript 00:00:46 · 00:05:07 (definition, Translate, Siri, autocomplete)

1. The AI You Already Use

Break the name in half. Artificial intelligence is a fancy way of saying: get a computer programme to do a job a human would otherwise do. Generative is the fun bit: the computer creates new content. It has not necessarily seen that exact content before — it has seen parts of it, and it synthesises those parts into something new. The new content can be audio, computer code, an image, text like an email or essay, or video. This lecture focuses on text, because that is natural language processing, but the same idea runs through all of it.

Here is the myth-busting headline: generative AI is not a new concept. It has been around for years and is already part of your phone. Google Translate launched in 2006 — 17 years of Greek text in, English text out, and nobody made a fuss. Siri launched in 2011 and was a sensation — ask Siri to set an alarm and it talks back; that is generative AI, less sophisticated than ChatGPT but the same family. Then came Amazon Alexa. And every day you use it without noticing: the phone completing your email or text, Google guessing your search term before you finish typing. That is language modelling — making predictions about what the continuation is going to be.

So the real question is not “what is this new thing?” It is: what changed, and why is everyone suddenly talking? The tools were always generative; the difference is how much they can now do.

  1. AI = computer does a human job.
  2. Generative = synthesises parts it has seen into new content.
  3. Output can be text, code, image, audio, or video.
  4. Google Translate (2006), Siri (2011), Alexa, autocomplete: all generative AI.
ObviousGoogle Translate: Greek in, English out — new text each time.
Counter-intuitiveYour phone’s text autocomplete is generative AI you already trust.
Edge caseAutocomplete “thinks like you” — it predicts continuations, not facts.
Analogy: A chef who has tasted many dishes but cooks you a new one — generative means new, built from parts it has seen. It creates new output from learned pieces; it doesn’t replay a recording.
What people get wrong

That generative AI started with ChatGPT. Translate, Siri, Alexa, and your phone’s autocomplete were all doing it for years — ChatGPT is a leap in capability, not a new category.

Issue 02GPT-4: What Changed

From transcript 00:05:12 · 00:08:17 (GPT-4, prompts, 100M users)

2. The Moment Everything Got Loud

In 2023, OpenAI — a company in San Francisco — announced GPT-4 and claimed it could beat 90% of humans on the SAT (the standardised admissions test American students take for university, and not an easy one). They also showed it getting top marks in law exams, medical exams, and a whole suite of others. Passing exams is one thing; the point is what a normal person can now ask it to do.

Enter the prompt — the little instruction that says what the human wants the tool to do. Real prompts from the lecture: “I’m writing an essay about the use of mobile phones during driving — give me three arguments in favour”; “Act as a JavaScript developer — write a programme that checks a form (name and email required, address and age not)”; “Create an About Me page for a website — I like rock climbing, outdoor sports, and programming.” Give it the specification and it produces the thing. That is the leap: from single-purpose tools to one tool that writes text, code, and web pages on request.

And the world noticed instantly. The lecture shows the race to 100 million users: Google Translate took 78 months, TikTok took 9, and ChatGPT took two. Two months, and users pay a little to use it — do the multiplication yourself. That speed is why “the fuss” happened: not a new category, but a generational jump in what the same old principle can do.

  2. A prompt is the human’s instruction: the task, in words.
  3. Prompts produce essays, working code, and complete web pages.
  4. ChatGPT hit 100M users in 2 months (Translate 78, TikTok 9).

Issue 03The Engine: Language Modelling

From transcript 00:09:17 · 00:12:22 (predictions, likelihood, why they fail)

3. Context In, Next Word Out

All these models — GPT and its many variants, plus academic models nobody has heard of — work on the same principle: language modelling. It assumes you have a sequence of words, the context so far, and it predicts what comes next. That is all. The lecture’s toy example: given the context “I want to,” the model offers several predictions — shovel, play, swim, eat. Pick one and there are more continuations: shovel → snow; play → tennis or video; eat → fruit. The “tree” of language branches as you go.

Now imagine the computer has seen a huge amount of text and knows which words follow which. We used to count: download data, count how often “I want to” appears and what follows it. That counting approach is gone — neural networks don’t exactly count; they learn in a more sophisticated way. But the task is unchanged: given the context, predict the most likely continuation. That is also the prompt — the prompt is just the context, and the model does “what comes next”: three arguments, a webpage, whatever the task asks.

And here is the honest catch that explains many failures: likelihood matters. These models are trained to output the most likely next word. Sometimes the answer you actually wanted is the less likely one — so the model gives you something reasonable-but-wrong, or wordy, or off-target. It is not being clever or malicious; it is doing exactly what it was trained to do: predict the most probable continuation. The lecture’s example: “The colour of the sky is…” — the model fills in the most likely ending, not necessarily the one you wanted.

  1. Context in: “I want to…”
  2. Model predicts: shovel, play, swim, eat.
  3. Each choice branches to further continuations.
  4. It picks the most likely continuation — which is why it sometimes misses what you wanted.
Obvious“I want to” → “eat” → “fruit.”
Counter-intuitiveThe same principle powers autocomplete and ChatGPT — just at different scales.
Edge caseThe model gives the most likely answer; your desired answer was the less likely one.
Analogy: Finishing someone’s sentence — you guess the most likely ending from context, and sometimes you guess wrong. The model is a next-word guesser, trained to pick the probable ending.
What people get wrong

That the model “knows” the answer. It knows probabilities — it chooses the most likely continuation, which is not the same as knowing.

Mnemonic: “Context → most likely continuation”

Issue 04The Recipe: Data + Self-Supervised Learning

From transcript 00:12:29 · 00:20:54 (corpus, truncate/predict, neural net, transformer)

4. How You Build a Language Model

Building a language model is a recipe, and everyone uses the same one. Step one: collect a ginormous corpus — a huge body of words. Where from? The web: the whole of Wikipedia, Stack Overflow, Quora, social media, GitHub, Reddit — whatever you can legally get. Step two: you need the machinery — a neural network that does the learning. The genius part is step three: self-supervised learning. Take sentences from the corpus, truncate (remove) the last part, and have the model predict the missing words. This is dead cheap, because you removed the words yourself — you already know the ground truth. Predict, compare to the truth, and if you’re wrong, adjust the model’s internals and go again. For months. Eventually it converges: a model that knows which words follow which.

What is inside? A simple neural network has input nodes, middle layers, and output nodes. The inputs are vectors (series of numbers), not words. The connections between nodes are weights — the numbers the network learns. Count them and you have the model’s size: this toy network has 99 trainable parameters (input 5 × next layer 8, plus a bias term that nudges the prediction when it’s off). Remember 99 — because when you hear a model has a billion parameters, imagine how much bigger that is. And the real monsters are transformers: neural networks made of blocks, each block itself a mini network. GPT literally stands for generative pre-trained transformer. Transformer blocks take embeddings (another word for vectors), process your context — “the chicken walked” — and predict the continuation: “across the road.” Even the end-of-sentence marker has to be spelled out (EOS), because the network is dumb in that charming way: you have to tell it everything.

  1. Collect a ginormous corpus from the web.
  2. Truncate the end of sentences.
  3. Predict the missing words; compare to the known ground truth.
  4. Adjust the weights when wrong; repeat for months.
  5. Deploy a transformer: blocks of mini networks, GPT = generative pre-trained transformer.
ObviousCorpus: Wikipedia, Stack Overflow, GitHub, Reddit.
Counter-intuitiveTraining is cheap because the labels are free — you remove words you already know.
Edge caseToy network: 99 parameters. GPT-4: a trillion. Same recipe, wildly different scale.

Issue 05Scale, Fine-Tuning, Alignment

From transcript 00:22:45 · 00:32:17 (scale, fine-tuning, HHH)

5. Bigger, Specialised, Aligned

Once you have the pre-trained model (that’s the “PT” in GPT — it has seen a lot about the world in the form of text), how does it become great? The blunt answer from the lecture: size is all that matters. Since 2018, model sizes have exploded. The toy network had 99 parameters; GPT-4 has roughly one trillion — and the human brain sits at about 100 trillion, for scale, though comparing GPT to the brain isn’t really possible. Words matter too: GPT-4 has seen a few billion words, approaching the roughly 100 billion words of all human-written text. There’s a plateau coming — training on AI-generated text gives diminished returns. And it costs: GPT-4’s training ran about $100 million, so you engineer the process carefully because a mistake can cost $50 million; not everyone has Microsoft backing.

Scale buys capability — more parameters, more tasks: summarisation, question answering, translation, code completion, reading comprehension. But a raw language model doesn’t behave the way people want. So comes fine-tuning: start from the pre-trained weights and narrow them to a speciality — take a general model and teach it, say, medical data, so it can write a diagnosis from a report. And to make it generally useful, they collect ~2,000 instruction examples (answer the question; answer step by step) and fine-tune on those — and the model generalises to unseen tasks you and I invent.

Finally, the alignment problem: how do we make an agent behave in accordance with what a human wants? The framework is HHH: helpful, honest, and harmless — the bare minimum. Helpful means follow instructions, clarify, ask relevant questions. Honest means accurate (not 100% there yet). Harmless means avoid toxic, biased, or offensive responses. How is it trained in? The same fine-tuning, but with humans: raters pick the better of two answers (“What causes the seasons to change?” — “the tilt of the Earth’s axis” beats “changes occur all the time”), and the model learns the preference. It’s expensive — humans in the loop — but it’s how ChatGPT became helpful.

  1. Pre-train on all the text you can find.
  2. Scale up parameters — size adds new capabilities.
  3. Fine-tune on a speciality (medical data, instructions).
  4. Align with HHH: helpful, honest, harmless — trained via human preferences.
ObviousFine-tune a general model on medical data to write diagnoses.
Counter-intuitivePeople think GPT is general-purpose by nature — it was fine-tuned to be general-purpose.
Edge caseGPT-4 training cost ~$100M; a mistake can cost half that.
Analogy: A brilliant intern still needs a code of conduct — fine-tuning is the training manual, HHH is the workplace rulebook. Capability gets the model hired; alignment makes it safe to keep around.
What people get wrong

That “general purpose” is the model’s nature. It’s engineered: pre-training gives capability, fine-tuning shapes behaviour.

Issue 06Risks, and the Future

From transcript 00:38:02 · 00:45:35 (bias, environment, jobs, fakes, future)

6. The Honest Risk Picture

Is it good, is it bad, are we in danger? The lecture’s answer: it’s a tool, with real, named risks. Content is virtually impossible to regulate, and historical bias shows up: Google’s Bard told users the James Webb telescope took the first picture of an exoplanet — it didn’t (that was 2004) — and the error wiped $100 billion off Google’s market value. Ask ChatGPT for a joke about men and it delivers; ask about women and it refuses — the fine-tuning shows its seams. Ask it to rank dictators and it declines to take a stance. These are alignment trade-offs in the wild.

Then the environment: one ChatGPT query takes roughly 100× the energy of a Google search, and producing language (inference) can cost more than training. Training Llama 2 emitted about 539 metric tonnes of CO². Bigger models need more energy, forever, during deployment. Society: some jobs will be lost — Goldman Sachs predicted 300 million affected; repetitive text-writing is clearly at risk. And fakes are real: a college student’s AI blog fooled people, a fake “Drake & The Weeknd” song sounded authentic, and deepfakes — like the fake “Trump arrested” image — can be spotted by details (that hand is too short).

The future, per the lecture: we can’t predict it, and no, evil computers aren’t coming to kill us. Tim Berners-Lee’s take: we don’t know what superintelligent AI would look like because we haven’t made it; there will be many capable AIs, some in harmful hands, and it’s easier to minimise their harm than to prevent the systems existing at all. The Australian Research Council tested whether GPT-4 could autonomously replicate and acquire resources — it failed every test. So: the bigger threat to mankind is probably climate change, AI is controlled by humans, benefit outweighs risk in some cases and not others, and history says risky tech (like nuclear) gets strongly regulated. Regulation is coming.

FAQ

Wasn’t Google Translate around long before ChatGPT? So what’s new?

Exactly — generative AI isn’t new. Translate (2006), Siri (2011), and autocomplete are all the same family. GPT-4 is a leap in capability (exams, code, essays) that hit 100 million users in two months, not a new category of thing.

Why does ChatGPT give wrong or weird answers sometimes?

It’s trained to predict the most likely continuation. When the correct answer is the less likely one, or the context is ambiguous, it produces something reasonable but wrong. That’s the engine working as designed.

Why did ChatGPT’s knowledge stop in 2021?

It’s a snapshot: the model was pre-trained on text available up to its knowledge cutoff (the demo shows “as of my last knowledge update in September 2021”). It isn’t connected to live facts unless a system adds that.

Why won’t ChatGPT say certain things or take some stances?

That’s fine-tuning for HHH — helpful, honest, harmless. Human raters taught it preferences (e.g., the seasons answer), which is why behaviour varies by topic: jokes about men but refusal about women.

Should I worry about AI taking over?

The lecture’s answer: no — GPT-4 failed every autonomous-replication test, humans control it, and climate change is the bigger threat. Worry instead about bias, energy, jobs, and fakes — and the regulation that’s coming.
