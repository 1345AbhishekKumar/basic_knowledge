Motherboards, BIOS/UEFI Firmware & Power Supplies (PSU)

Explore motherboard form factors, standoffs, legacy jumpers, BIOS vs. modern UEFI firmware, the POST startup routine, CMOS battery backup, disk reimaging, and power supply unit (PSU) electrical dynamics.

📐 1. Motherboard Form Factors & Standoffs

A motherboard's form factor specifies its physical dimensions, mounting hole locations, and available expansion slots:

Form Factor Typical Dimensions Expansion Capability Target System
ATX (Advanced Technology Extended) 12 × 9.6 inches (305 × 244 mm) Up to 7 PCIe expansion slots, 4-8 RAM slots Full-sized desktop towers, high-end workstations, gaming PCs.
Micro-ATX (mATX) 9.6 × 9.6 inches (244 × 244 mm) Up to 4 PCIe expansion slots, 2-4 RAM slots Compact office desktops and budget tower builds.
ITX (Mini-ITX / Nano / Pico) 6.7 × 6.7 inches (170 × 170 mm) 1 PCIe slot, 2 RAM slots Small Form Factor (SFF) cubes, home theater PCs (HTPC), IoT appliances.
Motherboard Standoffs (Critical Installation Step)

Standoffs are threaded brass or metal spacers screwed into the computer chassis before placing the motherboard. They elevate the PCB off the bare metal casing to prevent electrical shorts between solder joints and the steel frame.

🔘 2. Motherboard Switches & Jumpers

Jumper Pins & Jumper Caps

Small gold-plated pins protruding from the motherboard. Placing a plastic conductive jumper cap over two pins shorts/completes a circuit to change a setting (e.g. resetting BIOS/CMOS settings to factory defaults or clearing passwords).

DIP (Dual In-line Package) Switches

Legacy banks of tiny rocker/slide switches flipped with a pointed object (pen or tweezers) to configure bus speeds and voltages on older motherboards. Obsolete in modern automated systems.

💾 3. BIOS vs. UEFI Firmware & Startup Functions

Definition: BIOS / UEFI

BIOS (Basic Input/Output System) and its modern replacement UEFI (Unified Extensible Firmware Interface) are low-level firmware stored on a non-volatile ROM chip that initializes hardware components and hands control over to the operating system.

Feature Legacy BIOS Modern UEFI
Architecture 16-bit processor mode 32-bit or 64-bit native mode
Interface Text-only, keyboard-only blue screen Graphical User Interface (GUI) with mouse support
Boot Drive Capacity Max 2.2 TB (MBR partition scheme) Up to 9.4 ZB (GPT partition scheme)
Security Basic password protection Secure Boot (cryptographically verifies OS loaders against rootkits)

The Four Core Functions of Firmware

1. Power-On Self-Test (POST)

Runs automated diagnostic tests across CPU, RAM, GPU, and controllers during cold boot. Emits audible beep codes or diagnostic Q-LED error codes if hardware is missing or faulty.

2. Bootstrap Loader

Locates the active bootable device (according to configured Boot Priority), reads the EFI partition or Master Boot Record (MBR), and loads the OS kernel into RAM.

3. Hardware Drivers / Services

Provides basic low-level instructions enabling the CPU to interact with keyboards, displays, and storage prior to OS driver loading.

4. CMOS Setup Utility

The interactive configuration screen accessed via hotkey (F2, F10, DEL) to configure clock settings, fan curves, boot devices, and TPM security.

🔋 4. CMOS RAM & The CMOS Battery

The system settings configured in BIOS/UEFI (system time, boot order, drive configurations) are stored inside a dedicated low-power volatile chip known as CMOS RAM (Complementary Metal-Oxide Semiconductor).

The Role of the CMOS Battery (CR2032)

Because CMOS RAM is volatile, motherboards feature a 3V lithium coin-cell CMOS battery. This battery keeps CMOS RAM energized when the computer is unplugged from the wall. A dying CMOS battery causes the system clock to reset to default (e.g., Jan 1, 1970) on every cold boot and throws "CMOS Checksum Error" warnings.

⚡ 5. Power Supply Units (PSU): Volts, Amps & Watts

The Power Supply Unit (PSU) converts high-voltage Alternating Current (AC) from wall outlets into steady, regulated Direct Current (DC) rails (+12V for CPU/GPU, +5V for storage/USB, +3.3V for motherboard logic).

Voltage (V)

Electrical Pressure: Analogous to water pressure in a pipe. Measured in Volts (e.g., 120V in North America, 220-240V in Europe/Asia).

Current / Amperage (A)

Volume of Flow: The quantity of electrical charge flowing through the circuit. Measured in Amperes (Amps).

Wattage (W)

Total Electrical Power: The total rate of energy consumed: Watts = Volts × Amps.

⚠️ 6. Voltage Mismatch Scenarios & Safety

Scenario 1: 120V Appliance in a 220V Outlet

Result: Catastrophic Hardware Failure / Burnout

The double electrical pressure forces excessive current through circuits designed for lower voltage, causing overheating, fried capacitors, smoke, and fire hazards.

Scenario 2: 220V Appliance in a 120V Outlet

Result: Underpowered Operation

The lower voltage produces inadequate electrical current. The device will either fail to turn on or run at reduced power without immediate catastrophic physical destruction.
