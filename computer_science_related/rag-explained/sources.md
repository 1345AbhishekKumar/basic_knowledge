# Sources — RAG: how chatbots answer from your documents

Every fact in this lesson was verified during web research (see `interactive-lessons/references/research.md`). Claims below were cross-checked against 2+ independent sources.

## Facts used in this lesson

- **RAG was introduced in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" by Patrick Lewis et al. (2020, published at NeurIPS 2020). The authors were at Facebook AI Research / University College London.** — Source: arXiv:2005.11401 abstract ([arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)); *Wikipedia: Retrieval-augmented generation* ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation)); Meta AI research publications page ([ai.meta.com/research/publications/retrieval-augmented-generation-for-knowledge-intensive-nlp-tasks/](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/retrieval-augmented-generation-for-knowledge-intensive-nlp-tasks/)).
- **In the original paper the "parametric memory" is a pre-trained seq2seq model (BART) and the "non-parametric memory" is a dense vector index of Wikipedia accessed with a neural retriever (DPR).** — Source: arXiv abstract ("we explore a general-purpose fine-tuning recipe for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — models which combine pre-trained parametric and non-parametric memory"); author's page at patricklewis.io ([patricklewis.io/publication/rag/](https://www.patricklewis.io/publication/rag/)); multiple secondary summaries agree (e.g. alphaxiv.org/abs/2005.11401).
- **LLMs are trained on a finite, static snapshot of public data with a knowledge cutoff; training data being static and dated is what makes answers go stale or wrong, and retraining is expensive.** — Source: AWS "What is RAG?" ("LLM training data is static and introduces a cut-off date on the knowledge it has"; RAG is "cost-effective... without the need to retrain") ([aws.amazon.com/what-is/retrieval-augmented-generation/](https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/retrieval-augmented-generation/)); IBM "What is retrieval-augmented generation?" ("models have a knowledge cutoff... RAG connects models with supplemental external data in real-time"; "Retraining a foundation model or fine-tuning it... is computationally expensive") ([ibm.com/think/topics/retrieval-augmented-generation](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/retrieval-augmented-generation)).
- **Hallucination = the model presenting incorrect or made-up information as factual; RAG reduces the risk of hallucinations but cannot make a model error-proof.** — Source: IBM ("A hallucination or confabulation happens when models present incorrect or made-up information as though it is factual"; "While RAG can reduce the risk of hallucinations, it cannot make a model error-proof"); peer-reviewed study (Nishisako et al. 2025): "Using RAG with reliable information sources significantly reduces the hallucination rate of generative AI chatbots" ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425422/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425422/)).
- **RAG keeps knowledge outside the model, in an external, updateable knowledge base; the model itself is not retrained, and answers can carry citations back to the source documents.** — Source: AWS (external data → embedding → vector database "knowledge library"; "The output can include citations or references to sources"); IBM ("RAG systems can include citations to the knowledge sources... as part of their responses"; "adjusting its external knowledge sources as opposed to fine-tuning or retraining").
- **The RAG flow has two phases: offline indexing (load documents, chunk, embed, store vectors) and query-time retrieval (embed the user query, similarity search, take the top-k chunks, insert them into the prompt, then generate).** — Source: IBM's five-stage process and four-component description (knowledge base, retriever, integration layer, generator); AWS ("the RAG model augments the user input... by adding the relevant retrieved data in context"); Google Cloud RAG guide ([cloud.google.com/use-cases/retrieval-augmented-generation](https://cloud.google.com/use-cases/retrieval-augmented-generation)); surrealdb RAG architecture notes ([surrealdb.com/docs/learn/data-models/vector-search/rag-architecture-patterns](https://surrealdb.com/docs/learn/data-models/vector-search/rag-architecture-patterns)).
- **Embedding models convert text into vectors of numbers; embedding models have a maximum token limit (context window) so a chunk longer than that cannot be embedded as a single vector — one reason documents are split into chunks.** — Source: Pinecone, "Chunking Strategies for LLM Applications" ("All embedding models have context windows, which determine the amount of information in tokens that can be processed into a single fixed-size...") ([pinecone.io/learn/chunking-strategies/](https://www.pinecone.io/learn/chunking-strategies/)); Unstructured, "Chunking for RAG: Best Practices" ("Embedding models can only embed text up to their maximum token limit") ([unstructured.io/blog/chunking-for-rag-best-practices](https://unstructured.io/blog/chunking-for-rag-best-practices)).
- **Typical chunk sizes are on the order of a few hundred tokens with ~10–20% overlap between neighboring chunks (recommendations in guides; exact values are tuned per use case).** — Sources give overlapping ranges: Databricks community guide recommends "General text: 200–500 tokens, 10–20% overlap" ([community.databricks.com/t5/technical-blog/...](https://community.databricks.com/t5/technical-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-chunking-strategies-for-rag-applications/ba-p/113089)); newline guide: "chunks of 256–1,024 tokens, adjust overlap (10–20%)" ([newline.co/@zaoyang/fixed-size-chunking-in-rag-pipelines-a-guide](https://www.newline.co/@zaoyang/fixed-size-chunking-in-rag-pipelines-a-guide--af509f11)); Unstract docs: "Typically, an overlap of 10–20% of the chunk size is a good starting point" ([docs.unstract.com/.../chunking](https://docs.unstract.com/unstract/unstract_platform/user_guides/chunking/)). Because these are recommendations, the lesson says "a few hundred tokens per chunk with a small overlap".
- **Embedding dimensions are commonly in the hundreds to low thousands: BERT-base embeddings are 768-dimensional; OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small and text-embedding-ada-002 output 1536-dimensional vectors by default; text-embedding-3-large outputs 3072.** — Source: OpenAI vector embeddings guide ([developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)); Stack Overflow Blog "An intuitive introduction to text embeddings" ("often 768 or 1536 [dimensions]") ([stackoverflow.blog/2023/11/09/an-intuitive-introduction-to-text-embeddings/](https://stackoverflow.blog/2023/11/09/an-intuitive-introduction-to-text-embeddings/)); BERT-base 768 dims widely documented (e.g. vickiboykis.com/2025/09/01/how-big-are-our-embeddings-now-and-why/). The lesson says "e.g. 768 for BERT-base, 1536 for OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small" — both verified.
- **Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their magnitudes; it ranges from −1 (opposite direction) through 0 (orthogonal / unrelated) to +1 (same direction). With normalized vectors it equals the dot product.** — Source: Wikipedia "Cosine similarity" ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine_similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine_similarity)); IBM "What is cosine similarity?" ("values range from -1 to 1... 1 indicates the vectors are pointing in the same direction") ([ibm.com/think/topics/cosine-similarity](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/cosine-similarity)); Elastic blog on vector similarity measures ([elastic.co/search-labs/blog/vector-similarity-measures-and-scoring](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/vector-similarity-measures-and-scoring)).
- **Semantic (vector) search finds text by meaning even when no words are shared — "similar content produces similar vectors" — whereas exact-keyword search only matches literal words; this is why paraphrase questions trip up keyword search but not vector retrieval.** — Source: AWS ("relevancy was calculated and established using mathematical vector calculations"; also distinguishes semantic search from conventional keyword search which "produce[s] limited results"); Google Cloud RAG guide; SnapLogic embeddings explainer ("similar content... closely together... semantic meaning") ([snaplogic.com/blog/embeddings-and-vector-databases](https://www.snaplogic.com/blog/embeddings-and-vector-databases)).
- **Vector stores must stay fast with millions of vectors, so they typically use approximate-nearest-neighbour (ANN) search rather than comparing the query against every vector.** — Source: Wikipedia "Vector database" ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_database)); FAISS tutorial "Vector Search with FAISS: Approximate Nearest Neighbor" ([pyimagesearch.com/2026/02/16/vector-search-with-faiss-approximate-nearest-neighbor-ann-explained/](https://pyimagesearch.com/2026/02/16/vector-search-with-faiss-approximate-nearest-neighbor-ann-explained/)); Elastic blog (approximate vs exact search trade-offs).
- **Retrieval quality — chunking, embeddings, and the top-k setting — largely decides RAG answer quality: if the wrong chunk is retrieved, the model answers confidently from irrelevant context ("garbage in, garbage out").** — Source: Snorkel AI "RAG failure modes" ("Ineffective document chunking can lead to information loss, noisy context, or irrelevant retrievals") ([snorkel.ai/blog/retrieval-augmented-generation-rag-failure-modes-and-how-to-fix-them/](https://snorkel.ai/blog/retrieval-augmented-generation-rag-failure-modes-and-how-to-fix-them/)); AWS Dev.to "Why RAG gives wrong answers (and how to fix retrieval failures)" ([dev.to/aws/why-rag-gives-wrong-answers-and-how-to-fix-retrieval-failures-gbj](https://dev.to/aws/why-rag-gives-wrong-answers-and-how-to-fix-retrieval-failures-gbj)); IBM ("RAG models tend to provide more accurate answers... cannot make a model error-proof").

## Notes on the playground's toy data

- The policy document, its sentence topics, and the 2-D meaning points are **illustrative stand-ins** tuned for teaching. Real embeddings are hundreds of dimensions and the similarity arithmetic is usually cosine over those vectors; this lesson flattens the idea to a 2-D map (chunk point = mean of its sentences' points) so the *geometry of retrieval* — meaning lands near meaning, mixed chunks drift to the middle, nearest wins — is visible. The pipeline itself (chunk → embed → store → query → top-k → prompt → answer) and the failure modes demonstrated (over-large chunks blur meaning, keyword search is lured by incidental words) are the real behavior, sourced above.
- "Top-k" in the playground is capped at 3 chunks simply to keep the toy readable; production k is tuned (sources above describe k as a tunable parameter; e.g. a 2025 peer-reviewed evaluation used k=5).
