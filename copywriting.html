01

Module 01 — Behavior Design

Copywriting Isn't Writing. It's Getting What's in My Head into Yours.

A — The Core Idea

Most people think copywriting is writing. Sam Parr thinks it's behavior design. His working definition, pulled from his own tweet: "Good copywriting means getting what's in my head into yours and influencing your behavior — getting you to do what I want." Notice what's missing from that definition: words. Words are just the delivery vehicle. The actual job is understanding what people already want, then arranging stimuli — a joke, a question, a detail, a demand — until the reader walks themselves to the action you chose for them.

The oldest scaffold for that arrangement is AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. First you interrupt the pattern (Attention). Then you earn the next few seconds by making the reader see themselves in the scene (Interest). Then you make them want the transformed version of themselves (Desire). Only at the end do you ask for the move (Action). Sam ran this exact sequence, live, the night he met his wife: Attention was an absurd shouted question about chickpeas versus lentils; Interest was the laugh that made her stop; Desire was the promise of more fun if she kept talking; Action was "I'm Sam. Nice to meet you."

The sequence matters more than the material. Sam's rule for his salespeople — "you can say virtually anything as long as you make them laugh" — is an AIDA claim: once Attention and Interest are locked in with an emotional state, the specific words barely matter. Skip a step and the structure collapses. Ask for the action before you've built desire and you're a stranger shouting. Grab attention without interest and you're just a clown.

"You can say virtually anything as long as you make them laugh."— SAM PARR, ON SALES AND ON FLIRTING

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Lentil Opener

Sam spots a pretty woman at happy hour, turns to his friend Lily and says — loud enough for the stranger to hear — "What's the difference between a chickpea and a lentil?" She stops, stunned. He lands it: "I don't pay $500 to have a lentil on my face." Classic garbanzo. She laughs. "I'm Sam. Nice to meet you." A full AIDA funnel executed at a bar: pattern interrupt → laugh → promise of fun → the ask.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

Say Almost Anything

Sam's sales rule says the words are fungible; the emotional state is not. A lentil joke should fail. It works because laughter is the carrier wave for Attention and Interest — as long as it's obvious you mean no harm, the content of the sentence barely registers. State first, substance second.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

Restaurant Owner Energy

Talk to strangers like you already know them. Sam watched a restaurateur in Little Italy greet a table he'd never met — "How's the daughter? You like ice cream?" — asking the three-year-old before her parents sat down. Stiffness reads as threat; pre-familiarity reads as status. The energy itself is the attention mechanism.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

AIDA is a maître d'.

A great maître d' doesn't recite the menu through the door. He catches your eye (Attention), walks you through the room describing tonight's special (Interest), seats you where the kitchen smells incredible (Desire) — and only then hands you the menu and takes the order (Action). Copy performs the same four jobs in the same order. Nobody orders at the door.

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

Sequencing beats vocabulary. The lentil joke uses fourth-grade words and out-converts most landing pages. The skill is reading what people want and ordering the moments — the closing line can be as plain as "I'm Sam. Nice to meet you."

Module 02 — Headlines & Open Loops

Mind the Gap: The 80-Cent Sentence

A — The Core Idea

David Ogilvy's arithmetic: on average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body. Which means when you finish writing your headline, you've spent eighty cents of your dollar. Amateurs write the copy and slap a headline on at the last minute; professionals do it in reverse. Sam notes that young creators already get this — the YouTube generation knows the hook and the packaging matter more than the video itself.

The engine that makes a headline work is the curiosity gap — what Sam calls "minding the gap." Open a loop the brain refuses to leave unresolved, and the reader must read on to close it. The most famous headline ever written for a music course did it in a single line: "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but when I started to play…" Laughed at whom? Played what? Were they stunned? You cannot walk away mid-gap; the tension demands resolution, the way a melody demands its final note.

Two warnings ship with the tool. First, the gap must be paid off — Upworthy proved the mechanic ("You won't believe number seven") can drag 200 million monthly visitors out of thin air, and also that unpaid gaps decay into clickbait. Second, the headline is your targeting system. Rolex didn't write for everyone: "The men who shape destinies wear Rolex." Buzzfeed wrote "50 Signs You're From Denver" knowing only Denverites would click — and 30% of Denver did. Sam's Hampton ads open with "If you make over $3 million a year, I know exactly how you feel." Niches make riches: a headline that speaks to one specific person converts a small audience at a huge rate, which beats a vast audience at zero.

"On average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you've written your headline, you've spent 80 cents of your dollar."— DAVID OGILVY, VIA SAM'S DOG-EARED COPY

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Rolls-Royce Clock

"At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." Ogilvy spent weeks on that one headline. A specific, verifiable detail that quietly asks "so what makes this the best car in the world?" — gap and proof in twenty words.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

The Clickbait Machine

"You won't believe number seven." Junk content, perfect mechanics. Joe's competitor rode gap-stacking from zero to 200 million monthly visitors in about six months. And the piano headline ran the same loop a century earlier — with a payoff waiting at the end.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

The Headline as Sniper

Rolex: "The men who shape destinies." Homesick Candles sold the smell of all 50 states (Washington = evergreen; expats bought it for their moms). Plus loop-stacking tactics: Tony Robbins' "wait until the next chapter," a curiosity beat every ~8 seconds in short-form video, and the pickup-artist time constraint — "real quick, I gotta run, but…" — that lowers defenses by promising an exit.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

A headline is a door left three inches open.

You can walk past a closed door. You cannot walk past a door left ajar, an itch you can't reach, or a melody that stops one note before resolving. The Rolls headline is a door left open; the body copy is the room behind it. Clickbait leaves the door open and boards up the hallway.

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

Sam flatly disagrees: attention has always worked the same way. 27-minute video sales letters and 2,000-word white pages still convert — when they're interesting. "No one will read this" is code for "this isn't interesting enough."

F — Field Test

G — Memory + Pop Quiz

Module 03 — The Slippery Slope

Sentence One Has Exactly One Job

A — The Core Idea

Once the headline buys the first read, Joe Sugarman's axiom takes the wheel: the purpose of sentence one is to get you to read sentence two. The purpose of sentence two is to get you to read sentence three. And so on. Sam calls this the slippery slope — good copy is a greased chute where every line exists only to slide the reader into the next one. His favorite hook demonstrates the mechanic while performing it: "You're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to. Now you're reading this second sentence because, again, I'm telling you to. This is called copywriting." The fourth wall breaks, the reader laughs, and — having now read three sentences — is measurably more likely to read a fourth.

The psychology underneath is the rule of consistency: people who've said a small yes are dramatically more likely to say a big one. In the classic study, homeowners who first agreed to a tiny sticker in their window became something like 40 percentage points more likely to later accept a giant campaign sign on their lawn. Copy runs the same play with micro-agreements: "You know that feeling when you wake up at 3 a.m. and can't tell if you need the bathroom or an existential crisis?" Nod once, and you've started down the slope.

This redefines the goal. The goal is not to get 100% of people to buy. The goal is to get the right person to read the entire ad — because a large percentage of people who finish will buy. Sam's living proof: copythat.com, a sales page that is 2,000 words on a white background, converting around 5% and paying a full-time salary. Long isn't bad. Ugly isn't bad. Boring is bad.

"The purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second sentence."— JOE SUGARMAN, SAM'S MOST-QUOTED AXIOM

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Hook That Performs Itself

"You're reading this because I want you to" — a hook that breaks the fourth wall and proves its own thesis. Sam's copythat.com: 2,000 words, white background, ~5% conversion, a full-time income from a slope with no decoration at all.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

The 27-Minute Sales Letter

Random GLP-1 affiliates running 27-minute video sales letters before YouTube videos, betting nobody clicks skip — and spinning up nine-figure funnels. Long and ugly out-converted short and slick. Because it was interesting.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

The Sticker Study

Homeowners who agreed to a tiny window sticker became ~40 points more likely to later accept a huge yard sign. The copy version: plant head-nod yeses early — "Isn't it frustrating that…?" — because a small yes greases the big one.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

Copy is a waterslide, not a staircase.

Nobody climbs copy. They fall down it. Each sentence is a flume segment whose only job is delivering the reader — wet, committed, slightly thrilled — to the next segment. A reader who "bounces" is just a rider who found a flat spot in the slide.

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

The slope is a filter, not a net. Let the wrong readers slide off early — the right readers who make it to the bottom buy at a huge rate. Chasing 100% is how copy gets boring.

F — Field Test

G — Memory + Pop Quiz

📌 1 → 2 → 3. Every sentence has exactly one job: the next sentence. And a yes, however small, makes the next yes easier.

Module 04 — Rhythm & The Red Pen

Write Like Hemingway, Edit Like Buffett

A — The Core Idea

Sam's editing rules fit on an index card. Write at a 7th-grade reading level — paste your copy into hemingwayapp.com and it will tell you where you stand; USA Today writes at 4th grade, the New York Times at 7th. One sentence, one point: if you reach for a comma, try a period instead. Start sentences with And or But — it creates rhythm and it sounds like a person. And cut: write the first draft drunk on enthusiasm, then edit sober. Cut a third, then cut a third of what's left, then cut again — "kill your darlings." Ogilvy's confession is the whole profession in one line: "I'm a lousy writer. I'm a great editor."

Why does simplicity convert? Constraint forces clarity, and clarity is what readers experience as intelligence. Warren Buffett explains insurance — genuinely complicated — in sentences averaging 17 words; when he started they averaged 25, and the number fell for decades as he got better. Hemingway wrote "The man was sad. He wanted to fish." and broke hearts with it.

The other half of the rule is rhythm. Good copy reads like music: a short sentence hits like a drum. A medium sentence carries the melody. And then a long sentence unspools and gathers momentum and stacks detail on detail until it pulls the reader around the corner into whatever you wanted them to see next. Short. Medium. Long. Repeat. Rambling — the same length, the same length, the same length — is what people actually mean when they say a page is "too long."

"Write drunk. Edit sober. Then cut a third, and cut a third again."— THE ROOM'S CONSENSUS, VIA SEAN AND OGILVY

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Hemingway Stack

7th-grade ceiling via hemingwayapp.com. One sentence = one point. Comma → period. Open sentences with And or But. USA Today reads at 4th grade and everyone in America understands it — that's the benchmark, not the exception.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

The Smart Person's Paper

The New York Times — the paper people assume is written for geniuses — reads at 7th grade. Big words signal insecurity; clarity signals command. Hemingway: "The man was sad. He wanted to fish." Profound and monosyllabic.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

Buffett's Measurable Eraser

Sam analyzed the annual letters: Buffett's average sentence fell from ~25 words to ~17 (possibly 14) across decades. Mastery leaves a data signature, and the signature is shorter.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

Copy is sheet music.

Short sentences are staccato. Long ones are legato. A page where every sentence is the same length is a metronome — technically correct and utterly unbearable. And editing isn't adding clay to a sculpture; it's chipping away everything that isn't the statue.

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

17 words. The "smart person's paper" is written at 7th grade. Persuasion is the reader never having to re-read a line — every word they spend decoding is a word they don't spend desiring.

Module 05 — Story Sells

The $2 Billion Ad That Ran for 28 Years

A — The Core Idea

The most profitable ad in Wall Street Journal history was not a pitch. It was a story about two young men who graduated from the same college twenty-five years earlier — same grades, same charm, same ambitious dreams. At the reunion they met again, and they were very different: one was a department head; the other owned the company. The only difference the ad named? One of them had subscribed to the Journal. That ad ran, essentially unchanged, for 28 years and is credited with more than $2 billion in revenue. Sam stole it almost wholesale for The Hustle's Trends publication — "twisted maybe 3%" — and it minted money.

Why does a story outsell a pitch? Because a story is a beginning, a middle, and an end with tension in the middle — and tension is just a curiosity gap that lasts for pages. The best-selling stories follow the hero's journey with one non-negotiable casting decision: the customer is the hero; your brand is the guide. In the Journal ad, the boy is Luke and the WSJ is Yoda. Sam's best-performing Hampton ad cast Sam himself as the hero — sitting in a room with seven lawyers and seven accountants, about to sell his company, feeling totally overwhelmed — with Hampton as the guide he wished he'd had.

When people resist story, they say it makes copy too long. Sam's answer: there's no such thing as too long, only too boring. The final tool here is the failed-solution frame: before presenting your product, absolve the reader's past failures — "It's not your fault. You just didn't have the right mechanism." Guilt drops, defenses drop, and the guide can finally hand the hero the sword.

"There's no such thing as too long. Only too boring."— SAM PARR, DEFENDING THE 27-MINUTE SALES LETTER

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Two Young Men

Same college. Same promise. Twenty-five years later, two very different lives at the reunion — the only named difference was a subscription. 28 years in print, $2B+ in revenue. Sam twisted it ~3% for Trends ($300/year) and it crushed.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

The Ad They Mocked

Sam's Hampton founder story — seven lawyers, seven accountants, drowning at his own exit — drew comments making fun of his age. It still became one of their best ads. Vulnerability plus structure beats polish.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

The Failed-Solution Frame

From Alex's UGC lab (~8,000 ads with performance data): "You tried to fix this. It didn't work. That's not your fault — you didn't have the right mechanism." Absolve the guilt, drop the defense, hand over the sword. Plus Trey Parker's "but / therefore" rule and the hero's return to the origin — the reunion scene.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

A pitch is a billboard. A story is a campfire.

People drive past billboards. They sit around campfires — sometimes for 28 years. And at the campfire, the customer is Luke and you are Yoda: your job is to hand over the lightsaber, not to wear it.

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

There's no such thing as too long — only too boring. The story is precisely what makes length survivable: the WSJ tale ran for 28 years and cleared $2 billion, and 27-minute VSLs build nine-figure funnels.

Module 06 — Copywork

Steal Like a Copywriter: The Copy Hour

A — The Core Idea

Sam's answer to "how do I become a great copywriter?" is one word: copywork. Find writing that's regarded as great — the Boron Letters, The Great Gatsby, SNL scripts, classic ads — and copy it out, word for word, by hand. Not typed. Handwritten; the physical act encodes the patterns deeper, the way writing things down in therapy changes you. His logic is piano-lesson logic: nobody sits down at a piano and composes a hit. You learn Jingle Bells, then Happy Birthday, then a Green Day song — and only after your hands have absorbed the rules of music do you write your own. Sam did copywork for months. Alex did two focused days of it on five viral scripts, mapping exactly where the pros placed transitions, tension, and loops — and his next video opened at two million views.

Copywork powers everything else in this lesson, because Sam's entire method is theft with taste. The Hustle ad he calls "singularly responsible for the exit" — "My friends think I'm smart. I'm not." — had roughly $10 million spent behind it. Hampton's brand green was lifted from vintage British racing ads. His cadences come from Churchill's anaphora ("we shall fight on the beaches") and FDR ("the only thing we have to fear is fear itself"). His swipe file is a shelf of Thrasher magazines, a backwards Japanese Popeye issue, and a first-edition George bought for $30 and now worth about $1,000.

The prescription: Copy Hour. One hour a day, three to six months, copying the classics of whatever genre you want to write — ads, short stories, standup, TV scenes. Then remix. First drafts are supposed to be bad; as Ogilvy said, "I'm a lousy writer. I'm a great editor." The eraser is the instrument.

"My friends think I'm smart. I'm not."— THE HUSTLE AD SAM CALLS "SINGULARLY RESPONSIBLE FOR THE EXIT" · ~$10M SPENT BEHIND IT

B — Three Exhibits

Exhibit A · The Obvious One

The Copy Hour

The Boron Letters. The Great Gatsby. SNL scripts. Classic ads. Copied word-for-word, by hand, for months. Alex's version: five viral scripts transcribed → first 2M-view video out of the gate, because he could see where the pros placed every loop.

Exhibit B · The Counter-Intuitive One

Copying Made Him Funnier

"I'm not funny — it helped me get funnier." Same with recreating Kanye beats to learn production. You're not plagiarizing outputs; you're absorbing the rules of the language until they live in your hands.

Exhibit C · The Edge Case

Stealing Beyond Words

British racing green, lifted for Hampton. Churchill's and FDR's phrase structures, lifted for cadence. Thrasher, a Japanese magazine read backwards, a JFK Jr. first edition — the swipe file is a lead magnet waiting to happen. Steal the syntax, not the sentence.

C — The Diagram

D — The Analogy

Copywork is how musicians learn.

You don't compose first. You play other people's songs until the rules live in your fingers — Jingle Bells, then Green Day, then your own thing. Every great copywriter in this episode steals; the amateurs just don't admit it, and the pros keep receipts (magazines, first editions, subway photos).

E — The Misconception

VERDICT

Originality is downstream of absorption. "I'm a lousy writer. I'm a great editor." First drafts are supposed to be bad — write drunk, edit sober — and taste is built by hand-copying people who already have it.

Bonus Card — Objection Judo & Humor

8-Mile Rule

Say the Bad Thing First

In the rap battle, Eminem lists his own insults before his opponent can — and takes away all their ammunition. In copy: "I know what you're thinking — who is this person, and why should I trust them?" The Lenovo headphone ad did it with a story: "I accidentally wore them in the shower" beats the word "waterproof."

The Humor Clause

Funny Is a Brand Decision

Humor is one of the easiest levers to sell with — if it's your brand's voice. The Hustle ran "So Fi, more like So [__]." The New York Post files someone daily. Hampton's About page is written to be funny. If you're not funny, copywork SNL — Alex did, and it worked.

Still Confused?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is long copy really better than short copy?

Long copy converts better when it's interesting — that's the whole claim. Sam points at 27-minute video sales letters building nine-figure funnels and his own 2,000-word white page converting at 5%. The objection "no one will read this" translates to "this isn't interesting enough." That said, short copy has its place: bottom-of-funnel, high-intent readers (like Harley's pre-qualification ads) don't need a campfire — they need a door. Match length to the reader's distance from the buy, and make every sentence earn the next one either way.

Isn't the curiosity gap just clickbait?

The mechanic is identical; the difference is the payoff. Upworthy proved the gap can drag 200 million monthly visitors through a door — and also that never paying the loop off rots your brand into clickbait. The piano headline ran the exact same loop a century earlier and became the most famous ad of all time because the body copy delivered exactly what the headline promised. Rule: never open a loop you won't close, and close it better than the reader hoped.

Do I have to be funny to use humor?

No — humor only works when it's part of your brand's voice. The Hustle ran "So Fi, more like So [__]" and hired funny people specifically because Sam isn't the funny one. The New York Post and Hampton's About page prove humor sells when it's native to the brand and clearly harmless. If it's not your voice, don't force it — but if you want to develop it, Alex's prescription is copywork on SNL scripts and standup specials until timing becomes instinct. He wasn't funny either. It helped.

What reading level should I actually aim for?

Seventh grade. Paste your copy into hemingwayapp.com and it will score you. The benchmarks: USA Today writes at 4th grade and nearly everyone in America understands it; the New York Times — the "smart person's paper" — writes at 7th. Practically: one sentence per point, turn most commas into periods, start sentences with And or But for rhythm, and chase Buffett's 17-word average. Constraint forces clarity, and clarity is what readers experience as intelligence.

How long does copywork take to work?

Sam's prescription is Copy Hour: one hour a day, three to six months, copying the acknowledged classics of your genre by hand — ads, the Boron Letters, Gatsby, SNL scripts, standup. But the loop can move fast: Alex did two focused days of copywork on five viral scripts, mapped where each pro placed transitions and loops, and his next video opened at two million views. Handwriting matters — the physical act encodes patterns deeper than typing, which is the same reason journaling changes how you think.

Does any of this work for B2B or "boring" products?

Yes — the $2 billion ad was a B2B-ish pitch for a newspaper subscription, sold to executives with a story about two boys. Hampton targets founders making $3M+ with intimacy and specificity, not glamour. The lever is never the product's excitement level; it's specificity (the electric clock at 60 mph), targeting (the headline as sniper), tension (the 25-year gap), and absolution (it's not your fault). Onions — $10 million a year of them — went viral because a desk, two phones, and a calculator broke the frame of what a big business "should" look like.

  • Ride the subway with your eyes open; photograph the good ones
