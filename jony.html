The Simple Genius of Jony Ive

How a dyslexic English art-school kid who loved taking radios apart became the designer behind the iMac, the iPod, and the iPhone — and, with Steve Jobs, turned Apple from an engineering company into a design company. A timeline of five stops, from a childhood workshop to 450 million iPods.

What you'll learn
  • Start every design from the story of the product — and what it should be.
  • Apply "simplify, remove, reduce" until only the essential remains.
  • See how Jobs and Ive reversed a consensus culture into a design-led one.
  • Build a small team with a hiring bar set at intimidation.

01 / CRAFT
Where the genius came from

Care is the whole secret.

Before Apple, before the iMac: a boy dismantling radios, a father's workshop, and a habit of doing far more work than anyone asked for.

True interest is revealed early. As a boy, Jony Ive dismantled radios and cassette recorders to see how the pieces fit. His father Mike — a master silversmith turned England's design-education inspector — gave him the workshop and, more importantly, the conversations: pointing at street lamps and asking why they differed. From him Jony took two lifelong rules. Care: "What I really despise is when I sense some carelessness in a product." And respect for the work: "If you didn't take the time to do it, why should anybody else?" The volume followed the care — when a friend visited his student flat, he found over a hundred foam prototypes of a single project.

  1. Take things apart to learn how they're made.
  2. Talk about made objects constantly — make it a conversation.
  3. Put care into things people may never notice.
  4. Respect the work: do it properly or don't do it.
  5. Build volume: many models, many attempts.
Example · What

Walking down the street, Mike points out different street lamps and asks young Jony why they differ.

So what: design sense is trained through everyday conversation, not classes.

Example · What

Most design students build half a dozen models; Jony's flat held 100 foam prototypes of one project.

So what: "the sheer focus to get it perfect" — volume is how care becomes craft.

Example · What

He meets the Mac as a student and feels, for the first time, "the humanity of a product" — a sense of the people who made it.

So what: care is visible; people can feel the maker behind the object.

AnalogyCare in a product is like seasoning in a home meal — you can't name the ingredient, but you taste that someone bothered.

02 / PHILOSOPHY
How he thinks

Start from what it should be.

Jony's method never changed: ask for the product's story, imagine the ideal with no limits, then reduce until nothing non-essential survives.

Every design at Jony's firm Tangerine — and later at Apple — starts the same way: "What's the story of this product?" Not how it's currently made, not what the engineers say it must be — what it should be. On the Newton he asked why the first version "didn't offer a metaphor that users could grasp," then obsessed over the lid: "Before you can turn the product on, you must first open it. I wanted that moment to be special." The ideal is then reached by subtraction: "We kept going back to the beginning again and again. Do we need this part? Can one part do the work of these other four? It became an exercise to reduce and reduce and reduce."

  1. Ask: what is the story of this product?
  2. Start from what it should be — ignore how it is.
  3. Design the first interaction with obsessive care.
  4. Reduce and reduce and reduce.
  5. Ask of every part: do we need it? Can one part replace four?
Example · What

The iMac's handle was never really for carrying — "if you're scared of something, you won't touch it. A handle gives you permission to touch."

So what: the story is friendliness; the part exists to change how you feel.

Example · What

He tells suppliers: "Imagine I have a bucket of money. Pull out as much as you want" — because he refuses to hear "impossible."

So what: starting from the ideal means ignoring constraints until the design is right.

Example · What

"We don't do focus groups — it is unfair to ask people in the context of today to design the opportunities of tomorrow."

So what: the designer's job is to see what should be, not to average current opinion.

AnalogySculptors say the statue is already in the stone — the work is removing everything that isn't it. Design at Jony's level is the same act.

03 / REVERSAL
The great flip

From consensus to design-led.

The old Apple told designers to skin whatever engineering produced. Jobs and Jony reversed the process — and cut forty products down to four.

Before Jobs returned, engineers ruled: they designed the enclosure, and designers "put a nice skin on it." A conceptual brief took four years to become a product; every decision needed consensus in steering committees — "an experiment in extreme democracy." Jony was months from quitting. Then Jobs stood up and screamed "Stop! This is crazy," drew a 2×2 grid — consumer/professional across, portable/desktop down — and Apple would sell four machines, nothing else. Engineers had 38 reasons the iMac handle couldn't be built; Jobs said, "Because I'm the CEO and I think it can be done." Development fell from three years to nine months, and design got the final say.

  1. See the failure: consensus produces middle-of-the-road products.
  2. Cut the portfolio: 40 products → 4 (the 2×2 grid).
  3. Flip the process: design leads; engineering serves it.
  4. Support the design with authority — 38 reasons overruled.
  5. Compress time: three years becomes nine months.
Example · What

Performa 5200CD, 5210CD, 5215CD, 5220CD… Jobs spent three weeks and couldn't explain which to recommend.

So what: if the CEO can't tell the products apart, customers certainly can't.

Example · What

The recessed handle cost real money to manufacture; at the old Apple, "I would have lost that argument."

So what: design-led means someone with power defends intangible value.

Example · What

Jobs approved all the iMac colours in half an hour — "in most places that decision would have taken months."

So what: speed of decisions is a design feature too.

Example · What

A BMW gets you where a half-price Chevy does — "but there will always be those who pay for the better ride."

So what: make first-class products with high margins instead of racing commodity makers to the bottom.

AnalogyThe old Apple was a committee writing a group email; Jobs and Jony replaced it with one author — and the voice finally sounded human.

04 / TEAM
Who did the work

A players, hired to intimidate.

Sixteen designers in one studio — against Samsung's thousand. The hiring bar, the culture of care, and the protection that made it work.

"A small team with A players can run circles around a giant team of B and C players." Jony's studio had about 16 designers in one room; Samsung employed a thousand across 34 centres. The hiring bar was blunt: "We wanted to be impressed with a designer to the point of intimidation." And the bar only works with protection: Jony fell on his sword for the weakest part of any design and never threw teammates under the bus. He kept the work itself above ego — when he asked Jobs to soften the critiques, Jobs answered, "No, Jony, you're just really vain. I thought you held the work up as the most important."

  1. Stay small: a handful of obsessives beats a department.
  2. Hire only people who intimidate you a little.
  3. Obsess past the obvious — 50 models of one button.
  4. Protect the team: absorb blame, never deflect it.
  5. Keep the work, not your image, as the most important thing.
Example · What

"If we design a button, there might be 50 models of it. Is it round? Metal? Plastic? How far out does it protrude?"

So what: "fanatical care beyond the obvious" is a team habit, not a slogan.

Example · What

Graham Duncan's hiring test: "What I'm looking for is a trace of fear in myself that this guy is coming for me."

So what: the same intimidation bar, in a different industry, decades apart.

Example · What

If a design fell short, Jony said it was his fault — never the team member's.

So what: psychological safety is what makes fanatical standards survivable.

AnalogyA jazz sextet versus a thousand-piece orchestra: fewer players, more listening, and nowhere for anyone to hide.
16 in one spot vs 1,000 in 34 centres
Concentration beats scale — when every hire is an A player.

05 / PROOF
What the philosophy produced

Design is how it works.

The iMac saved Apple, the iPod redefined it, and the iPad buried a whole product category — each one a market full of second-rate products, attacked by subtraction.

The iMac shipped August 15, 1998: 278,000 units in six weeks — the fastest-selling computer in Apple history. Jobs spotted markets "filled with second-rate products": MP3 players that "stank." The iPod's design effort "seemed appropriate to simplify, remove, and reduce" — and though the launch drew a muted shrug at $499 ("idiots price our devices"), it eventually sold 450 million units. When everyone else made netbooks — "they aren't better than anything, they're just cheap laptops" — Jony proposed a tablet instead: an inexpensive laptop without a keyboard. By 2011, 63 million iPads outsold 30 million netbooks. One product beat an entire category.

  1. Find the market full of second-rate products.
  2. Design the ideal: simplify, remove, reduce.
  3. Ship — then polish relentlessly with rapid releases.
  4. Ask the netbook question: what is your product better than?
  5. Keep the goal great products; trust profit to follow.
Example · What

iMac: 278,000 sold in six weeks, 800,000 by year end — after the tech press predicted it would flop.

So what: friendliness and a story beat spec sheets; the press measures parts, people buy feelings.

Example · What

The iPod launch was muted — $499, Mac-only, mocked by reviewers. Two years later, Windows support, and it took off: 450 million sold.

So what: a right design can look wrong at first; the parade arrives eventually.

Example · What

"Netbooks aren't better than anything. They're just cheap laptops." Apple made the iPad instead — 63M vs 30M.

So what: refuse me-too products; one better idea outsells a category.

Analogy"Most people think design is how it looks… Design is how it works." The shell is the last inch of a mile of care.
One product vs a whole category
2011 shipments: the iPad, designed around what it should be, buries the netbook — the cheap compromise.

FAQ
From the confusion points

Questions worth asking.

"It is unfair to ask people who don't have a sense of the opportunities of tomorrow, from the context of today, to design." Seeing what should be is the designer's job — the public judges later.

At $499, Mac-only, reviewers mocked it. It sold modestly until Windows support arrived two years later, then reached 450 million units. A right design can look wrong on day one.

Only 10% is traditional industrial design. The ideal must survive contact with reality — months in Chinese factories (including a 3-month SARS quarantine at Foxconn) turning "impossible" into built.

He says no: "You could transplant me and this design group to another place, and we wouldn't work at all." The environment — empowerment, protection, speed — is part of the output.

Denied a raise he had earned, the 23-year-old founded Tangerine — then found partners spent "up to 90% of our time selling our services." "I was really only interested in design. I was neither interested nor good at building a business." Bob Brunner's Apple project (Juggernaut) offered the chance to "give personality and meaning to a technology treated as anonymous" — and "through some sort of reckless sense of faith, I got to yes."

"Our goal is not to make money. Our goal is to make great products. We trust that if we are successful, people will like them, and if we are operationally competent, we will make a profit."
