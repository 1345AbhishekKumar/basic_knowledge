4.5B yrs ago

Chapter 1 · The metal

Gold: The Metal from the Stars

If you took every bit of gold ever mined in all of human history and stacked it into one giant cube in Washington, D.C., it would look like this — pretty big, but smaller than you’d expect. So what is all that gold even for? About 45% of it is decoration and jewelry — necklaces, earrings, a solid-gold Buddha, an ancient Egyptian coffin, and a giant coin with a kangaroo on it. A small portion goes to industry: the fillings in your teeth, electronic components that rely on gold’s special conductive properties. And then a huge portion just sits in vaults around the world, mostly in New York and London, in the form of bars gathering dust.

Where did the gold even come from? A lot of it arrived on Earth aboard an asteroid — heavy metals forged inside exploding stars. If you zoom into one of those metals you’d see something strange: electrons moving nearly at the speed of light, absorbing blue light and reflecting yellow and red. That’s why gold is yellow and shines like the sun. No other metal does this quite like it.

These deposits sat in Earth’s crust for billions of years. Rivers eroded the ground and revealed pieces of shiny stardust, and humans noticed: it was dense but soft and workable, it didn’t rust, it was incorruptible. From China to the Andes, people independently fell under gold’s spell — for decoration, for gods, for rulers. To the Inca, gold was the sweat of the sun. Chinese emperors even ate it trying to become immortal. (Spoiler: that doesn’t work.)

What we do with all the gold ever mined

Hover the bars. Most gold is decoration or idle reserve — not money, not industry.

Step by step

  1. Stars explode. Heavy metals, including gold, are forged in stellar explosions.
  2. An asteroid delivers it. Much of the gold humans have mined arrived on Earth aboard an asteroid.
  3. Rivers reveal it. Erosion uncovers deposits that sat in the crust for billions of years.
  4. Humans notice it’s different. It’s dense, soft, workable — and it never tarnishes.
  5. Cultures fall for it. From China to the Andes, gold becomes sacred, royal, and decorative.
  6. It goes to work. 45% jewelry, a little industry, and a huge pile sitting in vaults.

Three examples

The obvious case

A wedding ring. Most of the gold we touch is on our fingers and necks — 45% of all gold ever mined is jewelry and decoration. That’s the metal’s most famous job.

The counter-intuitive case

Gold is not mostly money, and it’s not mostly industry. A giant share of all gold ever mined is sitting in vaults, gathering dust. Its value isn’t coming from being used — it’s coming from what people believe it means.

The edge case

Gold in your teeth and in electronics. Dental fillings and circuit components use gold’s special physical properties — it conducts, it doesn’t corrode — for reasons that have nothing to do with how it looks.

Visual analogy

Gold is like a souvenir from an exploded star — literally born in a stellar explosion, delivered by asteroid, and so rare-but-not-too-rare that every culture on Earth independently decided it was special.

❌ What people get wrong

“Gold is extremely rare.”
Reality: Gold is rare but not too rare. There’s enough of it to circulate as money, but not so much that it’s everywhere. That balance is exactly what makes it useful.
“Gold is valuable because of what it does.”
Reality: It does surprisingly little. It doesn’t feed you, keep you warm, or shelter you. It’s valuable because people believe it’s valuable — a point that becomes the whole story of money.
“Most gold is used for money or industry.”
Reality: About 45% is decoration and jewelry, and a huge portion sits in vaults. Industry uses only a small slice.

Why this matters

Gold’s physical properties — durable, divisible, scarce, shiny — made it the perfect raw material for money. But the metal alone isn’t enough. Money needs one more ingredient, and that ingredient changes everything.

10,000 yrs ago

Chapter 2 · The leap

The Invention of Money

Before money, there was barter — trading something I want for something you want. But soon humans started settling on commonly useful things they could carry, valuable to most people: barley, salt, cattle. Cows are the most obvious version of money. They’re food, they’re transportable (they can walk), and they’re easy to maintain (they eat grass). Money that helps you survive.

But cows make bad money. They spoil. They’re hard to divide. You can’t easily bring them far away to trade. So eventually humans found something better — that shiny, soft metal from the riverbeds. Gold is easy to shape, easy to split up, easy to carry around, and it just looks cool. It was rare, but not too rare: enough of it, but not everywhere.

Here’s the twist. Gold doesn’t feed you, keep you warm, or shelter you. You can make jewelry or a dagger, but it’s not that intrinsically useful — unless you add a very powerful, very human ingredient: belief. This person believes gold is valuable, and so does that person. Belief turns metal into a system. He trades gold for wheat; you take the gold and trade it for salt or fabric. Gold becomes valuable not because of what it does, but because of what we believe it means.

That’s the big psychological step: from cattle, which has utility, to gold, which has no utility but is scarce, durable, divisible, universal — and shiny. And it all works because of collective belief: if everyone believes they can spend it later, it works. Gold and silver became the dominant monies of the world, stamped into standardized coins.

The leap from useful things to believed things.

Step by step

  1. Barter first. You have salt, I have wheat — we swap.
  2. Common goods emerge. Barley, salt, and cattle become early forms of money because most people need them.
  3. Cattle fail the test. They spoil, can’t be divided, and can’t travel far.
  4. Gold passes the test. It’s easy to shape, split, carry — and it looks great.
  5. Belief is added. Gold becomes valuable because people agree it is, not because it feeds you.
  6. Coins are stamped. Gold and silver become standardized, near-universal money.

Three examples

The obvious case

Cattle as money. Cows were something many people needed for food, they were transportable because they could walk, and they were easy to maintain because they eat grass. The most practical, useful version of money.

The counter-intuitive case

Gold has no utility — you can’t eat it, sleep under it, or burn it for heat — yet it beat cattle. Its value comes entirely from collective belief: “if I have this metal, I can trade it for what I need.”

The edge case

The Aztecs. When the Spanish arrived in 1519, the Aztecs had enormous amounts of gold — but used it for decoration, worship, armor, and blades, not as currency. Same metal, different belief system. Gold’s role depends on what people believe it’s for.

Visual analogy

Money is a shared password. Anyone can say the word “gold,” but it only unlocks value when enough people agree it does. The metal is just the container — the belief is the treasure.

❌ What people get wrong

“Money has always been coins and paper.”
Reality: Money has been cattle, barley, salt, and shells. The form changes constantly; the engine — collective belief — never does.
“Gold is valuable because it’s useful.”
Reality: Gold is barely useful. Its value is psychological: “gold becomes valuable not because of what it does, but because of what we believe it means.”
“Money was invented by governments.”
Reality: Money emerged bottom-up, from trade and shared needs — barley, salt, cattle — long before governments stamped coins with their authority.

Why this matters

Every form of money that follows — paper, fiat, crypto — runs on the exact same engine: collective belief. If you understand the leap from cattle to gold, you understand the dollar and Bitcoin.

1900

Chapter 3 · The abstraction

Paper and the Gold Standard

Gold has a problem: it’s heavy. If you want to trade it in large quantities, carrying it around is a pain. So people decided: deposit your gold in a bank, and the bank gives you a bank note — essentially an IOU. One ounce of gold was worth approximately $20. So if you had a $20 bill, you could go into a bank and redeem it for one ounce of gold.

Most people got paid in envelopes stuffed with banknotes — that was the everyday medium of exchange. As long as you trusted that the notes were good, you didn’t even think about which bank issued them; they all exchanged one-to-one against each other and against gold and silver dollars. And if you wanted gold instead of the note, you could come in and redeem it.

So now, instead of believing in gold, we’re believing in banks — trusting that this piece of paper represents gold sitting safe in a vault, and that at any moment you could walk in and get the real thing. That’s a pretty far abstraction from cows. But it follows the same rule: if everyone believes it, and the banks don’t give us reason not to, then worthless paper turns into economic power. It makes trade way easier.

There was one famous subplot: silver. Many economies tried to run on both metals, pegging their values so they wouldn’t drift apart. But it’s really hard to have two metals as the foundation of your paper money. When a big silver discovery hit Nevada, silver’s value wobbled against its peg, and all this arbitrage happened. It became a decades-long, hugely political US debate — some people even think The Wizard of Oz is an allegory for it. By 1900, gold had won: $20 for one ounce, a finite supply, the gold standard.

The gold standard: paper was a promise redeemable in gold.

Step by step

  1. Gold is heavy. Large trades are impractical with physical metal.
  2. Deposit gold at a bank. The bank gives you a note — an IOU.
  3. Notes circulate. Pay envelopes full of banknotes become everyday money.
  4. Trust replaces metal. You believe in the bank, not just the gold.
  5. The gold standard locks in. $20 = 1 oz of gold; a finite supply; stability.
  6. Silver loses. Bimetallism breaks down under arbitrage, and gold endures alone.

Three examples

The obvious case

A $20 bill. Under the gold standard, that bill wasn’t just paper — it was a claim on one ounce of gold sitting in a bank vault, redeemable on demand.

The counter-intuitive case

Nobody actually redeemed most notes. The system worked because people trusted they could — the paper circulated on faith in the bank, a much bigger psychological step than anyone noticed at the time.

The edge case

The silver debate. Running money on two metals at once is fragile: when Nevada found more silver, its value shifted against the peg and arbitrage exploded. The gold-versus-silver fight turned into decades of US politics — and possibly a children’s book.

Visual analogy

A bank note is a coat-check ticket. The paper itself is worthless — its value is the coat (the gold) hanging in the vault, and your trust that you can go get it whenever you want.

❌ What people get wrong

“Paper money used to be made of or backed by gold.”
Reality: Bank notes were IOUs redeemable for gold — but what made them circulate was trust in the banks, not the metal itself. Even then, banks had limits on how many notes they could issue.
“The gold standard made the economy stable.”
Reality: It also created financial panics. When people wanted more currency than the banks could issue, they drained gold from vaults — a setup for bank runs.
“The Wizard of Oz is just a children’s story.”
Reality: Many economists and historians read it as a commentary on the gold-versus-silver debate — Dorothy’s silver shoes and the yellow brick road being the two metals.

Why this matters

Paper money moved belief one step further away from physical metal. It also revealed the system’s weakness — panics — which would lead to the creation of a central bank.

1913–1933

Chapter 4 · The leap of faith

The Fed and Fiat Money

Early in the 1900s, a series of financial panics exposed the weakness of private banks handing out paper money. Banks could only issue so many notes; when people wanted more currency, they pulled gold and silver coins out of vaults, draining reserves. And when everyone rushes to pull their money out at once — a bank run — the whole system wobbles. An economy grows when banks lend money out and trust that not everyone will demand it back simultaneously.

So in 1913, the US government created one bank to rule them all: the Federal Reserve. The Fed would be the monopoly provider of dollars, cope with panics, and set the rules for other banks by issuing more currency when desired. It came with levers — most famously the interest rate. Lower the rate, and more people borrow, spend, and start businesses; more jobs. The Fed was still technically pegged to gold — but a huge difference: you could no longer simply walk into the bank and exchange your paper for gold.

Then came the Great Depression. Most economists agreed the Fed should have lowered interest rates and pumped money into the economy. But it couldn’t — the money supply was fixed to gold. If it issued more notes, it would run out of gold to back them; if people demanded gold bars, the whole system would collapse. So, to defend gold, the Fed hiked rates instead. Historians broadly agree this made the Depression last longer — a decade of misery.

In 1933, FDR issued an executive order: Americans turned in their gold bars for flimsy dollar bills. The government could now print as much or as little money as it wanted — and the more it prints, the less each dollar is worth. That’s inflation. This is fiat money: value by decree, backed by belief in the government, not by gold.

Sixteen years from the Fed’s founding to the end of the gold standard.

Step by step

  1. Panics spread. Bank runs drain gold reserves, destabilizing private banks.
  2. The Fed is born (1913). One central bank to rule them all, with levers like the interest rate.
  3. The 1920s boom. Critics say cheap credit fed an overinvestment boom — the Roaring Twenties.
  4. The bubble bursts. The Great Depression begins; bank runs drain gold into mattresses.
  5. The Fed can’t act. The money supply is fixed to gold, so it hikes rates to defend the metal — making things worse.
  6. 1933: fiat money. FDR orders gold turned in for dollars. Money is now belief in government, not metal.

Three examples

The obvious case

The interest-rate lever. Lower it, and borrowing is cheaper — more people take loans, start businesses, and create jobs. That’s the Fed’s core tool for steering the economy.

The counter-intuitive case

During the worst depression in history, the Fed raised interest rates — the opposite of what economists recommended — because it was defending the gold peg. Protecting the metal made the misery last a decade longer.

The edge case

The “unholy trinity.” You can’t simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate (gold), free capital flows, and independent monetary policy. Pegged to gold, the Fed lost its ability to rescue the economy — the technical trap behind 1933.

Visual analogy

Fiat money is a handshake with the government. No gold, no silver — just a shared agreement that the institution will keep the machine running. The strongest backing isn’t metal; it’s credibility.

❌ What people get wrong

“Fiat money is backed by nothing.”
Reality: It’s backed by collective belief in the government and its institutions — the same belief that made gold valuable, just one more step removed from the metal.
“The Fed is a literal machine with levers.”
Reality: The famous machine is completely symbolic. The Fed’s “levers” are policies like interest rates and money supply — indirect, invisible nudges on how people borrow and spend.
“Printing money always means hyperinflation.”
Reality: The more you print, the less each is worth — that’s inflation. But managed printing is a tool, not automatic doom. The Fed’s job is to guide the amount to keep prices roughly stable.

Why this matters

This was the biggest psychological leap in the story: from believing in a shiny metal to believing in an institution. Every dollar, euro, and yen in your wallet is fiat — and it works only as long as the belief holds.

1944–1971

Chapter 5 · The global currency

Bretton Woods and the Dollar

In 1944, with World War II winding down, the United States was the most powerful country on Earth. It gathered its allies — a couple dozen countries — in a hotel in the mountains of New Hampshire to design a new global economic system. The US proposal: instead of a universal global currency, make the US dollar the universal, globally accepted currency that everyone else’s money attaches to.

The other countries were skeptical. Trusting the US and its dollar seemed sketchy. So the US made a promise: every dollar is backed by gold. Ordinary citizens couldn’t turn in dollars for gold anymore — but foreign countries could. $35 per ounce, redeemable anytime, held in the vault at Fort Knox, Kentucky. And the US had a lot of gold, because much of the world had traded its gold to America in exchange for weapons during the war. The deal was accepted. The dollar became the king of global trade and investing — and gold was once again at the center of the concept of money.

But not for long. The US kept printing dollars, and the world caught on. Germany and France started redeeming — France even sent a Navy ship to New York in the 1960s to demand its gold. Eventually the Treasury ran out. In 1971, President Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold: he closed the gold window. His argument to the world: you’re used to the dollar, we’re America, we’re stable, look at our economy and military — just keep trusting us. And the world, without much choice, said, “Okay, fine.”

So here we are: a global economy running on a currency built on collective belief in a story — a system, an S with a line through it.

The promise that made the dollar the world’s money — and the promise that broke.

Step by step

  1. 1944, Bretton Woods. Allied countries gather in New Hampshire to design a global system.
  2. The dollar is crowned. Instead of a global currency, the US dollar becomes the anchor everyone attaches to.
  3. Gold backs the promise. $35 per ounce, redeemable by foreign governments from Fort Knox.
  4. The world takes the deal. The dollar becomes king of global trade and investment.
  5. Printing gets out of hand. France and Germany redeem; France sends a Navy ship; the Treasury runs dry.
  6. 1971: Nixon closes the gold window. Convertibility ends. The world stays on dollars anyway.

Three examples

The obvious case

Oil, trade, and government bonds — priced and settled in dollars. The US dollar became the world’s reserve currency, and most of the global economy still runs through it.

The counter-intuitive case

France literally sent a naval ship to New York in the 1960s to demand gold for its dollars. The US ran out of gold — the strongest country in the world couldn’t keep the promise that anchored the entire system.

The edge case

After 1971, the world stayed on the dollar anyway — out of habit, necessity, and lack of a better option. Path dependence is powerful: once everyone uses a currency, it’s very hard to switch.

Visual analogy

The dollar became the world’s shared language. Even after the “gold dictionary” that promised its meaning was withdrawn, everyone kept speaking it — because everyone else was speaking it.

❌ What people get wrong

“The US went off the gold standard in 1933.”
Reality: Domestically, yes — Americans had to turn in gold. But internationally, the dollar remained gold-convertible for foreign governments at $35/oz until Nixon closed the gold window in 1971.
“Fort Knox is empty / just a museum.”
Reality: The US still holds thousands of tons of gold at Fort Knox and other facilities. Governments never got rid of their gold — they kept it as a “just in case” reserve.
“Nixon’s 1971 move was a small technicality.”
Reality: It ended the last link between the world’s money and a physical metal. After 1971, global money became pure fiat — belief in governments and systems.

Why this matters

Since 1971, the entire global economy runs on fiat — on trust. And trust is exactly what starts to wobble when people get nervous, which brings us to why gold is making a comeback.

Today

Chapter 6 · The return

Gold’s Comeback and Digital Gold

So why is everyone buying gold again? The price of gold keeps hitting record highs. Foreign governments never stopped buying it — Russia and China have been loading up on bars, and some places are even trying to make gold usable as money again. It seems like gold is having a comeback. But the truth is, it never went away.

Since the world left the gold standard in 1971 — and even before — people have held gold as a hedge against inflation. Governments never gave up their reserves: the US still holds thousands of tons in Fort Knox, and many countries store gold in New York City, in a vault buried 50 feet into the bedrock of Manhattan, sitting there as a just-in-case reserve.

Regular people buy gold when they start feeling sketchy about the big psychological bargain they’ve made with the government. That bargain is squishy — it requires us to believe, and even to trust. Recently that trust has been waning: rival nations want the world to stop trusting the dollar, tariffs and policy whiplash have spooked people, and when the president attacks the Federal Reserve, people turn to gold in a bigger and bigger way. People rush to stores of value that don’t rely on a central government.

And there’s another currency that looks a lot like gold — at least mentally: Bitcoin. Scarce, divisible, no central authority — “digital gold.” Some think crypto could be the next chapter in this psychological journey. It’s too early to tell. But one thing seems certain: whenever things feel uncertain, that shiny metal will always remain in the background — the thing we turn to when we lose faith.

Interactive: Why people buy gold

Inflation hedge

Since 1971, gold has been held as protection against the dollar losing value — when the more you print, the less each is worth, gold is the classic refuge.

Step by step

  1. 1971. The gold window closes; the dollar becomes pure fiat.
  2. Inflation fears rise. Investors reach for gold as a hedge against the dollar losing value.
  3. Governments never left. Central banks keep thousands of tons of gold as just-in-case reserves.
  4. Trust wobbles. Tariffs, attacks on the Fed, and geopolitical rivals shake faith in the dollar.
  5. Gold rallies. Record prices as people rush to stores of value outside government control.
  6. Digital gold arrives. Bitcoin offers scarcity and divisibility without any central authority.

Three examples

The obvious case

Investors buying gold when inflation worries spike. Central banks in Russia and China have been on a gold-buying spree, and gold prices have hit record highs.

The counter-intuitive case

Gold pays no interest, produces nothing, and just sits there — yet governments never got rid of it. The US still holds thousands of tons. Its value is pure belief: a just-in-case reserve against a loss of faith in the system.

The edge case

Bitcoin. It shares gold’s psychology — scarce, divisible, no central authority — but it’s digital and volatile. It may be a future path for money, or it may not. Too early to tell, but it proves the belief engine is still running.

Visual analogy

Gold is the emergency exit of the money system. You hope you never need it — but when the building feels shaky, it’s the door everyone instinctively heads for.

❌ What people get wrong

“Gold is obsolete.”
Reality: Governments still hold thousands of tons of it. Gold never went away — it stayed in the background as the reserve asset of last resort.
“Buying gold is a guaranteed investment.”
Reality: Gold is a hedge and store of value, not an income-producing asset. Its price fluctuates; its role is stability of belief, not growth.
“Bitcoin is mainly a currency for buying things.”
Reality: Its real pitch is “digital gold” — a scarce, divisible store of value outside government control. A psychological twin of gold, not a replacement for dollars at the checkout.

Why this matters

The story of money is really the story of trust. When people trust institutions, paper works. When they don’t, they reach for metal — or math. Understanding that engine is the whole lesson.

1950→today

Chapter 7 · The dream

The American Dream, Measured

The economy has grown immensely since the 1950s — the largest economic pie on Earth, even after adjusting for inflation and population. But a crucial question remains: is that prosperity actually shared? In the 1950s, the answer looked like a yes. A median factory worker earned about $4,400 a year (roughly $41,000 in today’s dollars), could afford an $84,000 house, and had a nearly 100% chance of earning more than their parents. Government programs — veterans’ mortgages, public education, a high top tax rate — helped build the suburbs and let ordinary families start building wealth.

But look closer and the picture splits. That prosperity was broadly shared among high, middle, and low earners — all three lines rising together — but women were mostly left out of this version of the dream, and so were Black Americans, who returned from the war to redlining maps that decided where they could and couldn’t buy homes. Still, if you were a typical white male earner, the 1950s and 1970s delivered: incomes grew together, and CEO pay was a modest 26 times the typical worker’s salary.

Then, around 1980, the lines diverged. Top earners started pulling away while median pay flattened. By the late 1990s, CEOs made almost 400 times the typical worker. In 2010 the median household earned $81,000 — the same as in 1997 — even though the economy had grown enormously. And the odds of out-earning your parents fell from nearly 100% to about 50%. The economy isn’t broken — it’s working exactly as designed. The rules and policies we choose decide who gets the money.

Incomes rose together through the 1970s — then diverged around 1980.

Step by step

  1. 1950s: shared prosperity. A factory job supports a family; an $84,000 house is affordable; government helps.
  2. 1970s: still together. Women enter the workforce (at 60% of men’s pay), and all income brackets keep rising at the same rate.
  3. ~1980: the divergence. Top earners pull away; median pay starts to flatten.
  4. Productivity splits from pay. The economy produces more, but the typical worker’s wage stalls.
  5. CEO pay explodes. From 26× the typical worker in the 1970s to ~400× by the late 1990s.
  6. The dream fades. Odds of out-earning your parents drop from ~100% to ~50%.

Three examples

The obvious case

The 1950s median earner. One factory salary bought a house and supported a family; nearly everyone earned more than their parents. The American dream was, for many, a statistical near-certainty.

The counter-intuitive case

The economy grew massively from 1997 to 2010 — yet median household income stayed at $81,000. More prosperity, same pay for the middle. That’s not a failure of growth; it’s a choice about who receives it.

The edge case

The 1950s dream wasn’t universal. Women and Black Americans — a tenth of the population, over a million of whom fought in the war — were largely excluded, with redlining maps deciding who could build wealth through a home.

Visual analogy

Think of the economy as a rising tide. Until the 1980s, it lifted all boats — yachts, dinghies, everything. Since then, it lifts the yachts while the dinghies sit still. The water is rising; not everyone is in it.

❌ What people get wrong

“The economy is broken.”
Reality: It’s working exactly as designed. Growth continues; the issue is distribution. Rules and policies — taxes, regulation, union power, privatization — decide who gets the money.
“Inequality is just the price of growth — it lifts all boats.”
Reality: The data says otherwise: productivity rose while median pay flatlined, and poverty stopped falling after ~1980. Growth and broad sharing are not opposites.
“The American dream is alive if you work hard.”
Reality: For those born in the 1950s, ~100% out-earned their parents. By the 1980s, a majority of median earners made the same or less. The rungs of the ladder have spread apart.

Why this matters

Money runs on belief — and belief in the system erodes when the system stops delivering for most people. Inequality isn’t just unfair; it’s destabilizing for the trust that makes money work at all.

1964→today

Chapter 8 · The middlemen

Who Gets Rich Off the Poor

In 1964, Lyndon Johnson declared an unconditional war on poverty — and for a while it worked. Social spending skyrocketed: Medicare, Medicaid, food stamps, public education. The poverty rate fell from about 22% to 12–13%, and poverty declined 30% in just five years. Direct government help was reaching people.

Then came the 1980s. The economy soured, taxpayers tired of subsidizing welfare, and CEOs were put on a pedestal as the people who could make government as efficient as business. Ronald Reagan kicked off, and Bill Clinton accelerated, a fundamental shift: instead of the government giving money directly to citizens in need, the money now flows taxpayer → government → states → for-profit companies → eligible people. Those companies verify who qualifies and check that recipients are working. The theory: private incentives would cut waste.

Sometimes it worked — EBT cards replaced paper food-stamp coupons. But the incentives also run the other way. A company like Maximus, one of the largest welfare middlemen, went from $88 million in revenue in 1995 to $4.9 billion today. When it ran Tennessee’s Medicaid program, uninsured children jumped 23% — the worst in the nation. In Kansas, applications piled into months-long backlogs. Why would a company kick kids and elderly people off a health program? Because its “customers” are governments, not the needy — and every case processed is a cost. The vulnerable aren’t the customer; they’re the inventory.

The pattern repeats across the system: tax-prep chains skimming earned-income tax credits, Section 8 landlords overcharging by a million dollars a month in DC alone, Medicaid dentists billing for root canals on toddlers, dialysis chains delaying transplants to keep the $380-per-treatment chairs full, and middlemen paid per foster child to divert their $700-a-month benefits into state coffers.

Since the 1980s, aid flows through for-profit middlemen — whose incentives are not your incentives.

Step by step

  1. 1964: War on Poverty. Direct government aid; poverty falls from 22% to 12–13%.
  2. 1980s: privatization. Money flows taxpayer → government → states → for-profit companies.
  3. Middlemen take over eligibility. Companies verify who qualifies and who’s working.
  4. Incentives kick in. For a company, fewer recipients means less work — and more profit.
  5. Exploitation follows. Tax prep skims credits, landlords inflate rents, dentists bill for fake procedures.
  6. The vulnerable pay. Children, the elderly, and the disabled bear the cost of “efficiency.”

Three examples

The obvious case

The earned income tax credit. In most countries it’s a checkbox on a free government site. In the US it’s a 45-page instruction manual — and companies like Liberty Tax advertise to low-income people, then skim refunds or sell refund-anticipation loans at high interest.

The counter-intuitive case

Maximus in Tennessee. A for-profit company running a state health program oversaw a 23% jump in uninsured children — more than any other state. Kicking kids off Medicaid isn’t a bug; it’s a cost-cutting feature.

The edge case

Foster children. The federal government entitles them to ~$700/month if a parent died or they’re disabled. Middlemen scour databases to find qualifying kids — and divert that money into state coffers, earning a fee per child found.

Visual analogy

Privatizing welfare is like hiring a toll collector who gets paid per car that doesn’t reach the destination. The road exists to help people cross — but the business model rewards keeping them from arriving.

❌ What people get wrong

“Privatization made welfare more efficient for everyone.”
Reality: Some things improved (EBT cards). But the same profit motive that cuts waste also cuts service — delays, denials, and backlogs land on the most vulnerable.
“Welfare fraud by recipients is the big problem.”
Reality: The bigger distortion is corporate: middlemen paid to minimize cases, dentists billing for procedures that never happened, dialysis chains delaying transplants to keep machines full.
“These companies are evil — they should be blamed.”
Reality: They’re behaving exactly as for-profit corporations should: maximizing profit. The failure is the incentive structure and the missing oversight — not the nature of any single CEO.

Why this matters

Money is belief — and when the system meant to help the poorest instead enriches middlemen, that belief breaks down. The fixes exist: oversight, competition, profit caps. What’s missing is the demand for them.

Chapter 9 · The food bill

Why Your Groceries Got Expensive

Grocery prices usually rise 1–2% a year. Recently they’ve jumped closer to 13% a year — and for people who spend a big share of their income on food, a small price shock hits hard. Everyone blames COVID and inflation, and those are part of the story. But something bigger is going on.

First, the good news: trade made food cheap. In the late 1700s, British families spent about 75% of their income on food (in France, ~70% on bread alone). By 1900 it was 42%; by the 1930s, 34%. After World War II, standardized containers, global standards, and a dollar-backed trading system stitched the world together — that $2 tomato in your store traveled 620 miles from Mexico through cold storage, customs, and distribution centers in nine days. A $6 farmers-market tomato grew five miles away. The global system is amazing — and fragile.

So why the price spike? Answer one: supply chain shocks. Droughts, wars, a freeze in Texas, a hurricane in Florida, bird flu — each ripples through the chain, and the last customer pays. Answer two is the uncomfortable one: pricing power. After COVID, companies realized they could inch prices up with almost no drop in demand — so they kept doing it. Eggs are the cleanest case: bird flu was real, but production barely dipped — while the largest producer, controlling ~25% of the market, saw profits triple and paid shareholders $250 million, forty times the year before. Four firms handle 85% of US beef; three control 78% of pasta; two own 70% of canned tuna. When a few giants dominate, they don’t compete on price — they match prices. Corporate margins hit their highest level since 1950.

Step by step

  1. Trade makes food cheap. Global systems cut food from 75% of income to ~11%.
  2. Supply chains get fragile. One tomato: 620 miles, nine days, dozens of links.
  3. Shocks hit. COVID, droughts, wars, freezes, bird flu — each ripple raises prices.
  4. Pricing power appears. Companies find they can raise prices without losing customers.
  5. Consolidation concentrates. Four firms = 85% of beef; giants match prices instead of competing.
  6. Margins soar. Corporate profits hit record highs while families pay more.

Three examples

The obvious case

Supply chain shocks. The Ukraine war spiked wheat and fertilizer; a Texas freeze wiped out citrus; droughts hit olive oil and canola. Real events, real cost increases — and the consumer at the end of the chain pays.

The counter-intuitive case

Eggs. Bird flu was real, and hens died — but national egg production barely dipped. Meanwhile the biggest producer’s profits tripled. The crisis was as much storytelling as supply and demand — a smokescreen for raising prices beyond costs.

The edge case

Mexico’s “basic basket.” The government negotiated a fixed low price for a basket of staples — rice, beef, vegetables, milk — with producers and retailers. Prices there actually fell ~12% in a year. Policy can fight the trend.

Visual analogy

Your grocery store is the end of a suspension bridge built over centuries. Crossing it is cheap and miraculous — but when one cable snaps, everyone on the bridge sways, and the toll operators (a few giant firms) get to set the price.

❌ What people get wrong

“Inflation is all COVID and supply chains.”
Reality: Shocks are real, but record corporate margins show another driver: prices raised beyond costs. The CFO of Hershey said it plainly: pricing “more than offset” higher costs.
“Companies just pass on their costs.”
Reality: They pass on costs — and then some. When one company controls a quarter of a market, it can raise prices without competitors undercutting it.
“Competition keeps food prices low.”
Reality: Not when the market is consolidated. Four firms handle most US beef, three most pasta, two most canned tuna. Giants match prices; they don’t compete on them.

Why this matters

Food isn’t a luxury — it’s survival. When the system that feeds you also inflates your bill to boost shareholder payouts, trust in the whole economy erodes. That’s a market failure — and the place where government steps in matters.

Chapter 10 · The fortress

The Scale of Ultra-Wealth

Let’s zoom to the very top of the wealth ladder — from $100 million to $100 billion. Meet Scott, worth $100 million from selling a candy company. His money sits in the stock market, growing about 10% a year. If he sells to spend, he pays ~37% in taxes. So instead, he does what the ultra-rich do: he borrows. A bank will lend him up to 85% of his portfolio’s value, using that portfolio as collateral. The loan isn’t income — it’s debt — so he pays zero tax on the money he spends. And his portfolio grows at 10% while his loan costs ~5.9% interest. His wealth outruns his debt, forever.

Now meet Javier, worth $1 billion. He wrote a $200,000 check to a startup that became an $80 billion company. He owns seven homes, a $75 million private jet, and a bunker in New Zealand. His army of tax professionals finds legal ways to shrink his bill — including donating $20 million to a senator’s campaign, who later inserted eight words into a bill that saved Javier hundreds of millions in taxes.

And Bradley, worth $100 billion. He has 2,000 cars, a $600 million yacht, and $2 billion in homes. He doesn’t sell stock (that would trigger taxes) — he borrows billions against it, like Elon Musk did to buy Twitter. And when he dies, the “step-up basis” resets his gains to zero, so his heirs inherit a fortune with no capital-gains tax at all. Wealth begets wealth: assets become collateral for tax-free loans, which buy influence, which writes policy, which grows the assets. Meanwhile, 44 million Americans don’t have enough food. This isn’t a broken system — it’s one working exactly as designed.

The buy-borrow-die loop: never sell, borrow instead, reset taxes at death.

Step by step

  1. Assets, not cash. The ultra-rich hold wealth in stocks, growing ~10% a year.
  2. Don’t sell — borrow. Loans against portfolios are debt, not income: no tax.
  3. Let growth outrun interest. Portfolio +10% vs loan at ~5.9% = the debt pays for itself.
  4. Buy influence. Campaign donations, lobbying, foundations — all tax-advantaged.
  5. Reset at death. The step-up basis wipes out capital gains for heirs.
  6. The fortress grows. Each layer — assets, loans, influence, policy — protects the next.

Three examples

The obvious case

Scott’s loan trick. A $100 million portfolio can borrow tens of millions at ~5.9% — tax-free spending, while the portfolio grows at 10%. The richer you are, the cheaper borrowing gets relative to your growth.

The counter-intuitive case

The richest can pay lower effective tax rates than the middle class. Robert, at $25 million a year, can pay less than Tim at $100,000 — because Robert’s wealth is mostly unrealized stock, untaxed until sold, and he borrows rather than sells.

The edge case

The step-up basis. When Bradley dies, his heirs inherit his fortune as if the gains never happened — billions in unrealized gains are never taxed. Buy, borrow, die: the estate tax is the only checkpoint, and it can be planned around.

Visual analogy

Wealth at this scale is a fortress that builds its own walls. Money buys loans; loans buy influence; influence writes policy; policy protects money. Each ring of the fortress makes the next one easier to build — while the ground outside stays still.

❌ What people get wrong

“The rich pay more taxes than anyone.”
Reality: In dollar terms, yes — in rate terms, often no. Unrealized gains aren’t taxed until sold, and borrowing means never selling. Effective rates can be lower than the middle class.
“Philanthropy is pure generosity.”
Reality: It can be — but foundations also function as tax shelters and vanity projects, and “accessibility” requirements are sometimes met with two-hour lottery tours on random Wednesdays.
“The system is broken — it must be failing.”
Reality: It’s working exactly as designed: wealth compounds, influence is purchasable, and the rules are written by those with the most to gain. If that feels unfair, the question is whether to redesign it.

Why this matters

Money is belief — and concentrated wealth concentrates the power to shape that belief. The psychology of money ends where it began: with a choice about what we collectively agree to value, and who the system serves.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is gold actually a good investment?
Gold is a store of value and a hedge against inflation and distrust — not an income-producing asset. It pays no interest and its price fluctuates. People buy it for stability of belief, not for growth.
Did pirates really bury chests of gold?
Mostly a myth. Most pirates weren’t after gold at all — they chased more boring cargo like lumber, and they didn’t really make X-marks-the-spot maps. The story is a useful example of how gold fired the imagination and inflamed greed.
Was The Wizard of Oz really about gold and silver?
Many economists and historians argue the story is an allegory for the late-1800s gold-versus-silver political debate — Dorothy’s silver shoes and the yellow brick road representing the two metals. It’s a theory, but a famous one.
If the dollar isn’t backed by gold, what backs it?
Collective belief — trust in the US government, its economy, its institutions, and the Federal Reserve’s ability to keep the system stable. The dollar works because everyone agrees to accept it, exactly like gold did.
Does the Fed control everything about the economy?
No. The Fed steers — mostly through interest rates and the money supply — but the economy is a giant, confusing system shaped by policy, markets, technology, and human behavior. Economists themselves disagree about how well the Fed does.
Is Bitcoin really “digital gold”?
Psychologically, yes: it’s scarce, divisible, and outside central-government control. But it’s volatile and very new. It may become a chapter in the journey of money — or it may not. Too early to tell.
Is the American Dream really dead?
Not dead — but measurably harder. People born in the 1950s had a ~100% chance of out-earning their parents; people born in the 1980s face a majority chance of earning the same or less. The economy still grows; the sharing of it changed after ~1980.
Do the ultra-rich really pay less tax than the middle class?
In rate terms, often yes. Unrealized stock gains aren’t taxed until sold, and the wealthy borrow against their assets instead of selling — so they can have enormous spending power while paying little income tax. At death, the step-up basis can erase the tax entirely.
Are grocery prices going up just because of inflation?
Inflation and supply shocks are real — droughts, wars, bird flu. But record corporate margins suggest something else too: in consolidated markets, a few giants can raise prices beyond their costs and keep them there.
