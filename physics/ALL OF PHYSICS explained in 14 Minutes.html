MODULE 01

Force & Motion — Newton's Laws

The ground rules. Everything else in this lesson is these rules wearing different costumes.

A force is simply a push or a pull on something, in a certain direction. Mass tells you how much stuff there is — and it doubles as a measure of inertia, the reluctance of an object to change its motion. Acceleration is just how fast velocity is changing. Put those together and you get Newton's second law: F = ma. Apply a known force to a known mass and you get a predictable acceleration — which is why, if you know the forces on a basketball mid-air, you can predict with near-certainty whether it drops through the hoop or through your neighbour's windshield.

The famous first law is sneakier. Objects don't need a force to keep moving; they need a force to change their motion. A skateboard rolling on a flat path keeps rolling until something — friction, a wall — interferes. The planets got their velocity when the solar system formed from a spinning cloud of gas, and since there's nothing in space to slow them down, they just keep going. That single sentence explains the whole sky.

Notice the emphasis: force causes a change in motion, not motion itself. If an object moves at constant speed in a straight line, the net force on it is zero — even if it's a jet cruising at 900 km/h. Understanding that distinction is where most intuition about the physical world quietly goes wrong.

✕ What people get wrong

"No force, no motion." That was Aristotle's guess — and it's wrong. A puck on ice keeps gliding with zero net force. Friction is the force that stops it, not the absence of push.

Once you see it this way, "force causes change in motion" explains why you lurch forward when a bus brakes: no force pushed you, your body just kept its old velocity (Newton's first law) while the bus slowed beneath you.

Why this matters

Every engineered motion is F = ma tuned: brake pads sized for a car's mass, rocket thrust sized for payload, airbags designed around the force of sudden deceleration. Get this law, and the rest of physics is mostly new costumes on the same idea.

MODULE 02

Gravity & Weight — The Pull That Never Stops

Why an apple falls, why planets orbit, and why you weigh less on the Moon.

When an apple fell on Newton's head, he didn't just shrug — he proposed that every mass attracts every other mass. The pull depends on how much mass there is and how far apart the two bodies are. More mass, bigger pull. More distance, smaller pull — much smaller than you'd guess, because the force shrinks with the square of the distance. Double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter. That's the inverse-square law, and it's the quiet engine behind orbits, tides, and falling apples alike.

Orbits are the payoff. When the solar system formed from a spinning cloud of gas, the planets inherited sideways velocity. Nothing in space stops them — Newton's first law again — so they keep moving. Meanwhile the Sun's gravity keeps bending their path inward. Put the two together and you get continuous falling that never lands: the planet falls toward the Sun, misses, curves around, and repeats forever. That inward-bending pull is called a centripetal force — "centre-seeking." Real orbits are mostly egg-shaped ellipses rather than perfect circles, and Pluto's is, frankly, a mess. But the idea holds: orbit = falling sideways forever.

Finally, the mass-versus-weight trap. Mass is how much stuff you are — the same on Earth and the Moon. Weight is the force gravity exerts on that mass. Weaker gravity, smaller weight. So really, you're not overweight — you're just on the wrong planet.

✕ What people get wrong

"Astronauts float because there's no gravity up there." There is plenty — the ISS feels about 90% of Earth's pull. The crew floats for the same reason a dropped elevator would make you float: they're all falling together.

Same for "you're weightless in orbit": the floor, the walls, the astronauts — everything accelerates toward Earth at the same rate, so nothing presses on anything else. Weightlessness is not zero gravity; it's zero contact force during free fall.

Why this matters

Satellites stay up with zero fuel by falling around Earth; GPS needs to know where those orbits are to the metre; ocean tides are the Moon's pull stretching the oceans. Even the "wrong planet" joke is physics: mission designers budget rocket fuel by weight, which changes with gravity.

MODULE 03

Energy & Work — What Powers Change

The thing kids have after gummy bears, measured in Joules — and what it actually does.

Energy is a number — a property of a thing, with no direction — measured in Joules. It comes in two headline flavours. Kinetic energy is energy of movement: a car rolling, an arrow flying, air molecules jiggling. Potential energy is stored energy waiting for a circumstance: a phone held above the floor has gravitational potential energy because of its height. Let go, and potential converts into kinetic as the phone falls — then into a cracked screen when the ground gets involved.

Now the careful definition. Work is a force applied over a distance. Lift an apple one metre and you've done about one Joule of work — your body's chemical energy was converted into gravitational potential energy stored in the apple. Here's the twist: work and energy share the same unit, but they are not the same thing. Energy is the total amount of work a thing could do. Work is what actually happened — force times distance — and it usually means energy changed form. This is why your feelings are invalid in physics: if you strain against a weight that doesn't move, mathematically you did zero work. Force, yes. Distance, no. Work, none.

The master rule is conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted. When a car brakes, friction between brake and wheel slows the car and produces heat; the heat warms the air; the air's molecules move faster; and faster-moving molecules have kinetic energy. The car's kinetic energy didn't vanish — it moved into the air. This single habit — always ask where the energy went — teaches you more physics than any formula.

✕ What people get wrong

"Energy gets used up." It doesn't — it converts. Burn gasoline and the chemical energy becomes motion plus heat. The heat is still energy; it's just spread out into the air where it can't push your car anymore.

That's also why "recharging" your body isn't creating energy — you're converting food's chemical potential energy into muscle motion, heat, and thinking. Every joule was already somewhere else first.

Why this matters

Every battery, engine, and meal is an energy-conversion machine. Efficiency isn't about making energy — it's about avoiding the heat "fee." Electric cars, solar panels, and your own metabolism are all scored on how little energy leaks out as waste heat.

MODULE 04

Entropy & Thermodynamics — The One-Way Street

Why ice melts, why coffee cools, and why the universe only goes one direction in time.

Last module ended with heat leaking out of your brakes. That leak deserves a theory of its own, and it starts with a definition: temperature is just the average kinetic energy of the atoms in a system. All atoms wiggle; the faster they wiggle, the hotter the thing. A hot coffee is coffee whose molecules are doing a lot of jiggling.

Now the star of the show: entropy. Loosely, it's how much disorder a system has — more precisely, it counts the number of possible states the system could be in. Take an ice cube in the sun. Ice is a neat, ordered crystal; the sun's heat breaks it into liquid water, whose molecules slosh around in a huge number of arrangements. The water could look like this, or this, or this; the ice pretty much always looks like ice. So the system went from low entropy (few arrangements) to high entropy (many arrangements). And here's the kicker: that direction is not optional. Systems left alone drift toward more possible states — the whole universe is on an unstoppable path to higher entropy. Physicists believe this one-way drift is why time itself seems to only move forward.

Practically, entropy is about the quality of energy. Burn gasoline and your car moves, spitting out heat and exhaust. That exhaust is still the same atoms, just in a high-entropy form — and it will not spontaneously recombine into liquid fuel. Low-entropy energy is useful for doing work; high-entropy energy is spread thin. Your fridge seems to beat the rule by freezing water, but the fridge is not an isolated system — it heats the room more than it cools the water, so total entropy still rises. Jumping in lava remains a bad idea.

✕ What people get wrong

"Entropy means things always get messier, so organized life shouldn't exist." Entropy isn't a moral judgement on tidiness — it's about the number of possible states. Life, fridges, and cities are local islands of order that run on a bigger current of disorder around them.

Every ordered thing — a growing plant, a cooled drink, a built house — pays for its order by dumping even more disorder (heat) into its surroundings. The total ledger still moves one way.

Why this matters

Entropy decides what's possible: why engines need fuel, why recycling can never be perfect, why the future and past feel different. If you understand entropy, you understand why "energy crisis" is really a "low-entropy energy" crisis.

MODULE 05

Electromagnetism — One Force, Many Masks

Charge, current, magnets, induction — and the light that reaches your eye.

Objects carry a property called charge, which comes in positive and negative flavours. Equal amounts of both and the object is neutral. Electrons carry a single negative charge, and when electrons flow, we call it electric current. To describe that flow we use three knobs: current (how many electrons pass per second), voltage (what pushes them — a difference in electric potential, best imagined as a slope running downhill from high to low potential), and resistance (how hard the path fights back).

Then the pattern that should make you suspicious: Coulomb's law, describing how charges attract and repel, is Newton's law of gravitation in disguise — same shape, same inverse-square behaviour. Opposites attract; like charges recoil from each other. The four Maxwell equations then bundle the whole of electromagnetism. The first says a charge creates an electric field around it. The second says the same for magnetic fields — with one cruel twist: magnetic poles always come in north–south pairs and can never be separated into a lone pole. The third and fourth are the freaky part: a moving magnet creates an electric field, and a moving charge creates a magnetic field.

That mutual creation is induction — and it's why your phone charges on a pad with no cable: a changing magnetic field nudges electrons in the phone's coil, and current appears as if from nowhere. Chase the logic further: an accelerating charge shakes its electric field, which stirs a magnetic field, which stirs the electric field again, and the pair radiates outward as an electromagnetic wave. Depending on frequency, that wave is radio, Bluetooth, or — at just the right frequencies — light. Electric and magnetic fields aren't two forces; they're two faces of one bigger thing.

✕ What people get wrong

"The bulb consumes the current." Electrons aren't fuel — they're the delivery trucks. The bulb converts the energy the current carries into light and heat; the electrons keep flowing, same as ever.

That's why a wire doesn't run out of electrons, and why generators can keep a circuit running for decades. What gets "used up" is the energy carried by the moving charges — converted to light, heat, and motion.

Why this matters

Electromagnetism runs your life: the power grid, every wireless link, every screen, every motor and generator. And the same equations describe light itself — so understanding this module is understanding why you can see at all.

MODULE 06

The Atom & Radioactivity — Inside the Core

What everything is made of, why some atoms fall apart, and why you shouldn't play with them.

Zoom into any molecule and you find atoms: a tiny core surrounded by electrons. The core holds protons and neutrons, and both of those are themselves made of smaller particles called quarks — strange yet charming. Together this menagerie (plus the electron's heavier cousins) makes up the Standard Model, our best inventory of the smallest things in the universe, and our standing excuse for not knowing what quarks are made of.

The counting rules are beautifully simple. The number of protons decides the element: one proton is hydrogen, six is carbon, ninety-two is uranium. The number of neutrons decides the isotope: same element, different heft. And here's where it gets interesting — many isotopes are a little overweight and very unstable. They fall apart on their own, decaying into smaller atoms and releasing ionizing radiation in the process. Not a fun fact: that stuff can kill you. Do not play with radioactive atoms.

But decay isn't chaos — it's statistics. You can't predict when one atom will decay, but give yourself a large group and physics becomes a bookie: after one half-life, half of them will have fallen apart. After another, half of the remainder. Some isotopes barely want to live (half-life: fractions of a second), some really don't want to live (minutes to years), and some will outlive you by billions of years. Same rule, wildly different clocks.

✕ What people get wrong

"After one half-life, the stuff is gone." No — half of what remained decays each half-life. After two, a quarter remains; after three, an eighth. That's why nuclear waste stays dangerous for a very long time.

It's also why "radiation glows green" is a movie invention: ionizing radiation is invisible and silent. You can't see, smell, or hear it — which is precisely why it's dangerous without proper shielding.

Why this matters

Radioactivity runs nuclear power, medical imaging and cancer therapy, and carbon dating — and it's the reason nuclear waste demands careful handling. Knowing what an isotope is tells you whether it's a medicine, a fuel, or a hazard.

MODULE 07

Light & Relativity — When Time Bends

The fastest thing in the universe, the packets it comes in, and what that does to time and space.

Light is the fastest thing in the universe: exactly 299,792,458 metres per second in a vacuum. And "light is a wave," everyone said — because shoot it through two tiny slits and you get a striped interference pattern, which is a wave thing. Where two waves cross they can add up (bright band) or cancel out (dark gap). Then Einstein came along, heard about the photoelectric effect, and announced that light also arrives in tiny packets called photons. Wave and particle at once — a clue that physics was about to get strange.

Then he dropped the bomb: special relativity. He assumed the speed of light is constant because it arises from two other constants, and that the laws of physics are the same for everyone, moving or at rest. Now think: if you stand still with a flashlight and a friend passes on a moving train with a flashlight, shouldn't you see their light as faster than yours? The reality: no — you both measure the same speed. The only way that works is if time itself passes differently for the two of you. If light speed is constant, time must be relative.

And gravity? Sorry Newton — it's not a force. Einstein pictured the universe as a mesh of space and time, and anything with mass bends this fabric. Objects always move straight through space; gravitation is just what happens when the "straight lines" they follow are curved by the mesh — lines that look straight to them. If two people walk due north, one from the east coast and one from the west, they meet at the North Pole: straight paths on a curved surface converge. Oh, and energy and mass are the same stuff — E = mc² — which is why tiny atoms can release enormous energy.

✕ What people get wrong

"Time dilation is a sci-fi trick, not real." It's measured constantly — in GPS satellites, in particle accelerators, in the muons that reach the ground. The effect is tiny at human speeds and enormous near light speed, but it's as real as gravity.

Note it's symmetrical from each observer's view — each sees the other's clock run slow. There's no "true" clock in the universe; relativity means precisely that. Only acceleration and gravity break the symmetry.

Why this matters

Relativity isn't exotic — it's in your phone's GPS, it predicts black holes and gravitational waves, and E = mc² explains why stars shine and why nuclear energy is so concentrated. It's also the cleanest proof that physics describes one coherent universe, not a pile of separate rules.

MODULE 08

Fission & Fusion — Energy From the Nucleus

Two ways to make atoms lighter, one receipt: E = mc².

Module 6 left us with unstable nuclei decaying on their own. This module is about making that happen deliberately — and the two ways to squeeze energy out of a nucleus. Fission splits a big nucleus into smaller ones, usually by blasting it with neutrons. Fusion does the opposite: it slams two small nuclei together until they merge into one bigger one. Same goal, opposite directions.

Where does the energy come from? Something physicists call the mass defect. Weigh the pieces after the reaction and the resulting nucleus is lighter than the nuclei you started with. Mass doesn't just vanish — per Einstein's E = mc², the "missing" mass is converted into energy. Even a fraction of a gram, multiplied by c² (a staggeringly large number), produces a humongous amount of energy. That's the arithmetic behind both the Sun's furnace and the most destructive weapons ever built.

Fission is what Oppenheimer used to make that thing go boom — and what today's nuclear reactors use to boil water and spin turbines. Fusion is the Sun's engine: hydrogen nuclei crushed together into helium, releasing the light and heat that power every living thing. Fission and fusion are cool — but you have to be careful. Get the chain reaction wrong and you might blow up the planet. That totally didn't almost happen before… multiple times.

✕ What people get wrong

"Fission and fusion are basically the same." They're opposites. Fission splits heavy nuclei (uranium) into lighter ones; fusion merges light nuclei (hydrogen) into heavier ones. Both release energy — but through different nuclear arithmetic.

Also common: "mass is destroyed." Nothing is destroyed — mass is converted into energy. The books balance exactly if you use E = mc² as the exchange rate. Conservation holds; the currency just changes.

Why this matters

Fusion powers the Sun and every star; fission powers reactors and shaped the 20th century. Fusion research (like ITER) is the quest for a near-limitless, clean energy source — the same physics that's been running overhead your whole life.

MODULE 09

Quantum Mechanics — The World Probability Built

Where particles are waves, clouds decide where things are, and looking changes the answer.

When Einstein said light comes in packets, he accidentally opened a new field he hoped was a hoax. Another German, Max Planck, doubled down: all energy comes in tiny packets — quanta — and he became the father of quantum mechanics. Then the weirdness began.

Ask where an electron is inside an atom and the honest answer is: here! And there! And everywhere, at the same time. That's superposition — a particle in multiple states at once, at least until you measure it. Schrödinger's equation turns that into a probabilistic picture: a probability cloud around the nucleus, denser where the electron is more likely to be found. But where it actually lands when you measure is genuinely random.

Particles are also touchy about privacy. Try to pin down where a flying ball is and you lose track of how fast it's moving; measure its speed and its position blurs. That's Heisenberg's uncertainty principle: you can never know both the exact position and the exact speed of a quantum particle at the same time — not because your instruments are weak, but because the information isn't there to be had. And remember the double-slit? Send photons one at a time and the interference pattern still builds — meaning each photon acted as a wave and went through both slits at once. The instant you measure which slit it uses, the wave behaviour collapses and the pattern disappears. Small things can be particle and wave at once — and when we look at them, weird stuff happens.

  • Every chip in your pocket. Transistors — billions of them in your phone — work because quantum mechanics governs how electrons tunnel and switch inside semiconductors. Quantum physics is the hardware of the modern world.obvious
  • MRI and lasers. MRI scanners read the quantum spin of nuclei in your body; lasers exist only because atoms emit photons in quantized jumps. Medical imaging and fibre optics are quantum machines wearing lab coats.counter-intuitive
  • A single photon, two slits. Fire photons one at a time and the interference bands still form — each photon went through both slits as a wave. Measure which slit, and the pattern dies. The observer changes the outcome.edge case
Analogy

A spinning coin. While it spins, it's neither heads nor tails — it's a blur of both, a superposition. The moment you catch it (measurement), it commits to one face. But unlike a coin, an electron's "heads or tails" is genuinely random — no hidden trick decides it.

The coin analogy has a limit worth knowing: the coin secretly had a definite state all along — you just couldn't see it. Quantum particles don't. The superposition is real until the measurement, and the randomness is built into nature, not into your instruments. That's the part Einstein refused to believe: "God does not play dice."

✕ What people get wrong

"Quantum means anything is possible / it's all magic." Superposition is specific and fragile: it applies to well-defined states, and it collapses the moment the system interacts with its surroundings (decoherence). You are not simultaneously in the kitchen and the garden.

And the uncertainty principle isn't "our tools aren't precise enough." It's a hard limit in nature: position and momentum are not both defined for a quantum particle, period. No instrument, however perfect, can beat it.

Why this matters

Quantum mechanics is the most precisely tested theory in history — and it's inside everything: transistors, lasers, MRI, solar cells, GPS. Quantum computers aim to exploit superposition itself. Understanding it isn't about predicting cat jokes; it's about knowing how your phone works.

Questions people actually ask

Why do I weigh less on the Moon but my mass is the same?

Mass is how much stuff you are — fixed. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass. The Moon's gravity is weaker, so the force (your weight) is smaller. A bathroom scale measures force, not stuff — so it lies about your mass depending on which planet you stand on.

If energy is never destroyed, why do we have an energy crisis?

Because it's an entropy problem, not a quantity problem. Burning fuel converts low-entropy, concentrated energy into high-entropy heat and exhaust spread through the air. The energy is still there — it's just no longer useful for doing work. Crisis = running out of useful energy, not energy.

How can a phone charge with no cable?

Induction. The pad runs a changing current through a coil, creating a changing magnetic field. That changing field pushes electrons in a matching coil inside the phone — current appears without wires. A moving magnet creates an electric field; that's Maxwell's third equation doing your charging.

If the train rider's time runs slow, whose clock is "right"?

Neither — that's the point of relativity. Each observer measures the other's clock as slow, and there's no privileged frame to appeal to. The symmetry only breaks when someone accelerates or experiences gravity: that's why GPS satellites (weaker gravity, fast motion) genuinely tick at a different rate than your wristwatch.

Can a photon really go through two slits at once?

Yes — that's superposition. Until it's measured, the photon's state includes both paths at once, and the two possibilities interfere to build the striped pattern. The instant you measure which slit it used, the superposition collapses, the interference vanishes, and it acts like a single particle again.
