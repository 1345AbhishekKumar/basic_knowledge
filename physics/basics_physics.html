The Architecture of Physics

From the swing of a pendulum to the curve of spacetime, physics is the grammar the universe uses to speak. This lesson walks through six foundations — motion, force, energy, rotation, waves, and heat — and shows how each one quietly shapes the world you move through.

What You'll Learn

You'll build a working mental model of the principles that govern everyday motion and natural phenomena — not by memorising formulas, but by understanding the relationships between them. Each module pairs a plain-English explanation with diagrams, analogies, and short exercises that test the idea from more than one angle.

  • How physicists describe motion using position, speed, velocity, and acceleration
  • Why forces change motion, and how Newton's three laws tie everything together
  • How momentum, work, and energy flow through collisions and machines
  • The geometry of rotation — torque, angular momentum, and centripetal force
  • How oscillations and waves carry energy through space and matter
  • Why heat, temperature, and entropy set the direction of every natural process

Module One

The Language of Motion

Before physics can explain anything, it has to describe it. Motion is the change of position over time, and to track it we need a small vocabulary: position, distance, speed, velocity, and acceleration. Each word answers a slightly different question, and confusing them is the most common source of error in early physics.

Position is always measured relative to something — a reference point, a coordinate system, an origin. A train at platform 3 is stationary to a passenger sipping coffee inside, but moving at 120 km/h to someone watching from the platform. Neither is wrong; they're using different frames of reference. Distance is the total path length travelled, regardless of direction. Displacement is the straight-line change from start to finish — a vector, meaning direction matters.

Speed tells you how fast. Velocity tells you how fast and in which direction. Acceleration is any change in velocity — speeding up, slowing down, or turning. A car cornering at constant speed is still accelerating, because its direction is changing. This single insight unlocks most of classical mechanics.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Choose a reference frame — an origin and coordinate axes that make the problem simple.
  2. Record the object's initial position. Mark it on the axis.
  3. Measure distance as the total path travelled; measure displacement as start-to-finish straight line.
  4. Compute speed as distance ÷ time. Compute velocity as displacement ÷ time (with direction).
  5. Detect acceleration by checking whether velocity changed — in magnitude or direction.

Real-World Examples

  • Train passing a station. To a passenger reading a book, the train is still. To someone on the platform, it's a blur. Same event, two valid frames of reference.
  • Walker around a city block. Walks 400 metres total but ends where she began. Distance: 400 m. Displacement: zero.
  • Earth in orbit. Moves at roughly 107,000 km/h around the Sun. We don't feel it because everything around us shares the same motion — the same reason you can pour coffee on a smooth flight.

The Concept

Position is a coordinate. Velocity is position's rate of change. Acceleration is velocity's rate of change — each one is the derivative of the one before.

The Analogy

Position is a bookmark — it tells you where you are. Velocity is the bookmark plus which way you're turning pages. Acceleration is when you start flipping faster, slower, or backwards.

Each quantity is the rate of change of the previous one — like pages turning, then turning faster.

What People Get Wrong

"Acceleration" is often used to mean only "speeding up." In physics it means any change in velocity — including slowing down, or turning at constant speed. A satellite in circular orbit is constantly accelerating, even though its speed never changes, because its direction is always shifting.

Every motion you'll ever study — a falling apple, a braking car, a spacecraft slingshot — begins with these four quantities. Get them straight, and the rest of physics becomes a conversation rather than a foreign language.

🔗 This connects to Module 2 because forces are exactly what cause acceleration. Once you can describe motion, the next question is: what changes it?

Because the universe is geometric. Two cars moving at 60 km/h toward each other will collide; two moving at 60 km/h in the same direction will never meet. The number alone doesn't tell you what happens — the direction does. Vectors exist because reality is directional.
Module Two

Forces and Newton's Laws

A force is a push or a pull — an interaction that can change an object's motion. It can start something moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down, or bend its path. Without forces, the world would be static: no rivers, no wind, no heartbeat. With them, everything moves, interacts, and responds.

Isaac Newton distilled three centuries of observation into three laws that still hold for nearly every everyday situation. The first law says objects resist changes to their motion — a property called inertia. The second law quantifies it: force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma). The third law says every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Together, they convert the kinematics of Module 1 into a story about causes.

Forces come in two broad families. Contact forces require physical interaction — friction, tension, normal force. Field forces act at a distance — gravity, magnetism, electricity. Whether balanced or unbalanced determines whether motion changes. When forces cancel, the object moves at constant velocity (or stays still). When they don't, it accelerates.

Newton's Three Laws

  1. First Law (Inertia): An object at rest stays at rest; an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity — unless an external force acts on it.
  2. Second Law: The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass. F = ma.
  3. Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Forces always come in pairs.

Real-World Examples

  • Book on a table. Gravity pulls down; the table pushes up with an equal normal force. Balanced — the book stays put.
  • Rocket launch. The engine expels gas downward at high speed; the gas pushes back up on the rocket with equal force. That upward push is thrust.
  • Car braking. Friction between tires and road provides the decelerating force. On ice, friction drops and the same brake pedal does almost nothing.

The Concept

Force = mass × acceleration. More mass means more force needed for the same acceleration. More force means more acceleration for the same mass.

The Analogy

Force is a hand pushing a shopping cart. Push harder and it speeds up faster. Load the cart with bowling balls and the same push barely moves it.

Mass is the cart's load. Force is your push. Acceleration is what you get. Same push, different loads, different results.

What People Get Wrong

"If there's no force, things stop." Actually, without force, things keep moving at constant velocity forever — that's inertia. Friction is what stops them on Earth. In deep space, a thrown wrench would travel in a straight line at constant speed until it hit something. The reason things "naturally" come to rest on Earth is that friction is everywhere, quietly applying opposing forces.

Newton's three laws are the bedrock of classical mechanics. Every bridge, vehicle, and satellite is designed using them. They tell you not just how things move, but why.

🔗 This connects to Module 3 because a force applied over a distance does work, and work transfers energy. Force is the bridge between motion and energy.

Mini-Challenge — The Subway Pole

You're standing in a subway car, holding nothing. The train suddenly brakes. You lurch forward. Why — and which of Newton's laws explains it?

  • First law: the train pushed you forward.
  • First law: your body kept moving at constant velocity while the train slowed around you.
  • Third law: the floor exerted an equal and opposite reaction on your feet.
  • Second law: a forward force acted on your body.
Answer: B. Your body was moving at the train's speed. When the train braked, friction between your feet and the floor wasn't enough to immediately slow you to match. By the first law, your body continued at its original velocity — forward — until something (a pole, a stranger, the door) applied a force to stop you.

A car drives 10 km north, then 10 km south, ending where it started. What is its displacement?

  • 20 km
  • 10 km north
  • 0 km
  • 20 km south
Displacement is start-to-finish straight-line change. Since the car returned to its starting point, displacement is zero — though the distance travelled is 20 km.

A satellite orbits Earth at constant speed in a circular path. Is it accelerating?

  • No — its speed is constant.
  • Yes — its direction is constantly changing, which means velocity is changing.
  • Only when it crosses the equator.
  • Only if its orbit is elliptical.
Acceleration is any change in velocity — including direction. A satellite in circular orbit is continuously accelerating toward Earth, even though its speed never changes.

Which of these is the cleanest statement of Newton's third law?

  • If A pushes B, B pushes A with equal force in the opposite direction.
  • Every force eventually runs out.
  • Heavier objects fall faster.
  • An object in motion stays in motion.
The third law is about force pairs. Whenever one object exerts a force on another, the second exerts an equal and opposite force back. The other options describe the first law or common misconceptions.
Module Three

Momentum, Energy & Work

Some quantities in physics describe state — where something is, how fast it's going. Others describe transfer — what moves from one object to another when they interact. Momentum, work, and energy belong to the second group. They are the universe's accounting system: they tell you what can happen, what must be conserved, and what gets shuffled around when things collide.

Momentum is mass times velocity — "motion with impact." A slow truck can have more momentum than a fast bicycle because mass dominates. Impulse is force applied over time; it's how momentum changes. This is why airbags work: they extend the time of collision, reducing the force for the same momentum change.

Work is force applied over a distance. If you push against a wall and it doesn't move, no work is done — no matter how tired you feel. Energy is the capacity to do work. It comes in two main flavours: kinetic (energy of motion) and potential (stored energy of position or configuration). The total amount in a closed system never changes — it only transforms. This is the law of conservation of energy, perhaps the most important principle in all of physics.

The Core Relationships

  1. Momentum (p = mv): mass × velocity. Vector — direction matters. Conserved in collisions.
  2. Impulse (J = F·t): force × time. Equals the change in momentum. Same impulse, longer time → smaller force.
  3. Work (W = F·d): force × distance, in the direction of motion. Measured in joules.
  4. Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²): energy of motion. Scalar — direction irrelevant. Speed matters quadratically.
  5. Potential energy: stored by position. Gravitational (mgh), elastic (½kx²), chemical (in bonds).
  6. Power (P = W/t): how quickly work is done. Measured in watts (joules per second).

Real-World Examples

  • Airbags. Same momentum change, longer collision time. By impulse (J = F·t), extending t reduces F — that's what saves your face.
  • Hydroelectric dam. Water at height has gravitational PE. Falling converts it to KE, which spins a turbine, which converts it to electrical energy.
  • Billiard ball collision. One ball stops, the other moves off at the same speed. Momentum is transferred almost perfectly; little energy is lost.

The Concept

Energy can't be created or destroyed, only transformed. PE ↔ KE ↔ heat ↔ sound — the total stays the same in a closed system.

The Analogy

Energy is like money in an economy. It changes hands (work) and forms (cash, bank balance, goods), but the total amount in circulation stays the same — unless you trace it outside the system.

Same currency, different forms. The total never changes — it just moves around.

What People Get Wrong

People confuse momentum and kinetic energy. Momentum is a vector (direction matters); KE is a scalar (direction irrelevant). Two identical cars moving at 50 km/h in opposite directions have equal and opposite momentum — their total momentum is zero — but their total kinetic energy is not zero. That's why head-on collisions are so destructive: all that KE has to go somewhere, and it goes into crumpling metal.

Conservation laws are nature's balance sheet. They tell you what's possible without needing to know the details. If energy and momentum must be conserved, you can predict the outcome of a collision before you understand the forces involved.

🔗 This connects to Module 4 because rotating objects carry angular momentum — and it, too, is conserved. The same accounting principle, applied to spin instead of straight-line motion.

Key Terms — Flip to Reveal

Momentum
Mass × velocity. A vector — direction matters. Conserved in closed systems.
Impulse
Force × time. Equals change in momentum. Longer time, smaller force — the airbag principle.
Work
Force × distance (in the direction of motion). No movement, no work — even if you're exhausted.
Kinetic Energy
½mv². Energy of motion. Speed matters quadratically — double the speed, quadruple the energy.
Power
Work ÷ time. How quickly energy is transferred. Measured in watts.
Module Four

Gravity & Rotation

So far we've treated motion as a straight-line affair. But the universe spins. Planets rotate, wheels turn, atoms tumble. Rotation follows rules that mirror linear motion — but with a twist. Position becomes angular displacement, velocity becomes angular velocity, force becomes torque, and mass becomes moment of inertia.

Gravity is the silent partner. Every mass attracts every other mass; the attraction grows with mass and falls off with the square of distance. Newton described it as a force. Einstein, two centuries later, recast it as the curvature of spacetime itself — a heavy ball denting a rubber sheet, with smaller objects rolling along the curves. Either way, gravity is what keeps the Moon in orbit, the Earth round, and your coffee in the cup.

Two ideas tie rotation and gravity together. Torque is the rotational equivalent of force — it depends on how hard you push, how far from the pivot you push, and the angle. Angular momentum is the rotational equivalent of linear momentum, and it is conserved. A figure skater pulling in their arms spins faster because their mass redistributes closer to the axis — to keep angular momentum constant, rotational speed must rise.

The Rotational Vocabulary

  1. Torque (τ = F·r): force × lever arm. Longer wrench, less effort. Same principle as a seesaw.
  2. Moment of inertia (I): rotational mass. Depends on how mass is distributed — closer to the axis means smaller I, easier to spin.
  3. Angular momentum (L = Iω): I × angular velocity. Conserved unless an external torque acts.
  4. Centripetal force: the inward force that keeps an object on a circular path. Without it, the object flies off in a straight line — that's inertia, not a mysterious outward push.
  5. Center of mass: the average position of an object's mass. The point where, if you support it, the object balances.

Real-World Examples

  • Figure skater's spin. Arms outstretched: large I, slow spin. Arms pulled in: smaller I, faster spin. Angular momentum is conserved throughout.
  • Wrench with a long handle. More lever arm means more torque for the same force. That's why stubborn bolts need a longer wrench, not a stronger arm.
  • Satellite in orbit. Constantly falling toward Earth, but moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it. Astronauts inside feel weightless not because there's no gravity, but because they're in free fall along with their spacecraft.

The Concept

Torque depends on where you push, not just how hard. The same force at twice the distance produces twice the torque.

The Analogy

Opening a door: push near the hinge and it barely moves. Push at the handle and it swings easily. Same force, different lever arm, very different result.

Lever arm matters as much as force. That's the whole secret of simple machines.

What People Get Wrong

"Centrifugal force" pushes you outward on a curve — or so it feels. In reality, your inertia wants to keep you going straight. The car (or the seat, or the seatbelt) pushes you inward — that's the centripetal force curving your path. The "outward push" you feel is just your body's resistance to being redirected. There is no centrifugal force in an inertial frame; it's a fictitious force that appears only in rotating frames of reference.

Rotation is everywhere — wheels, turbines, planets, atoms. Without angular momentum conservation, galaxies wouldn't hold their shapes and bicycles wouldn't stay upright. Mastering rotation unlocks everything from engine design to spaceflight.

Retrieval Practice — Modules 3 & 4

Three more questions, reaching back across the last two modules.

A truck and a bicycle are moving at the same speed. Which has more momentum?

  • The bicycle — it's more agile.
  • They have the same momentum.
  • The truck — mass dominates the equation p = mv.
  • Neither — momentum depends only on speed.
Momentum is mass × velocity. At equal velocity, the object with greater mass carries more momentum. That's why a slow truck can do far more damage than a fast bicycle.

You push a wall as hard as you can for a minute. It doesn't move. How much work have you done on the wall?

  • A lot — your arms are tired.
  • Zero — no displacement means no work, regardless of effort.
  • Equal to your force × 60 seconds.
  • Equal to your body weight × distance.
Work requires displacement. Without movement in the direction of the force, no work is done on the object — even though your muscles are burning energy internally. Effort ≠ work in the physical sense.

Why does a figure skater spin faster when pulling in their arms?

  • Angular momentum is conserved — reducing moment of inertia increases angular velocity.
  • They are doing work against friction.
  • Air resistance decreases when arms are tucked.
  • Gravity pulls harder on a tighter body.
Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved. Pulling arms in reduces I (mass closer to axis). To keep L constant, ω must increase. No external torque acts, so the principle holds exactly.
Module Five

Oscillations & Waves

An oscillation is any motion that repeats around a central position — a swing, a plucked string, a heartbeat. It happens whenever a system is displaced from equilibrium and a restoring force pulls it back. But instead of stopping at equilibrium, the system overshoots, and the cycle repeats. This back-and-forth is one of nature's most fundamental patterns.

The simplest case is simple harmonic motion (SHM), where the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement. A spring follows this rule: pull it twice as far, and it pulls back twice as hard. A pendulum does too, for small angles. SHM gives us two useful quantities: the period (time for one full cycle) and the frequency (cycles per second, measured in hertz).

When oscillations travel — when the disturbance moves through a medium without carrying matter with it — we get a wave. Waves are characterised by wavelength (distance between peaks), amplitude (maximum displacement, tied to energy), and frequency. Their speed is wavelength × frequency. Sound, light, water ripples, and earthquake tremors are all waves, differing only in medium and scale.

The Anatomy of a Wave

  1. Wavelength (λ): distance between two consecutive crests. Long wavelengths bend around obstacles; short ones cast sharp shadows.
  2. Frequency (f): cycles per second, in hertz. Determines pitch in sound and colour in light.
  3. Amplitude: maximum displacement from rest. Determines loudness in sound and brightness in light.
  4. Period (T): time for one cycle. T = 1/f. High frequency means short period.
  5. Wave speed (v = fλ): how fast the disturbance propagates. Sound in air: ~343 m/s. Light in vacuum: ~300,000 km/s.
  6. Resonance: when an external force matches a system's natural frequency, amplitude grows dramatically. Useful in music, destructive in bridges.

Real-World Examples

  • Guitar string. Plucked, it vibrates at its natural frequency. The soundboard resonates in sympathy, amplifying the sound into the room.
  • Pendulum clock. The period depends only on length and gravity — not on amplitude (for small swings). That's why pendulum clocks keep steady time.
  • Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940). Wind matched the bridge's natural frequency. Oscillations grew until the structure tore itself apart. Resonance, gone wrong.

The Concept

A wave transports energy without transporting matter. Particles oscillate in place; the disturbance travels.

The Analogy

A stadium wave: each person stands and sits in place. No one travels around the stadium — but the wave of standing-people does.

The wave moves; the medium stays. Energy travels; matter oscillates.

What People Get Wrong

Sound in space. Movies show explosions with booming sound effects — but in vacuum, there's nothing to carry the wave. Sound is a mechanical wave; it needs a medium. Light, an electromagnetic wave, doesn't — which is why we see distant stars but would never hear them, no matter how violent their deaths.

Waves carry energy and information across vast distances — through air, water, solid rock, and empty space. Understanding them is understanding how the universe communicates: from a whisper to a radio signal to the light of a distant galaxy.

Module Six

Heat & Thermodynamics

Heat is energy on the move — flowing from hot to cold, always, never the other way around. Temperature is its close cousin: a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance. A bathtub of warm water holds more heat than a spark of flame, even though the spark is far hotter, because there are vastly more molecules jiggling in the tub.

Heat moves in three ways. Conduction happens through direct contact — fast-moving particles in a hot pan bump into slower ones in your hand. Convection happens in fluids: warm regions rise, cool regions sink, currents form. Radiation needs no medium at all — infrared waves carry energy across empty space, which is how the Sun warms the Earth.

Four laws govern this domain. The zeroth defines temperature through thermal equilibrium. The first says energy is conserved — heat plus work equals the change in internal energy. The second says entropy, the measure of disorder, always increases in an isolated system: heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the reverse. The third says that as temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches its minimum. Together, these laws set the arrow of time — the reason yesterday's coffee doesn't spontaneously reheat.

The Four Laws of Thermodynamics

  1. Zeroth Law: If A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B with C, then A is in equilibrium with C. This is what makes temperature a meaningful, measurable quantity.
  2. First Law: Energy is conserved. Heat added to a system either raises its internal energy or does work. You can't get something for nothing.
  3. Second Law: The total entropy of an isolated system always increases. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold. No machine is 100% efficient.
  4. Third Law: As temperature approaches absolute zero (0 K, −273.15 °C), the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero. Absolute zero itself is unreachable.

Real-World Examples

  • Coffee cooling. Heat flows from coffee to air, never back. The total energy is conserved, but its usefulness drops — that's entropy at work.
  • Refrigerator. Forces heat to flow from cold (inside) to hot (outside) by doing electrical work. It's fighting the second law, which is why it needs a power source.
  • Hydraulic lift. Pressure applied to a small piston transmits through fluid to a large piston, multiplying force. Pascal's principle in action.

The Concept

Temperature = average kinetic energy of particles. Heat = total energy transferred. Different quantities, often confused.

The Analogy

Temperature is the average speed of cars on a highway. Heat is the total fuel burned by all of them. A few fast cars (spark) vs many steady ones (bathtub) — different averages, different totals.

Same average speed (temperature), very different total fuel (heat). The spark is hotter; the bathtub has more heat.

What People Get Wrong

"Cold flows into a warm room when you open the window in winter." It doesn't. Heat flows out — from warm room to cold outdoors. Cold isn't a substance or an energy; it's the absence of heat. The room feels colder because its heat escaped, not because cold came in. The same misunderstanding shows up in talk of "keeping the cold out" — what you're really doing is keeping the heat in.

Thermodynamics sets the direction of time itself. Every natural process — melting, burning, ageing, dying — obeys the second law. It explains why machines can never be perfectly efficient, why the universe trends toward disorder, and why yesterday doesn't run in reverse.

Fifteen Questions, All Modules

Choose the best answer for each. Explanations appear after you select.

Question 01

A runner jogs 5 km east, then 5 km west, ending where she started. Which is true?

  • Distance is 0; displacement is 10 km.
  • Distance is 10 km; displacement is 0.
  • Distance is 10 km; displacement is 10 km west.
  • Both are zero.
Distance is the total path travelled — 10 km. Displacement is start-to-finish — zero, since she returned to her starting point.

Question 02

A car turns a corner at constant speed. Is it accelerating?

  • No — speed is constant.
  • Only if the road is banked.
  • Yes — direction is part of velocity, and velocity is changing.
  • Only if the tires screech.
Acceleration is any change in velocity, including direction. A car turning at constant speed is accelerating — the force comes from friction between tires and road.

Question 03

A book rests on a table. Which pair of forces is the Newton's third law action-reaction pair?

  • The book pulls Earth up; Earth pulls the book down.
  • Gravity pulls the book down; the table pushes the book up.
  • The table pushes the book up; the book pushes the table down.
  • Both (a) and (c) are valid third-law pairs.
Tricky one. Gravity and the normal force are equal and opposite, but they act on the same object (the book) — they're not a third-law pair. The third-law pair is the book pulling Earth up and Earth pulling the book down. (The book-on-table and table-on-book forces are also a valid pair, but on different objects.)

Question 04

Why does an airbag reduce injury in a collision?

  • It reduces the change in momentum.
  • It increases the impulse.
  • It lowers the mass of the passenger.
  • It extends the collision time, reducing force for the same momentum change.
Impulse J = F × t equals the change in momentum. The airbag doesn't change the momentum change, but it increases t, which decreases F. Same impulse, less force per moment.

Question 05

A moving object doubles its speed. What happens to its kinetic energy?

  • It doubles.
  • It quadruples.
  • It halves.
  • It stays the same.
KE = ½mv². The v is squared, so doubling speed multiplies KE by 4. This is why crash severity rises so steeply with speed — small increases in velocity mean large increases in energy.

Question 06

A figure skater pulls in their arms and spins faster. Which principle explains this?

  • Conservation of linear momentum.
  • Newton's first law.
  • Conservation of angular momentum.
  • Conservation of energy.
Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved. Pulling arms in reduces I (mass closer to axis), so ω must increase to keep L constant. No external torque acts.

Question 07

An astronaut in orbit feels weightless because...

  • They are in continuous free fall, falling at the same rate as their spacecraft.
  • There is no gravity in space.
  • They are beyond Earth's atmosphere.
  • Their mass becomes zero in orbit.
Gravity at orbital altitude is still strong — about 90% of surface gravity. Astronauts feel weightless because they and their spacecraft are falling together. The spacecraft moves sideways fast enough to keep missing Earth, but the falling is real.

Question 08

A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 4 m. What is its speed?

  • 12.5 m/s
  • 200 m/s
  • 54 m/s
  • 0.08 m/s
Wave speed v = f × λ = 50 × 4 = 200 m/s. Frequency and wavelength trade off inversely for a fixed wave speed in a given medium.

Question 09

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 because of what phenomenon?

  • Resonance — wind matched the bridge's natural frequency, amplifying oscillations.
  • The bridge was overloaded with traffic.
  • Material fatigue from extreme cold.
  • An earthquake triggered the failure.
Wind induced oscillations that matched the bridge's natural frequency. Resonance amplified the motion until the structure tore itself apart. It's now a textbook case for engineering students everywhere.

Question 10

Which statement correctly distinguishes heat from temperature?

  • Heat is measured in kelvin; temperature in joules.
  • They are the same thing expressed in different units.
  • Temperature is average kinetic energy per particle; heat is total energy transferred.
  • Heat depends on mass; temperature does not.
Temperature is intensive — it doesn't depend on amount. A spark at 2000 °C has high temperature but little heat because few particles are involved. A warm bath has more heat but lower temperature.

Question 11

The second law of thermodynamics is most directly responsible for which observation?

  • A pendulum swings forever in a vacuum.
  • A hot coffee cools down; it never spontaneously reheats.
  • Energy is conserved in a closed system.
  • Absolute zero can be reached with enough cooling.
The second law says entropy increases — heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the reverse without external work. Coffee cooling is its everyday signature. (Energy conservation is the first law; reaching absolute zero is forbidden by the third.)

Question 12

Which type of heat transfer can travel through a vacuum?

  • Conduction only.
  • Convection only.
  • Conduction and convection.
  • Radiation only.
Radiation is electromagnetic — it needs no medium. That's how the Sun's warmth reaches Earth across 150 million kilometres of empty space. Conduction and convection both require matter.

Question 13

If you double the lever arm (distance from pivot) without changing the force, what happens to torque?

  • It doubles.
  • It halves.
  • It quadruples.
  • It stays the same.
Torque τ = F × r. Torque is directly proportional to lever arm — double the distance, double the torque. That's why longer wrenches make stubborn bolts easier.

Question 14

A car drives in a perfect circle at constant speed. What provides the centripetal force?

  • The engine, pushing forward.
  • Friction between the tires and the road, directed inward.
  • A fictitious outward force called centrifugal force.
  • Gravity, pulling the car down.
Friction supplies the inward force that curves the car's path. On ice, friction vanishes and the car continues straight — that's inertia, not "centrifugal force," which is a fictitious force that appears only in rotating frames.

Question 15

Two objects collide and stick together. Which quantity is conserved (assuming no external force)?

  • Kinetic energy only.
  • Both momentum and kinetic energy.
  • Momentum only — this is an inelastic collision.
  • Neither; both are lost.
In an inelastic collision (objects stick together), momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not — some is converted to heat, sound, or deformation. Only in perfectly elastic collisions is KE also conserved.
Questions You Might Still Have

FAQ

Why is acceleration measured in m/s² and not just m/s?

Because acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes — and velocity is already measured in m/s. So acceleration is (m/s) per second, which is m/s². It tells you how much the speed changes every second.

If energy is conserved, why do we say it's "lost" to friction?

It's not lost — it's transformed. Friction converts kinetic energy into heat, which dissipates into the surroundings. The total energy of the universe is unchanged; it's just less useful for doing work. "Lost" really means "no longer in a form we can easily use."

What's the difference between mass and weight?

Mass is the amount of matter in an object — constant everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass — it changes with location. A 70 kg astronaut has the same mass on Earth and on the Moon, but weighs about one-sixth as much on the Moon because lunar gravity is weaker.

Why can't we reach absolute zero?

The third law of thermodynamics. As temperature approaches zero, removing each additional bit of heat requires more and more effort — reaching absolute zero exactly would require infinite work. We've gotten to billionths of a kelvin in labs, but never to zero itself.

Is centrifugal force real?

It depends on your frame of reference. In an inertial (non-rotating) frame, no — there's only inertia trying to keep you going straight, and centripetal force curving your path. In a rotating frame, centrifugal force appears as a mathematical convenience to make Newton's laws work. Physicists call it a "fictitious force" — useful for calculation, but not a real interaction.

How is sound different from light?

Sound is a mechanical wave — it needs a medium (air, water, solid) and travels by particle vibration. Light is an electromagnetic wave — it needs no medium and travels through vacuum at the speed of light. Sound in air moves at ~343 m/s; light moves at ~300,000,000 m/s. That's why you see lightning before you hear thunder.
