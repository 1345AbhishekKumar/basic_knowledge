The Problem-First Mindset

Before Instagram, before Anthropic, Mike Krieger sat in a Stanford program called Symbolic Systems — roughly forty students a year, a strange little degree that fused computer science with design, philosophy and psychology. The premise: don’t just study computing, study the entire human context around why we build software. Three takeaways from that program became the load-bearing walls of everything he shipped afterward.

First: everything you build should solve a problem for somebody — the spine of design thinking, from identifying the problem to validating the fix. Second: the discipline of prototyping — instead of disappearing for six months and unveiling something fundamentally wrong, build cheap versions early and show them along the way. Third: the compounding value of the right team — he met Kevin Systrom at Stanford years before they founded anything. Notice what is missing from the list: technology for its own sake. Decades later, when Artifact failed, Mike traced the failure to this exact principle — “users are not going to adopt a feature just because of the technology underneath.” The foundations never changed; only the waves did.

THE LOOP, STEP BY STEP
  1. Identify a problem for somebody — a person, not a technology.
  2. Research how they actually live with it today.
  3. Build the smallest prototype you can show.
  4. Show it early and often — every prototype is a question.
  5. Validate: did you solve it? If not, loop again.
WHY IT MATTERS

Every later module in this lesson is a stress-test of this one. Artifact failed because the problem wasn’t felt on day one; Instagram succeeded because real user behavior pointed at the photo core. Problem-first is not a phase you graduate from — it is the operating system.

VISUAL ANALOGY

An architect who hides the blueprints for a year, versus one who hands the client a cardboard model every Friday. The model is uglier. The feedback is infinitely better.

MODULE 02 · REV A · TIMING

Catching the Wave

Rewind your time machine to 2009. Phone cameras were bad. Facebook’s mobile app was a shrunken website. Almost nobody was building native social products. Most people looked at that landscape and saw a wasteland; Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger saw a wave that hadn’t crested yet. Their method for reading waves is surprisingly concrete. Signal one: early adopters are already doing something interesting in the space — awkwardly, on inferior tools, but voluntarily. Signal two: the rate of change of the underlying technology.

Mike points at the hardware ladder — iPhone 3G to 3GS to 4 — and notes you can literally watch photo quality and network capability jump generation over generation. If the curve keeps jumping one or two more times, the thing that looks like a toy becomes a platform. The builder’s move: ship something genuinely useful today while architecting it to catch the next leap. He ran the identical calculation on LLMs at Anthropic — Claude 3 was limited, Claude 3.5 was a big leap, and the rational bet is that the jumps continue. Timing isn’t mysticism. It’s observing early-adopter energy, measuring the jumps, and asking one question: if this improves one or two more times, will it be incredible?

WAVE-READING PROCEDURE
  1. Scan for early-adopter energy — voluntary, awkward, enthusiastic use.
  2. Measure the rate of change: spec jumps per generation.
  3. Ask: “one or two more jumps — incredible, or still a toy?”
  4. Build something genuinely useful today.
  5. Architect it to catch the next leap.
WHY IT MATTERS

Timing is the one variable you can’t fix with effort. Right product on an early wave means a decade of silence; right product on a ready wave means 100 million users in two years. The rate-of-change test turns “too early” from a verdict into a countdown.

VISUAL ANALOGY

A surfer watching sets: you don’t paddle for the wave that already broke, and you don’t paddle for ripples. You count the jumps and paddle early enough that the wave finds you already moving.

✗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“You have to be first to win.”

CLICK TO FLIP →
✓ FIELD REALITY

You have to be ready when the wave becomes ready. Facebook wasn’t the first social network and Instagram wasn’t the first photo app — they were the first at the right moment of the technology curve.

CLICK TO FLIP BACK
MEMORY TECHNIQUE

W.A.V.E. — Watch early adopters, Assess the jumps, Verify “one more leap,” Execute for today.

OBVIOUS

EDGE CASE

Artifact: ML improved exactly as predicted, but the experience layer — the ad-laden mobile web — never got ready. A technology wave doesn’t automatically carry an experience wave.

MODULE 03 · REV A · FOCUS

The Pivot as Subtraction

Burbn was a fine product — and that was the problem. The location check-in app, built with photos and videos attached, had users and had promise, but it wasn’t heading where the founders wanted to go. So Mike and Kevin did something most founders find almost physically painful: they paused, laid every feature on the table, and asked which piece was actually working. The answer was unambiguous — people were taking photos, sharing what they were doing, and connecting through it. Everything else was noise around the signal.

They stripped the distracting things away and rebuilt the whole identity around the core, and Burbn became Instagram. The pivot is usually retold as a story of failure, but Mike’s version is the opposite: it’s a story of subtraction applied to success. The photo feature was already winning inside a mediocre app; the craft was noticing it, and having the nerve to delete everything around it. One caution hides inside the story, though — subtraction only works when a core exists. Years later at Artifact, adding and stripping never revealed a day-one hook, and recognizing that difference is itself a skill.

SUBTRACTION PROTOCOL
  1. Pause and admit the direction doubt — a pause is not a defeat.
  2. Inventory everything the product does. Everything.
  3. Find the one thing users would genuinely miss.
  4. Cut the rest without sentiment.
  5. Rebuild the identity around the surviving core.
WHY IT MATTERS

Most products don’t die from a lack of features; they die from a lack of a reason. Subtraction is the cheapest R&D there is — the evidence is already sitting in your usage logs, waiting for someone brave enough to read it.

VISUAL ANALOGY

The sculptor: the statue is already in the stone. Burbn was a block of marble with Instagram inside it — the pivot wasn’t adding material, it was chipping away everything that wasn’t the photo.

✗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Pivoting means the original idea failed.”

CLICK TO FLIP →
✓ FIELD REALITY

A pivot is usually a success hiding inside a mediocre product. Burbn’s photo feature was working — the craft was noticing it and deleting everything around it.

CLICK TO FLIP BACK
MEMORY TECHNIQUE

C.U.T. — Catalog every feature, Uncover what’s loved, Trim the rest.

OBVIOUS

MODULE 04 · REV A · PEOPLE

The Team Is the Product

Ask Mike Krieger what survives every acquisition, every pivot, every company death, and the answer is the team — not the strategy, not the roadmap. His criteria are specific. First, talent paired with low ego, a combination he calls genuinely rare and difficult to find. Second, generalists over specialists in the early days: Instagram engineers would write the backend for a feature on Monday and its iOS or Android client by Friday; designers wrote code; product managers pushed pixels. Nobody was boxed into a silo, and the fluidity was the point.

Third, and hardest to fake: people who actually care about the product they’re building. Mike has watched companies full of hard workers who feel no attachment to the thing itself, and he’s blunt about the outcome — you will never get great products that way, because breakthroughs come from people close to the details, generating the next idea themselves instead of waiting for a strategy meeting. When thirteen-person Instagram was absorbed by thousand-person Facebook, the culture lesson ran the other direction: Facebook protected startup behavior at scale with company-wide hackathons every few months, prized experimentation, and moved fast on purpose. Grow the team and the ambition — but never grow out of the habits that keep you quick.

THE HIRING FILTER, IN ORDER
  1. Are they genuinely talented at the craft?
  2. Is the ego low enough to share credit and swap roles?
  3. Can they operate across the stack — generalist range?
  4. Do they care about this product, specifically?
WHY IT MATTERS

Strategy decks don’t ship features; people do. The speed of a team is the ceiling on the speed of the product, and care is the only non-fungible ingredient — you cannot buy it, schedule it, or OKR it into existence.

VISUAL ANALOGY

A jazz combo, not an orchestra: everyone hears everyone, and soloists swap instruments mid-song. Early Instagram was a combo — backend players soloing on iOS, designers comping on code.

✗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Great products come from great strategy documents.”

CLICK TO FLIP →
✓ FIELD REALITY

Breakthroughs come from people close to the details who generate the next idea themselves — not from waiting for a strategy meeting. Strategy follows a caring team, not the other way around.

CLICK TO FLIP BACK
MEMORY TECHNIQUE

The hiring equation: (talent − ego) × range + care. Subtract first — ego ruins the multiplication.

OBVIOUS

One person, whole feature

Instagram engineers routinely shipped a feature across backend, iOS and Android — no handoff tax, no telephone game between roles.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE

Hackathons at a billion users

Facebook, thousands of employees strong, still stopped the entire company for hackathons every few months. Structured “chaos” was the speed machine.

EDGE CASE

Hard work without attachment

Mike has seen companies where everyone works hard and nobody cares. Full effort, zero love — and the product is never great. Effort and care are different inputs.

MODULE 05 · REV A · THE EXPENSIVE LESSON

The Cold-Start Trap

Artifact was the smartest product Mike Krieger ever built, and it taught him the most expensive lesson of his career. The bet: combine cutting-edge machine learning with good design to make software that feels deeply personal. The algorithm worked — feed it enough articles and it would learn that you care about one specific Formula 1 driver, and Brazilian modernist architecture, but only when paired with Scandinavian design. The problem was everything before “enough.” Most new users read two articles, decided it felt like Apple News, and never came back. The product got extraordinary with tenure — and that very property killed its growth.

A second wound came from outside the product: the mobile web pages Artifact sent readers to were stuffed with ads and pop-up videos, so even a perfect recommendation ended in a bad experience they didn’t control. Add a two-year private beta that exhausted the team and delayed every lesson, and the energy in the system never recovered. Mike’s synthesis is one sentence: users do not adopt a product because of the intelligence underneath — they adopt it because it solves a problem they can feel in session one. He now runs the opposite playbook at Anthropic: design Claude to be useful to someone meeting an LLM for the first time, offer concrete things it can do today rather than in theory, and ask onboarding questions to capture intent explicitly — personalization on day one, bought with questions instead of months of history.

COLD-START CURE, FIVE PARTS
  1. Make session one genuinely good with zero data.
  2. Ask for intent explicitly — onboarding questions beat inference.
  3. Own the destination experience end to end.
  4. Watch the energy in the system, not just the metrics.
  5. Shorten time-to-learning — no two-year private betas.
WHY IT MATTERS

Intelligence is a feature of the model; usefulness is a property of the product. Every AI product alive today is one cold-start problem away from being an Artifact — the cure is designing session one as hard as session one hundred.

VISUAL ANALOGY

A restaurant with a secret hundredth-visit menu: the food at visit thirty is life-changing — but nobody ever found out, because day one is the only meal most customers eat, and it tasted like every other restaurant on the street.

✗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Personalization is a feature you can ship later, once you have data.”

CLICK TO FLIP →
✓ FIELD REALITY

If the product is only great after months of usage, it isn’t great — it’s a loyalty program. Day one has to stand on its own, before any data arrives.

CLICK TO FLIP BACK
MEMORY TECHNIQUE

D.A.Y. O.N.E. — Delight with zero data, Ask intent, You own the experience, One short beta, No tenure required, Energy over metrics.

OBVIOUS

The generic first feed

A new Artifact user got a nearly generic feed. The magic required reading hundreds of articles nobody had time to give.

COUNTER-INTUITIVE

Better product, worse growth

The product improved with every session — and that improvement curve was the growth killer. Quality that requires tenure taxes every first impression.

EDGE CASE

Claude’s onboarding questions

The workaround, built from the wound: capture intent with explicit questions on day one, instead of waiting months to infer it implicitly. Artifact’s failure became Anthropic’s first-session design.

Closing Chapters Well

Every founder eventually faces the question Mike Krieger faced in late 2023: how do you know when the chapter is over? His answer is a mechanism, not a mood. He and Kevin sat down and wrote a list of every idea they would feel silly not having tried before shutting the company down. They prioritized three. They ran them. And they attached a hard checkpoint — the turn into 2024 — with one question: did any of this change the trajectory? When the answer was no, they closed Artifact. The method works because it replaces vague dread with concrete evidence: a date, a bounded set of experiments, a yes-or-no question.

Mike’s second piece of advice is even more counterintuitive: talk to your investors. Founders grind on for years imagining the investor expects relentless persistence — but as an angel investor himself, Mike has watched the other side of the table. Failure is baked into the model, and investors will often be the first to say you’ve tried everything and it’s okay to stop. It’s never a celebration, he says, but it doesn’t have to be a tragedy. It’s also not just a company question. He left Instagram after eight years at its peak because the learning had flattened, and he audits every year of his career with one question — am I still learning? Companies change, projects change, economies change; the relationships you build are the only assets that recur. Close chapters cleanly, and they compound.

CHAPTER-CLOSE PROTOCOL
  1. Write down the ideas you’d regret not trying.
  2. Prioritize three or fewer.
  3. Set a concrete checkpoint date.
  4. Run the experiments — no moving the date.
  5. Ask: did the trajectory change? Yes/no, not maybe.
  6. Talk to your investors honestly — they can handle it.
  7. Close cleanly. Invest in the relationships that recur.
WHY IT MATTERS

Chapters end whether you plan for it or not. The regret-list converts a fear-driven decision into an evidence-driven one, and the honest investor call converts a potential tragedy into a reputation asset you carry into the next wave.

VISUAL ANALOGY

Poker pot odds: good players don’t ask “how much have I invested in this hand?” They ask “which cards can still save me — and how many are left?” The regret-list counts the outs. When the list is empty, folding is math, not failure.

✗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Real founders never quit — and investors expect you to keep grinding.”

CLICK TO FLIP →
✓ FIELD REALITY

Failure is baked into the model. Investors will often tell you themselves it’s time to stop — and closing well preserves the relationships that show up in your next chapter.

APPENDIX B

Still Confused?

FAQ

No — and the distinction is evidence. A pivot is subtraction applied to something that is already working: Burbn’s photo behavior was real, measurable, and growing inside a mediocre app. Quitting, in Mike’s sense, is what you do when no such core exists — when the regret-list experiments run and the trajectory still doesn’t change. The pivot asks “what’s working that I’m ignoring?” The quit protocol asks “have I honestly exhausted what could work?” Confusing the two is expensive: pivoting too late wastes a core, and quitting too early wastes a company.

Run the two-signal test. First: are early adopters already doing the thing voluntarily, awkwardly, on inferior tools? Enthusiasm that survives bad UX is signal. Second: measure the rate of change in hard specs — camera megapixels per generation, model capability per release — and ask whether one or two more jumps would make it incredible. Hype has headlines; waves have measurable jumps. And run the Artifact counter-check: the technology wave must carry the whole experience chain. ML improved on schedule, but the ad-laden mobile web never became ready, and the wave passed underneath the product anyway.

Yes — and specifically, with a plan attached. Mike’s observation from both sides of the table is that founders imagine investors demand relentless persistence, while investors are quietly pricing in failure as normal. The entrepreneurs who suffer most are the ones who hide the flat numbers and grind for years out of imagined obligation. Bring the data, your three regret-list experiments, and a checkpoint date. You’ll often hear the sentence you were afraid of — “you’ve tried everything, it’s okay to call it” — and it will come with the relationship intact.

Mike’s own verdict on Artifact’s two-year private beta: too long, and he says so directly. The costs were double — the team arrived at launch exhausted, and every lesson the market could have taught was delayed by two years. Short betas aren’t about shipping something broken; they’re about compressing the time between assumption and evidence. If your product only gets good with usage, early exposure is the only way to discover that — and to fix the day-one experience before the team has nothing left.

That was Artifact’s second wound: perfect recommendations that ended on ad-stuffed mobile pages with pop-up videos. The lesson is brutal but useful — users experience the whole chain, and they blame you for all of it. Where you can, own the destination: render the content yourself, control the format, cut the last-mile friction. Where you can’t, treat ecosystem readiness as part of your wave-timing analysis. A great product on a broken last mile is a great product with a leaky bucket.
