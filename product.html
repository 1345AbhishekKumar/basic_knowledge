From facts to feelings

A 100-year history of emotional marketing, in four jumps — from the mail-order catalog to the olive-oil waitlist.

A CORE IDEA

Rewind to the early 1900s and marketing was, by modern standards, almost charmingly honest. Companies showed you the product, told you what it did, listed the price, and wished you a good day. The era's great innovation — the mail-order catalog — was revolutionary for its time: hundreds of products, descriptions and prices delivered to rural doorsteps. But flip through one today and you'll notice what's missing. No lifestyle photography. No emotional storytelling. No brand identity. Just cold, hard facts arranged in neat columns.

Then, in the 1920s, the ground shifted. Advertisers noticed something profound: people don't buy products so much as better versions of themselves. Nobody chose a car for horsepower alone — they bought the feeling of being someone who owned a car. Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew, turned that insight into a discipline. He reasoned that if you attached a product to unconscious desires — freedom, status, belonging — the product stopped competing on specs and started competing on feelings.

His 1929 proof of concept is infamous. Hired by the American Tobacco Company to make it acceptable for women to smoke in public, Bernays staged women lighting cigarettes during New York's Easter Sunday parade and told the press they were lighting "Torches of Freedom." Overnight, a tobacco stick became a symbol of women's liberation. The product never changed. The meaning did — and that is the entire trick. A century later, we join waitlists for olive oil the way our grandparents queued for appliances. The facts never left; they just stopped being the point.

The product never changed. The meaning did.

Knowing the origin story lets you recognize the machinery everywhere. Every "limited drop," every countdown timer, every founder "story" is a descendant of Torches of Freedom — a product wearing a meaning like a costume. Once you see the lineage, you can't unsee it.

Graza: the cool friend

How a squeeze bottle and a shade of green reimagined one of humanity's oldest products.

A CORE IDEA

Olive oil is one of the oldest foods in human history, and for most of that history it was sold the same way: a serious bottle, a serious label, a serious story about an Italian hillside. Graza looked at that script and tore it up. The product inside is good olive oil — but good olive oil is not what Graza is selling.

Start with the bottle. It's a squeeze bottle, the kind you'd expect in a diner, and that is a deliberate statement: we're not your grandmother's olive oil, we're not precious, we're here to be used — not saved for a special occasion. Then the color: a bold, bright green that isn't trying to look expensive or traditional. It's modern, fun, almost rebellious — leave it on the counter and it stops being an ingredient and becomes kitchen décor.

The content follows the same logic. Graza's channels aren't filled with serious cooking tutorials or traditional Italian recipes. They're filled with people drizzling oil on pizza, tossing five-minute pastas, dipping bread — simple, imperfect, doable. Marketers call this aspirational but achievable: you watch a thirty-second clip and think, "hey, I could do that." The feeling being sold is the feeling of being an effortlessly cool home cook — someone who knows good food without being pretentious about it. And it works: the first batch sold out in hours, building a cult following most brands would kill for.

"Hey — I could do that." ← the entire business model

D VISUAL ANALOGY

The friend who cooks

Graza is not the chef lecturing you about harvest dates and regions. It's the friend who throws together pantry pasta in twelve minutes and makes it look like a lifestyle. You don't envy them — you want to be them. That's the pull.

E MYTH CHECK
Myth

"People join the waitlist because the oil is objectively the best."

Quality must explain the frenzy, right? Flip to check.

Reality

The oil is the entry ticket.

What's sold out in hours is identity and belonging. Quality is what keeps people after the feeling recruits them — but recruitment is an emotional act. Great product, average story = no waitlist.

F WHY IT MATTERS

Graza hands you a transferable formula: distinctive design + achievable aspiration + community = emotional value that outruns the commodity. None of it requires inventing a new product. It requires reimagining how a product makes people feel about themselves.

Liquid Death: murder your thirst

Water — but make it punk rock. How absurdity became a business model.

A CORE IDEA

Say the name out loud: Liquid Death. It's water. Water called death, sold in a tallboy can that looks like it should be holding beer. On paper it's absurd — and the absurdity is the strategy.

The insight was psychological. The founders noticed a huge audience that felt actively alienated by traditional water branding: the pristine mountain springs, the yoga-at-dawn wellness messaging, the quiet superiority of "alkaline." So they went completely counterculture. The tagline is "Murder Your Thirst." The marketing includes supplying water to death metal concerts, cutting horror-movie trailers about killer water, and — this is real — pressing a heavy metal album made entirely from the hate comments people left about the brand.

The result is a masterclass in a crucial distinction: this isn't advertising, it's entertainment. When someone shares a Liquid Death video, they're not sharing an ad — they're sharing something that made them laugh, something that signals their humor and identity. Hydration becomes rebellion; drinking water becomes, genuinely, punk rock. And the brand never breaks character, which is what turns a joke into a community: an in-joke that simultaneously makes fun of and revolutionizes beverage marketing. People don't photograph themselves drinking water — except they do, proudly, can raised, because the can says something about them.

It's not an ad. It's entertainment — the ad just rides along.

The emotional brain buys first

Damasio's patients, the justification engine, and the number 64%.

A CORE IDEA

Here's the uncomfortable finding at the center of modern marketing: you decide with your feelings, then hire your logic to argue the case. Consumer psychology studies keep landing on the same sequence — the emotional brain makes the call first, and the rational brain drafts the justification afterwards. "It was on sale." "It'll last years." "I deserve it." Those aren't reasons; they're press releases issued after the decision has already been made.

The clearest evidence comes from neuroscientist Antonio Damasio, who studied people with damage to the brain regions that process emotion. These patients were fully intelligent. They could list every pro and con of a choice, fluently and accurately. And yet they couldn't choose — not what to eat, not what to buy, not anything. Without emotional input they were paralyzed by choice, because every option weighed exactly the same. Emotion isn't noise in decision-making; it's the scale.

This is what brands like Graza and Liquid Death understand at a structural level. They're not trying to win the rational argument — nobody is comparing water pH in a spreadsheet. They're trying to win the emotional one, then hand your rational brain some plausible talking points. It also explains the stat anchoring this lesson: roughly 64% of consumers say they're more likely to buy from brands that align with their values. Values are feelings with a spine. The purchase is an emotional act; the spreadsheet comes later.

Emotion isn't the noise in decision-making — it's the scale.

C THREE EXAMPLES
The obvious one

The impulse buy

The want arrives first, fully formed. The "because" — sale, durability, deserve — gets written afterwards by your in-house press office.

The counter-intuitive one

Damasio's patients

Perfect logic, zero ability to choose. Emotion isn't the enemy of good decisions — it's the mechanism that makes decisions possible at all.

The edge case

The 64% values stat

Even our most "principled" purchases are emotionally coded. Values are feelings that learned to dress formally for the meeting.

D VISUAL ANALOGY

The driver and the mapmaker

Your emotional brain grabs the wheel and picks the destination. Your rational brain sits in the passenger seat, drawing a map after the fact and insisting — sincerely — that it planned the route all along. Neither of them is lying. One of them is just telling a better story.

E MYTH CHECK
Myth

"I research everything, so I'm immune."

Surely due diligence protects you? Flip to check.

Reality

Research is often the lawyer, not the judge.

Most "research" is the rational brain collecting evidence for a decision the emotional brain already made. Immunity isn't the goal — consciousness is. You can watch the map being drawn and still enjoy the ride.

F WHY IT MATTERS

If feelings decide first, then selling and shopping are both emotional skills. For marketers: win the feeling, then arm the justification. For everyone else: the single most powerful consumer move is naming the emotion in the room before it names you. That pivot sets up the final module.

Community, ethics & the relationship economy

From fandom to greenwashing — and how to shop consciously inside a machine built to be loved.

A CORE IDEA

Social media didn't invent emotional marketing — it industrialized it. Graza's feed rarely mentions the technical qualities of its oil; it posts gorgeous, casual clips of simple meals, and when customers repost their own cooking, the message quietly lands: look at all these cool people who use this — you could be one of them. Liquid Death's content gets shared as entertainment, and every share recruits. Customers stop being consumers and become advocates — ambassadors with inside jokes. Community is the compounding asset; ads are the depreciating one.

The next wave is already visible. Call it micro-emotional targeting: AI and analytics that understand not just what you buy but how you feel when you buy it — Netflix-level personalization pointed at your emotions. Brands will increasingly act as content companies and storytellers, building deep bonds with small, devoted micro-communities instead of shouting at everyone.

And here is the elephant in the room. When 64% of consumers say they buy from brands that share their values, faking values becomes a business model — hence greenwashing, the flood of environmental claims that don't survive scrutiny. The better the technique gets, the harder it is to separate authentic belief from clever tactics. The defense isn't cynicism; it's conscious participation. Ask: is this feeling backed by real value? Patagonia is the standing example — the Worn Wear program and genuine activism mean the emotional connection is collateralized by action. It's fine to enjoy the show, join the fandom, buy into the story — as long as you know it's a story, and it adds real value to your life. We're moving from a transactional economy to a relationship economy. The useful question is no longer "what am I buying?" but "what am I joining?"

Community is the compounding asset. Ads are the depreciating one.

Graza's UGC loop

Customers post, the brand reposts, new customers want in. The marketing department is now everyone's camera roll.

The counter-intuitive one

Patagonia's Worn Wear

A brand actively discouraging new purchases — repairing old gear instead — and being trusted more for it. Anti-consumption as the ultimate authenticity signal.

The edge case

Greenwashing

The technique's dark mirror: emotional claims with no collateral, exploiting exactly the value-seeking instinct this lesson describes.

D VISUAL ANALOGY

Brands are becoming bands

You don't just buy the album — you wear the t-shirt, learn the inside jokes, join the fandom, and defend them in the comments. Nobody has ever identity-posted a spreadsheet about a band. That's the point.

E MYTH CHECK
Myth

"Knowing the tricks makes marketing powerless over me."

Awareness = immunity? Flip to check.

Reality

Awareness switches on choice, not off feeling.

Understanding the technique doesn't make it less effective — it makes you more conscious. You can enjoy the show and still know it's a show. That's not weakness; that's being an adult at the concert.

F WHY IT MATTERS

You are no longer a passive consumer — you're a participant in brand stories whether you opted in or not. The relationship economy rewards the people who can ask, calmly, in the middle of the feeling: "What emotion are they selling — confidence, rebellion, or belonging — and is it backed by real value?"

Make it stick

Three memory tools: a method you can run at any checkout, a shelf you can walk in your head, and cards to drill the vocabulary.

The P.A.U.S.E. Method

Run this the next time a brand makes you feel something. ~20 seconds, works at any checkout.

P

Pinpoint the feeling. "What emotion are they selling me right now — confidence, rebellion, belonging?"

A

Ask for the collateral. "Is this feeling backed by real value I can verify?"

U

Unpack the story. "Who do I become if I buy this? Who wrote that script?"

S

Separate product from pitch. "Would I want the thing without the story?"

E

Enjoy consciously. Participation beats immunity. Buy the story if it adds value — just know you're buying it.
