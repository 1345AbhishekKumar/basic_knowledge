Interactive Lesson · Based on a Conversation with Harry Dry

Write Copy That
Can't Be Copied

Three rules, six modules, and twenty-five rewrites: a field guide to writing sentences people actually remember.

Source: "Write of Passage" podcast · David Perell × Harry Dry · Lesson prepared July 2026

What You'll Learn

This lesson distils a two-hour conversation between writer David Perell and copywriter Harry Dry into six working modules. Harry is the founder of Marketing Examples and the author of a copywriting course built on a single conviction: most writing is rubbish, and the cure is not more creativity but better elimination.

By the end, you will be able to:

  • Run any sentence through a three-question filter and know, objectively, whether it works
  • Convert abstract fog into concrete pictures using the zoom-in method
  • Replace subjective adjectives with falsifiable claims that persuade by being verifiable
  • Write lines that no competitor can sign
  • Structure copy around who you're talking to, what you believe, and how you say it
  • Rewrite a draft twenty-five times without losing your mind, using Kaplan's Law and the tap metaphor
Memory anchor: The whole lesson compresses to one sentence — "If you can't see it, prove it, or own it, rewrite it."

Module 1

The Three Rules

Core Explanation

Every sentence you write has to survive three questions. Not two. Not "does it sound nice?" Three specific, answerable, yes-or-no questions that Harry Dry runs past every single line of copy he has ever written or reviewed. Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say this? That's the whole framework. Three questions. Three yeses and you're on to something. Three nos and you've probably written what Harry politely calls "a lot of rubbish."

Here's why this works when vaguer advice doesn't. Most writing tips are aspirational. "Be engaging." "Write with punch." "Make it memorable." Fine. But how do you know if you've done it? You don't. You're guessing. You're relying on the feeling in your gut at two in the morning, and your gut is a liar. These three questions replace the gut with a checklist. They turn a subjective art into something you can actually test, sentence by sentence, the way an engineer tests a bridge.

Think of them as three bouncers at the door of a club. Your sentence walks up. First bouncer: "Can I see you?" If the reader can't picture what you're saying, no entry. Second bouncer: "Is your ID real?" If your claim can't be proved true or false, it's a fake, and fake IDs don't get in. Third bouncer: "Are you on the list?" If any competitor could say the same sentence and slap their logo on it, you're not on the list. You need all three. Miss one and the sentence stays outside in the cold.

The power of this system is that it's ruthlessly democratic. It doesn't care whether you're a junior copywriter or David Ogilvy. It doesn't care whether the sentence took you ten seconds or ten hours. The sentence either passes or it doesn't. And that objectivity is what lets you kill your darlings without grief, because you're not killing them. They simply failed the test.

Step-by-Step: Running the Filter

  1. Write your sentence. Any sentence. A headline, a tagline, an email subject line.
  2. Gate 1 — Visualize: Close your eyes. Can you see it? If it's fog, rewrite toward a picture.
  3. Gate 2 — Falsify: Could someone prove this true or false? If it's unfalsifiable opinion, find a fact to point at.
  4. Gate 3 — Own: Put your competitor's logo next to it. Does it still work? If yes, dig deeper for what only you can say.
  5. Three yeses? Ship it. Any no? You know which gate to revisit.

Three Examples

Obvious case — fails all three

"Don't just get a job. Change an entire industry." A recruitment company's ad. Close your eyes. Can you see "changing an industry"? No. Can you prove it true or false? No. Could any recruitment company say it? Absolutely — Harry could start one tomorrow and write the identical line. Three nos. Rubbish.

Counter-intuitive case — passes all three

"New Balance worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." You can see the supermodel. You can see the dad with his barbecue and his New Balance trainers. It's true — supermodels have worn them, dads in Ohio do wear them. And no one else can say it, because dads in Ohio aren't wearing Prada and supermodels aren't wearing Reebok. The juxtaposition is what makes it sing. Three yeses.

Edge case — passes with a caveat

"A thousand songs in your pocket." Apple's iPod launch line. You can visualize it — a hand, a pocket, music. No competitor can say it because no one else had that storage in that form factor. But strictly, it's not literally true: the songs are in a media player, not your pocket. It's metonymy — a visual word substituted for a literal one. Falsifiability is looser here, and that's the edge: sometimes a line is so strong on two gates that a soft third is forgivable. But you should know you're bending the rule.

Visual Analogy

Three bouncers at a club door. Each checks one thing. Your sentence needs all three stamps to get in. No VIP list, no exceptions. The bouncer doesn't care how long you spent getting dressed.

What People Get Wrong

That the three rules are about being clever or creative. They're not. They're about elimination. Most writing is rubbish not because it lacks flair, but because it was never tested. The rules don't generate good copy. They help you see that what you've written is bad, so you can rewrite it into something that isn't.

Why This Matters

Without an objective test, you're stuck in the "I think this sounds good" loop, which is where most marketing goes to die. The three rules give you a shared vocabulary for editing. You can hand a sentence to a colleague and say "it fails gate two" and they know exactly what you mean. That turns copywriting from a talent contest into a craft.

Module 2

Concrete vs. Abstract

Core Explanation

Harry reads you six words. Seamless transition. Charging pitbull. Muscly Irishman. Better way. Leg of lamb. Which ones do you remember? You remember the pitbull. You remember the Irishman. You remember the leg of lamb. You don't remember "seamless transition." You don't remember "better way." And the reason is not that you're stupid. The reason is that your brain is a visual organ pretending to be a linguistic one. It doesn't store words. It stores pictures. And "charging pitbull" is a picture. "Better way" is fog.

Lisa Cron, the story consultant, puts it bluntly: "If I can't see it, it's not there yet." That's the whole lesson in nine words. Abstract language is intangible. You can't drop it on your foot. Try dropping "a better way" on your foot. It passes through your shoe like a ghost. Now try dropping a muscly Irishman on your foot. You'll feel that. You'll remember that. You'll tell your friends about the Irishman.

So how do you get from fog to foot? Harry's method is almost embarrassingly simple. You take a sheet of paper. You draw a line down the left side. At the top you write "abstract." At the bottom you write "concrete." You put your vague word at the top. And then you rewrite it. And rewrite it. And rewrite it. Each time you ask: "What do I actually mean here? What am I actually saying?" You keep zooming in until you hit something you can see, touch, or drop on your foot.

Someone comes to Harry with an app idea. "It's about regaining fitness." Regain. Fitness. Two abstract words. What do you mean, regain? Getting people off the couch. What couch? The one they've been sitting on for six months. What do you mean, fitness? Running. How far? Five K. And now you haven't got "regain fitness." You've got "Couch to 5K." Which, as it happens, is the name of the most downloaded fitness program in history. Not made by Nike. Not made by Adidas. Made by a television producer in Boston who understood that concrete beats abstract every single time.

Step-by-Step: The Zoom-In Test

  1. Write your abstract word at the top of a page. ("Regain fitness." "Better way." "Innovative.")
  2. Ask: "What do I actually mean?" Rewrite one level more specific.
  3. Ask again. And again. Each rewrite should be more visual, more physical, more droppable-on-a-foot.
  4. Stop when you hit a concrete object, person, place, or action you can photograph.
  5. Read the top word and the bottom word side by side. The distance between them is how much work your copy needed.

Three Examples

Obvious case

The six-word game. Pitbull, Irishman, leg of lamb — all concrete, all remembered. Seamless transition, better way — both abstract, both forgotten. The test takes ten seconds and it proves the point more effectively than any lecture. You don't need to be told that concrete is memorable. You just experienced it.

Counter-intuitive case

Couch to 5K. The most downloaded fitness program ever. Not made by Nike, not by Adidas — made by a Boston TV producer. "Regain fitness" is what a marketing committee would write. "Couch to 5K" is what you get when you zoom in: regain → off the couch → running → 5K. The concrete version didn't just describe the product better. It became the product name. The zoom-in test didn't just improve the copy. It invented the brand.

Edge case — the gradient

"Pretty people in big cities" vs. "supermodels in London." Harry points out that "pretty people" is almost concrete. You can kind of see it. But "supermodel" is sharper — everyone sees the same image. And "London" is sharper than "big cities." This reveals that concrete-vs-abstract isn't a binary switch. It's a gradient. You can always zoom in one more level. The question is whether the extra specificity serves the sentence or clutters it. "Supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" works because both halves are equally sharp. If one were vague, the juxtaposition would collapse.

Visual Analogy

Abstract words are ghosts. They pass through your foot. Concrete words are Irishmen. They hurt when they land. Your job as a writer is to stop throwing ghosts and start dropping Irishmen.

What People Get Wrong

That "concrete" means "use more adjectives." It doesn't. It means fewer, more specific nouns. "A beautiful, stunning, gorgeous woman" is three adjectives doing the work of zero pictures. "A supermodel in London" is two nouns doing the work of a photograph. Adjectives decorate. Nouns build.

Why This Matters

Memory is visual. If the reader can't see it, they won't remember it, and if they don't remember it, they certainly won't act on it. Every abstract word in your copy is a place where the reader's attention leaks out. Concrete words plug the leaks.

🔗 Concrete language is the foundation for falsifiability (Module 3) — you can't point at a ghost. And it feeds differentiation (Module 4) — "supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" is specific enough that no one else can claim it.
Memory anchor: "Can I drop it on my foot?" If the answer is no, zoom in further.

Module 3

Falsifiability: Don't Talk, Only Point

Core Explanation

Here's a sentence: "The earth has a harmonious connection with a celestial object." The Inquisition would have shrugged. Galileo would have gone to the pub. Instead, Galileo said: "The earth spins around the sun." And they gave him ten years of house arrest. Same information. Completely different voltage. The difference is falsifiability. The first sentence is so vague it can't be wrong. The second sentence is a claim that can be checked, measured, proved, or disproved. And that is exactly why it's dangerous. And exactly why it's persuasive.

Harry's best tip for this came from a deleted Reddit account, and he's still annoyed he can't credit it. The tip is four words: "Don't talk. Only point." Imagine you're setting up your best friend on a blind date. Version one: you can only use subjective adjectives. He's good-looking. He's intelligent. He's funny. He's got great values. And the person across the table is thinking: "Sure. Everyone says that." Now version two: you can only say things that are true or false. Looks like Ryan Gosling. Six foot two. Reads on the tube. Suddenly the person is leaning forward. Because you've given them something they can verify. And in verifying it, they persuade themselves.

This is the chopping block principle. When you write a falsifiable sentence, you're putting your head on the block. You're saying: "I will stake my credibility on this being true." And the reader feels that stake. Their ears prick up. This person is betting something. That's rare. That's worth listening to.

The gold seller example makes it physical. You can say: "Gold is a great investment. It never goes out of fashion." You're talking. You're waving your hands. Or you can point at the fifty-year graph. Point at what happened to gold in the last financial crash. Point at Warren Buffett's portfolio and ask how much gold is in it. You're not arguing anymore. You're showing. And showing is an argument that can't be talked back to.

Step-by-Step: From Talking to Pointing

  1. Find the adjective in your sentence. ("Great." "Innovative." "High-quality." "Passionate.")
  2. Ask: "What would prove this adjective true?" What evidence, fact, or image would make a skeptic nod?
  3. Replace the adjective with the evidence. Don't describe — point.
  4. Check: is the new sentence true or false? If someone could fact-check it, you've done it right.

Three Examples

Obvious case

The blind date exercise. "Good-looking, intelligent, funny, great values" is wallpaper. Every single person on earth has been described with those words. "Looks like Ryan Gosling. Six foot two. Reads on the tube" is a person. The shift is from adjectives (subjective, unfalsifiable, forgettable) to facts (objective, verifiable, sticky). The meme Harry references — women holding signs that read "6'5", works in finance, trust fund" — is the same principle turned into a joke. It's funny because it's so aggressively falsifiable.

Counter-intuitive case

Galileo's house arrest. You'd think the safe, vague sentence is the smart one. "The earth has a harmonious connection with a celestial object" wouldn't have got Galileo in trouble. But it also wouldn't have changed anyone's mind. The persuasive power and the danger are the same thing. Falsifiability is risky by design. That's why it works. If your claim can't get you in trouble, it can't move anyone either.

Edge case — pointing without numbers

The Heinz ketchup fact. Harry's example: restaurants pour factory ketchup into Heinz bottles. That's a fact. No numbers involved. But from that fact, you can build an ad: a woman pours generic ketchup into a Heinz bottle, hands it to a customer. Tagline: "Even when it's not Heinz, it's Heinz." The fact is falsifiable (you could go watch), it's visual (you can see the pour), and it's ownable (only Heinz can claim that even imposters choose their bottle). Falsifiable doesn't mean statistical. It means checkable.

Visual Analogy

The chopping block. A falsifiable claim is your head on the block. You're betting your credibility. The reader sees the blade and thinks: "This person must be sure." An unfalsifiable claim is a speech given from a safe distance. No blade. No bet. No reason to listen.

What People Get Wrong

That "falsifiable" means "numerical." It doesn't. "He reads on the tube" contains no numbers, but it's true or false. "Looks like Ryan Gosling" is a comparison that can be assessed. The test is not "does it contain a statistic?" The test is "could someone check this and say yes or no?" A fact can be an image, a comparison, a verifiable event. Numbers are one kind of pointing. They're not the only kind.

Why This Matters

Subjective claims bounce off the reader's skepticism like pebbles off a wall. The reader has heard "innovative" and "passionate" ten thousand times. Their brain auto-filters. But a falsifiable claim slips through because the reader can verify it — and in the act of verifying, they persuade themselves. You don't convince them. They convince themselves. That's the whole game.

🔗 Falsifiability depends on concrete language (Module 2) — you can't point at a ghost. And it strengthens differentiation (Module 4) — a verifiable claim about your specific product is harder for competitors to copy than an adjective.
Memory anchor: "Don't talk. Only point." Four words. If you're using an adjective, you're talking. If you're showing evidence, you're pointing.

Module 4

Differentiation: Can Nobody Else Say This?

Core Explanation

Jim Durky's rule is seven words long and it will save you years of mediocre advertising: "Never write an ad a competitor can sign." Read that again. If you could swap your logo for your competitor's logo and the sentence still works, you haven't written an ad. You've written wallpaper. You've written the thing that every company in your category says, which means you've said nothing, which means the reader's brain has already filed you under "noise" and moved on.

Harry's favourite test for this is almost childishly simple. He asks: "Can nobody else say this?" Not "is this clever?" Not "is this funny?" Can nobody else say it? Volvo ran an ad that said: "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six. One could get the impression that the people who made your car lack a little confidence." Nobody else can say that. The only way to verify it is to get in a Volvo and count the dials. The claim is welded to the product. You can't peel it off and stick it on a BMW.

Corvette took a different route. Their ad was six words: "They don't write songs about Volvos." They're selling a Corvette by taking a cheap shot at Volvo. And it works because it's true. Post Malone sings about Corvettes. Country singers sing about Corvettes. Nobody is writing a ballad about a Volvo estate. The differentiation is not in the Corvette's engineering. It's in the culture. And only Corvette can claim that cultural space.

Then there's Hinge. "The dating app designed to be deleted." Think about how strange that is. An app that wants you to delete it. Every other dating app wants you to stay forever, swiping, paying, subscribing. Hinge says: "No. We want you to meet someone and leave." That's not just a tagline. That's a business model turned into a sentence. And no competitor can copy it because copying it would mean admitting that their own product is one you should escape from.

Step-by-Step: The Competitor-Sign Test

  1. Write your headline or tagline.
  2. Now imagine your biggest competitor's logo underneath it. Does it still make sense?
  3. If yes, your line is generic. Ask: "What is structurally true about my product that isn't true about theirs?"
  4. Build the sentence around that structural truth. The claim should require your specific product to verify.
  5. Re-run the competitor test. If they can't sign it, you own it.

Three Examples

Obvious case

Volvo's speedometer. "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six." The claim is physically embedded in the product. You have to sit in a Volvo to check it. No other manufacturer can make this argument because it's literally about the number of dials on Volvo's dashboard. It's also visual (you can see the speedometer), falsifiable (count them), and ownable (only Volvo). All three gates, one ad.

Counter-intuitive case

Corvette's cheap shot. "They don't write songs about Volvos." You're selling a Corvette by talking about Volvo. That seems backwards. But the insight is cultural: Corvettes live in songs, in movies, in the American imagination. Volvos live in school run carpools. The differentiation isn't horsepower or price. It's romance. And the only way to claim romance is to point at the culture and say: "Look. They sing about us. They don't sing about them." No competitor can sign that line because no competitor has the song catalogue.

Edge case — the paradox

Hinge: "Designed to be deleted." This works because it's a paradox. Apps are designed to be kept. An app designed to be deleted is contradicting its own category. That contradiction is the differentiation. But here's the edge: this line is only ownable because Hinge's business model actually supports it. They make money from premium subscriptions during the search, not from keeping you single forever. If a competitor copied the line, users would say: "No, you're not. You want me to stay." The line is protected not by cleverness but by truth. That's the deepest form of differentiation: saying something that's only true for you.

Visual Analogy

A fingerprint. If anyone could leave it, it's not identification. Your copy should be a fingerprint — a mark that only your product can make. If the print could belong to anyone, it belongs to no one.

What People Get Wrong

That differentiation means being louder or more creative. It doesn't. It means being more specific and true about what you uniquely are. "We're the most innovative solution" is loud and generic. "Your car has five numbers. We have six" is quiet and uncopyable. The volume is irrelevant. The specificity is everything.

Why This Matters

In a world of infinite choice, the only reason to pick you over them is something only you can say. If your copy is interchangeable, your product is interchangeable. And if your product is interchangeable, you compete on price. And if you compete on price, you lose. Differentiation in copy is not decoration. It's survival.

🔗 Differentiation is the payoff of concrete language (Module 2) and falsifiable claims (Module 3). The more specific and verifiable your claim, the harder it is for a competitor to sign. And it feeds directly into Piece 2 of the Three Pieces (Module 5): having something to say means having something only you can say.
Memory anchor: The Competitor Swap. Physically imagine your rival's logo under your headline. If it fits, rewrite.

Module 5

The Three Pieces: Who, What, How

Core Explanation

Ask ten people what copywriting is and nine will say "saying it well." Fancy words. Persuasive rhythm. Clever turns of phrase. And they're wrong. Or rather, they're describing one-third of the job and calling it the whole thing. Harry breaks copywriting into three pieces, and the order matters. Piece one: who are you talking to? Piece two: do you have something to say? Piece three: say it well. Most people skip straight to piece three. They polish sentences that were never aimed at anyone and never meant anything. And then they wonder why nobody buys.

Piece one is the one that costs real money when you get it wrong. Snapchat spent seven million dollars on a Super Bowl ad. Seven million. The ad said something like "More Snapchat, less social media." The average age of a Super Bowl viewer is thirty-nine. Picture a thirty-nine-year-old who has just figured out Facebook. Who has six friends left. Who thinks Snapchat is something his niece does. That's who they were talking to. And they spent seven million dollars not thinking about it.

Now picture two cafés on the same street. The first has a blown-up menu outside. Seventeen types of latte. Prices for each. Follow us on Instagram. The second café has a sign that says: "Coffee and pastry equals five pounds." Everyone is queuing outside the second one. Why? Because the reader is a tourist walking past, not paying attention, taking in the sights. Their job is not to compare oat milk flat whites. Their job is to get caffeine and a croissant. Form follows function. The copy that works is the copy that matches the reader's actual moment.

Piece two is the one nobody teaches because it's not a technique. It's a belief. Why did you start this business? What do you think is wrong with the world? Dave Garroway asked Harry that, and Harry's answer was: "I think every marketer should learn to write." That's not a strategy. That's a conviction. And conviction is the only fuel that doesn't run out. Zoura's investors weren't buying a product. They were buying the belief that everything was going to be subscription. The belief came first. The product was the evidence.

Step-by-Step: Stringing the Wire

  1. Define Point A and Point B. John Henley's rule: the current attitude of the consumer is the starting point. The desired attitude is the finish line. You can't start a race in the middle.
  2. Piece 1 — Who: Where are they physically? What's their attention level? What do they already know? A tourist glancing at a café sign is not the same reader as a developer comparing API docs.
  3. Piece 2 — What: What do you believe? What's wrong with the world that your product fixes? If you can't answer in one sentence, you don't have a message yet.
  4. Piece 3 — How: Now — and only now — do you craft the sentence. Rhythm, alliteration, juxtaposition, metonymy. The decoration goes on last.

Three Examples

Obvious case — Piece 1 failure

Snapchat's $7M Super Bowl ad. "More Snapchat, less social media." Aimed at an audience with an average age of 39. A 39-year-old who just worked out Facebook. Who has six friends left. The message wasn't bad. The targeting was catastrophic. They answered "how do we say it?" without first asking "who are we saying it to?" Seven million dollars, and the answer was: nobody. Harry's dry verdict: "If you use Snapchat at 39, you're a pedophile."

Counter-intuitive case — less copy wins

The Yellow Canary café. Two cafés, same street. One has a seventeen-item menu with prices and Instagram handles. The other says "Coffee and pastry = £5." Everyone queues at the second one. The counter-intuitive lesson: the café with less copy won. Because the reader is a tourist, walking, not reading. Their cognitive budget is about four words. "Coffee and pastry equals five pounds" fits the budget. The seventeen-item menu doesn't. Form follows function. The best copy is sometimes the shortest copy, because the reader's moment demands it.

Edge case — Piece 2 rescues a weak product

Dave Kitson / The Secret Footballer. Kitson was a mediocre striker. One England cap. Innocuous career. His football book outsold Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard, and Owen combined. How? He couldn't compete on Piece 1 (nobody wanted a book by Dave Kitson). So he leaned entirely into Piece 2: "What can I say that no one else can?" He'd been in the Premier League. He could tell the truth. Gambling addictions. How many drinks the England squad had in Greece. The behind-the-scenes stuff that Beckham's PR team would never allow. And he published it as "The Secret Footballer." The product was objectively worse. The positioning was objectively better. Piece 2 did the heavy lifting.

Visual Analogy

Two telephone poles. Pole A is where the customer is now (skeptical, uninformed, happy with their current solution). Pole B is where you want them (convinced, curious, ready to buy). Your copy is the wire between them. You can't string a wire from the middle. You have to anchor both ends first. Who is Pole A? What is Pole B? Then string the wire.

What People Get Wrong

That copywriting is "saying it well." That it's a verbal skill — alliteration, rhythm, persuasion tricks. Actually, Piece 3 is the smallest piece. A beautifully written sentence aimed at the wrong person (Snapchat) or saying nothing (most corporate websites) is worse than a clumsy sentence aimed at the right person with a real message. The Yellow Canary's "Coffee and pastry = £5" is not elegant. It's not clever. It outsells the seventeen-item menu because Pieces 1 and 2 are right.

Why This Matters

Most marketing fails not because the writing is bad, but because it's aimed at the wrong person or says nothing. You can fix bad writing in an afternoon. You can't fix a wrong audience or an empty message without starting over. Getting Pieces 1 and 2 right first is the difference between rewriting a sentence and rebuilding a business.

🔗 Piece 3 ("say it well") is where the Three Rules (Module 1) do their work — they're the quality filter for the final sentence. And the Kitson example echoes differentiation (Module 4): "what can I say that no one else can?" is the same question at the product level.
Memory anchor: Telephone poles. A = current attitude. B = desired attitude. Copy = wire. Anchor both ends before you string anything.

Module 6

The Rewrite Process & Standards

Core Explanation

You can't write simply. You can only rewrite simply. That's the first and most important thing to understand about the process. The first draft is not the thing. The first draft is the raw ore. The thing is what you get after you've smashed the ore with a hammer twenty-five times and washed away everything that isn't the metal. Harry's "Throw Money and Pray" ad took two days and twenty-five rewrites. Not because he's slow. Because that's what it takes.

The process starts with a seed. A tweet. A fact. A line you saw four years ago that you couldn't stop thinking about. "The difference between one percent and two percent is not just one percent. It's one hundred percent." Harry saw that on Twitter, wrote it on a sheet, and let it sit. Then he found a structure. A Volkswagen ad from the sixties: "How to prepare your car for winter." Two columns. Ordinary car: drain the radiator, flush thoroughly, check the rubber hose, refill. Volkswagen: change the oil. The layout was the argument. Simplicity made visible. So Harry stole the structure. Not the words. The structure.

Then came the rewrites. And the rewrites are where Kaplan's Law of Words does its work. Kaplan's Law: "Any words that aren't working for you are working against you." Harry's corollary, which he calls Harry's Law: "You aren't taking Kaplan's Law seriously enough." You can cross stuff out. You should cross stuff out. The word "and" on a landing page is almost always a disease. "We sell jeans and t-shirts and socks and we have dressing rooms." Hiut Denim says: "We make jeans." Three words. Done. The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope.

The tap metaphor is the one that keeps you sane. The first water out of the tap is dirty. Mud. Rust. You wouldn't drink it. But you don't turn the tap off. You let it run. You write the bad version. And the next bad version. And the one after that. And after a few minutes the water runs clean. You just have to trust that it will. And then you text three friends, because after three hours you genuinely cannot tell if what you've written is brilliant or insane, and you need another set of eyes to say: "That one. Use that one."

Step-by-Step: From Seed to Final

  1. Collect the seed. A fact, a tweet, a line from a motor magazine. Write it down. Let it sit. Don't force it.
  2. Find the conflict. Draw a line down the page. Write opposites on each side. Before/after. Problem/solution. Corporate way/copywriting way. Conflict is the engine of interest.
  3. Steal a structure. Find an ad, a layout, a format that already works. Don't copy the words. Copy the skeleton. Harry stole the Volkswagen two-column layout. The structure carried the argument.
  4. Rewrite 20+ times. Let the tap run. Write bad versions. Kaplan's Law: cross out everything that isn't earning its place. Every word should be working or it's working against you.
  5. Get feedback. Show three versions to three friends. Don't ask "is this good?" Ask "which one is best?" Comparative feedback is ten times more useful than absolute feedback.
  6. Integrate design. Write in the tool you'll publish in. Figma for ads. ConvertKit for newsletters. The medium shapes the message. A line that looks right in a Word doc might break in three lines on a billboard.
  7. The garden walk. Step away. Walk around the garden. Come back. Can you remove anything? If not, it's done. You'll feel it: a small smile. "I got it."

Three Examples

Obvious case — the 25-rewrite ad

"Throw Money and Pray." Seed: a tweet about 1% vs 2% conversions. Structure: stolen from a 1960s Volkswagen ad. Twenty-five rewrites over two days. Placeholder images swapped for Fiverr illustrations. A header that went through "The Corporate Way" → "Go Fool Zuckerberg" → "Throw Money and Pray" based on friend feedback. A line that was two lines too long, so Harry cut "landing page" and moved "learn to write" into the header, which created a symmetry problem, which he solved with "Learn Copywriting." One step forward, one step back. The final ad looks inevitable. It wasn't. It was twenty-five drafts of mud before the water ran clean.

Counter-intuitive case — Kaplan's Law applied to ideas

Hiut Denim: "We make jeans." Kaplan's Law says any words not working for you are working against you. Harry extends this to ideas: "The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope." A company that sells jeans and t-shirts and socks and has dressing rooms is four ideas doing the work of zero. Hiut Denim does one thing. "We make jeans." Three words. Meghan Markle buys from them. The constraint is the brand. The same principle applies to album covers: Velvet Underground's banana. Dark Side of the Moon's prism. One image. Bowie's Reality cover has seven disparate images and nobody remembers it. Scope dilutes. Constraint concentrates.

Edge case — the ad with one element

The Economist: "I've never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42." Neil French's principle: most ads use four elements — header, subheader, picture, logo. If you can do an ad with just one, you're onto a winner. This ad has no picture. No subheader. The logo is the masthead text. It's just two lines of type on Economist-red background. And it works because the absence is the craft. The white space forces you to read. The lack of a picture makes it look like a confession, not an ad. The edge case: sometimes the most powerful rewrite is the one where you delete the most. The final draft isn't the one where you've added everything. It's the one where you can't remove anything else.

Visual Analogy

The tap. First water is dirty — mud, rust, the pipes clearing. You don't drink it. But you don't turn the tap off either. You let it run. Bad draft. Bad draft. Bad draft. And then, after a few minutes, the water runs clean. You just have to trust it will. The alternative — turning the tap off after the first muddy pour — is how you end up with no water at all.

What People Get Wrong

That good writing flows out fully formed. That talented writers sit down and produce the final version in one pass. They don't. Harry's ad took twenty-five rewrites. The freedom to write badly is what allows you to eventually write well. If you judge every sentence as you write it, you'll never get past the first paragraph. The tap has to run dirty before it runs clean. The misconception is that the mud is failure. It's not. It's the process.

Why This Matters

Standards are the work. Harry's line: "Your work is nothing but your standards. Nine out of ten plus it goes out. If not, you won't see it." The gap between amateur and professional is not talent. It's whether you stop at rewrite three or rewrite twenty-five. It's whether you text a friend at hour three and ask "which one?" It's whether you walk around the garden and come back and feel the small smile. The craft is in the iteration. The iteration is the craft.

🔗 The rewrite process is where the Three Rules (Module 1) earn their keep — you run each draft through the three gates and cut what fails. Kaplan's Law is the operational form of concrete language (Module 2): every abstract word you cut is a step toward the concrete. And the "garden walk" test is the final differentiation check (Module 4): if you can't remove anything, the line is ownable.
Memory anchor: "You can't write simply. You can only rewrite simply." The first draft is mud. The twenty-fifth is water. Trust the tap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be a "good writer" to use the three rules?

No. That's the point. The three rules are a filter, not a talent test. They tell you whether a sentence works. You don't need instinct. You need a checklist. Harry's own process involves twenty-five rewrites and texting friends to ask "which one?" The rules democratise the process. They replace "I think this sounds good" with "this passes three gates."

What if my product genuinely has nothing unique about it?

Then you haven't looked deep enough. Harry's Kitson example is the answer: a mediocre footballer with one England cap found something no one else could say. The question isn't "is my product unique?" It's "what can I say that no one else is saying that people care about?" Sometimes the differentiation isn't in the product. It's in the angle, the story, the positioning. Kitson's product was worse than Beckham's. His positioning was better.

Is "falsifiable" the same as "use data"?

No. Falsifiable means checkable. "He reads on the tube" has no numbers, but it's true or false. "Looks like Ryan Gosling" is a comparison you can assess. The Heinz ketchup fact — restaurants pour generic ketchup into Heinz bottles — is falsifiable without a single statistic. Data is one form of pointing. It's not the only form. The test is: could someone verify this and say yes or no?

How long should a rewrite process take?

Harry's ad took two days and twenty-five rewrites. His newsletter takes hours. His answer: "Every newsletter is tough. It's always a struggle." There's no fixed time. The test is the garden walk: step away, come back, and ask "can I remove anything?" If the answer is yes, you're not done. If the answer is no, and you feel the small smile, you're done. The clock is irrelevant. The standard is the clock.

Does AI change any of this?

Harry's answer: AI is a tool. Switching from pens to typewriters didn't make the work better. Switching from typewriters to laptops didn't make the work better. AI is the same. It's only as good as your taste. What AI doesn't have: conviction (it doesn't believe anything), experience (it hasn't sat in a Rolls-Royce at 60 mph hearing the electric clock), or the ability to arrange ideas in a way that hasn't been arranged before (it's predictive — it literally reassembles what's already been written). The three rules still apply. AI will generate three nos faster than you can. Your job is to keep asking the questions.

What's the single most important takeaway?

"Write copy that can't be copied." Everything in this lesson serves that sentence. Concrete language makes you visible. Falsifiability makes you credible. Differentiation makes you ownable. The Three Pieces make sure you're talking to the right person with a real message. The rewrite process is how you get from mud to water. But the destination is always the same: a sentence that only you could have written, that only your product could have earned, that the reader can see, verify, and remember.

Next Steps

  1. Run the filter today. Take the last email, ad, or social post you wrote. Run every sentence through the three gates. Count the nos. Rewrite the worst offender.
  2. Do the zoom-in exercise. Pick one abstract word from your website ("innovative," "seamless," "solutions"). Draw the ladder. Zoom in five levels. See what you land on.
  3. Collect seeds. Keep a note on your phone. Every time you see a line that makes you stop — an ad, a tweet, a headline — write it down. Harry's ad started with a tweet he saw four years ago. Seeds need time to germinate.
  4. Steal structures, not words. Find three ads you admire. Sketch their layout on paper. Two columns? A list? A single line? Now pour your own content into that skeleton.
  5. Do twenty-five rewrites. Pick one headline. Write twenty-five versions. Yes, twenty-five. Let the tap run. Text three friends. Ask "which one?" Walk around the garden.
  6. Read the sources. Harry recommends: On Writing Well (Zinsser), The Elements of Eloquence (Forsyth), That's Interesting (Murray Davis, 1971). And just read ads. Print them out. Pin them up. Ask: "Why does this work?"
