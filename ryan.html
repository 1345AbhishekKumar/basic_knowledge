Module 01

The Three-Slice Ad Skeleton

Hook · Meat · CTA — every ad that converts is built on these three beats, in this order.

A · Core The Anatomy of a 30-Second Ad

Every ad, regardless of length or channel, decomposes into three sequential slices. The hook grabs attention in the first 1–3 seconds — without it, the rest of the ad is invisible. The meat delivers value, framed as either "more good stuff" or "less bad stuff" (or both simultaneously). The CTA tells the prospect exactly what to do next.

Mint Mobile's "angry goat" ad demonstrates all three cleanly. The contrarian opening — "At Mint Mobile we like to do the opposite of what big Wireless does" — is the hook. It works through two mechanisms: recognition (Ryan Reynolds' face) and curiosity (what's the opposite?). The value layer follows: "They charge you a lot, we charge you a little… they announced they'd be raising their prices due to inflation, we decided to deflate our prices due to not hating you." Then the CTA: "mintmobile.com" with the $15/month unlimited anchor.

Hormozi notes the hook's job is singular: make the viewer think "this is for me." That's achieved through curiosity, contrast, recognition, or a direct call-out. The meat must convert attention into interest by maximizing perceived benefits and minimizing perceived costs (see Module 3). The CTA must be unambiguous — a URL, a price, an offer.

Many ads fail not because of weak creative but because the CTA is buried or vague. Mint Mobile ends every ad with the same destination (mintmobile.com) and the same anchor price ($15/month). Repetition compounds recognition. The same skeleton shows up in their 15-second UGC ads, their 6-second retargeting ads, and their 30-second brand spots — only the meat's thickness changes.

Example 1 · Obvious

The angry goat ad: contrarian hook → deflate-prices value → mintmobile.com CTA. Textbook three-slice structure.

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

The "$5 yard-sale stock footage" holiday ad. The frugality of the production itself becomes the value proof — the ad's cheapness mirrors the brand promise. The hook is Ryan Reynolds' face; the meat is meta (we're so cheap we made this ad for $5); the CTA is the offer.

Example 3 · Edge-case

The "chunky milk" ad. It had a hook and a CTA but the meat accidentally branded Mint Mobile with something disgusting. Structure alone isn't enough — the meat must be on-message. A skeleton with rotten meat still stinks.

Module 02

Us vs. Them — Underdog Positioning

Contrast is cognitively sticky. Use it when you're not #1. Don't use it when you are.

A · Core When Contrast Works (and When It Implodes)

The "Us vs. Them" framework is contrast positioning — defining yourself in opposition to a category leader. Mint Mobile does this relentlessly: "They charge you a lot, we charge you a little. They put their names on arenas, we put ours on my lower back." The strategy works because contrast is cognitively sticky — the brain processes differences faster than absolutes. You remember "cheaper than Verizon" longer than you remember "$15/month."

But Hormozi draws a critical boundary: this strategy works only when you are not the market leader. If you're number one, attacking competitors legitimizes them. The alternative for leaders is "Us vs. the Alternative" — e.g., a top fitness brand doesn't fight another gym, it fights "your couch." Mint Mobile can attack Verizon because Verizon is the default. Mint Mobile is the underdog; the contrast creates permission to consider switching.

The underdog must pick a fight it can win on a single axis — price, simplicity, ethics — without pretending to beat the leader on every axis. Mint Mobile never claims better coverage; they claim better value. The fight is chosen, not inherited. They also never name Verizon or AT&T directly. They use the abstraction "big Wireless," which triggers the category without inviting legal action or brand-equity transfer to a named rival.

Hormozi notes that Ryan Reynolds weaves this contrast into his personal brand sub-bullets throughout the ad — "they put their names on arenas, we put ours on my lower back" — making the positioning feel like personality rather than marketing. That tonal integration matters: contrast that feels like attack-advertising repels; contrast that feels like self-deprecation attracts.

Example 1 · Obvious

Mint Mobile vs. "big Wireless" — the named-category contrast appears throughout their entire ad library. Even their UGC creators say it: "you'll save huge compared to your current plan."

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

Liquid Death published one-star reviews in their own ads. They attacked the category expectation that water should look "pure." Negative reviews became proof of distinctiveness — the contrarian angle turned a liability into a brand asset.

Example 3 · Edge-case

A top-tier brand attempting Us vs. Them against a smaller rival backfires. The strategy legitimizes the rival by treating them as an equal. The rule: if you have to name them, you've already lost the framing war. Stick to "Us vs. the Alternative."

Module 03

The Reverse Value Equation

More good stuff, less bad stuff — across speed, ease, and risk. Twelve ad angles from one product.

A · Core The Four-Quadrant Value Engine

Value, in advertising math, is a four-quadrant equation. On the positive side: more good stuff — dream outcomes framed as fast, easy, and risk-free. On the negative side: less bad stuff — nightmare scenarios framed as slow, hard, and risky. The most compelling ads do both simultaneously.

Hormozi formalizes this as a "reverse value equation": maximize benefits, minimize costs, and explicitly sell against the don't — what happens if the prospect does nothing. If they do nothing, they keep experiencing more bad stuff and keep missing more good stuff. The four quadrants become: (a) more good stuff with us, (b) less bad stuff with us, (c) more bad stuff if you don't act, (d) less good stuff if you don't act.

Mint Mobile's UGC ads demonstrate the pattern. A reviewer says: "$15 a month, reliable, affordable" (more good stuff) and then immediately addresses the biggest fear — "I was worried the service would suck, but the service has been great" (less bad stuff). Critically, the fear addressed is the prospect's fear, not the brand's fear. Hormozi reads YouTube comments to find what prospects are actually worried about, then writes the next batch of ads to address those specific objections.

The four-quadrant model is also a generation system. Any ad can hit one of the four quadrants. Each quadrant can be expressed across three vectors — speed, ease, risk. That's 4 × 3 = 12 distinct ad angles from a single product. You never run out of creative. You just rotate quadrants and vectors until you find the combination that converts cheapest.

Example 1 · Obvious

Mint Mobile UGC reviewer — explicitly states the benefit ($15) and explicitly addresses the fear (service quality). Two quadrants in one ad.

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

Mint Mobile's landing page handles the coverage objection BEFORE pushing the offer. The zip-code checker is a value-equation tool — it reduces perceived risk before the prospect ever sees pricing. Removing bad stuff first makes the good stuff believable.

Example 3 · Edge-case

The "chunky milk" ad attempted contrast but delivered no positive value. Pure negative association without a positive anchor leaves the viewer with nothing to want. Less-bad-stuff only works if you've also established more-good-stuff in the same ad.

Module 04

Levels of Awareness

Match the hook to the audience's awareness layer. Mismatched hooks are the #1 cause of wasted ad spend.

A · Core Schwartz's Pyramid, Decoded

Eugene Schwartz's awareness pyramid segments any market into five layers: Unaware (don't know they have a problem), Problem Aware (know the pain, not the solutions), Solution Aware (know solutions exist, not your product), Product Aware (know your product, haven't bought), and Most Aware (know you, just need an offer).

The hook you write must match the layer you're targeting. Cold traffic at the Unaware level requires curiosity hooks: "Texas homeowners are getting reimbursement checks for up to X" or "5 weird foods that burn stomach fat." These ads say nothing about the product because the prospect isn't ready to hear about a product. The job is to provoke a single thought: "What is this?"

Most-Aware traffic — like Ryan Reynolds' warm audience — requires no curiosity hook at all. He simply says "Hey, it's Ryan Reynolds" and then makes the offer. The audience already knows him and trusts him; the only job is to deliver the deal. Hormozi emphasizes that Mint Mobile's Ryan Reynolds ads look like bad direct-response ads if you judge them by cold-traffic standards. They aren't bad — they're correctly matched to their layer.

Many businesses fail because they write Most-Aware hooks for Unaware traffic (no one clicks — the audience doesn't know who you are) or Unaware hooks for Most-Aware traffic (you waste attention building curiosity the audience already has). The right hook for the right layer is the single highest-leverage decision in ad creative. Hormozi notes that retargeting ads — which target the Most-Aware bucket — should use a completely different structure than top-of-funnel ads. Retargeting creative can be blunt: just restate the offer, because the audience has already self-selected by visiting your site.

Example 1 · Obvious

Ryan Reynolds opening with "Hey, it's Ryan Reynolds" — a Most-Aware hook for a warm audience. No curiosity needed; the recognition IS the hook.

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

"Texas homeowners getting reimbursement checks" — a curiosity hook for Unaware traffic. Says nothing about the product or even the problem. Pure pattern-interrupt for cold eyeballs.

Example 3 · Edge-case

A UGC creator opening with "PSA: if your grown kids sign their own phone plan, we're your man" — a Problem-Aware call-out. The hook filters for parents of adult children, surfacing the problem without naming the product yet. Narrow but precise.

Module 05

Offer Engineering

BOGO structures, three-tier pricing, urgency vs scarcity, and the "same offer, different wrapper" trick.

A · Core The Lever That Moves Everything Else

The offer is the lever; everything else is fulcrum. Hormozi breaks down four engineering decisions Mint Mobile makes repeatedly. First, BOGO structure. Mint Mobile runs "buy 3 months, get 3 months free." Hormozi argues asymmetric structures ("buy 1, get 2" or "buy 3, get 6") often outperform symmetric ones because the perceived gap between payment and reward widens. The prospect feels they're getting more free than they paid for — that asymmetry converts.

Second, three-tier pricing. Mint Mobile's plan page shows 3-, 6-, and 12-month options. The 6-month option functions as a "decoy" — its price is set close enough to the 12-month that the 12-month looks like the obvious upgrade. This is the small/medium/large soda play: how you space the middle tier determines which tier customers gravitate toward. If you want them at the 12-month, price the 6-month close to it. If you want them at the 3-month, price the 6-month close to the 3-month. The middle tier is a steering wheel.

Third, urgency vs scarcity. Urgency limits time ("offer ends soon"); scarcity limits quantity ("only 50 left"). Mint Mobile uses urgency (the offer has a clock) but not scarcity (the offer is unlimited — anyone can sign up). The "ends soon" phrasing is intentionally vague — subjective enough to be evergreen. "Soon" could mean tomorrow or next year; the prospect's imagination fills in the worst case.

Fourth, "same offer, different wrapper." Once an offer converts, you don't change it. You rewrap it. Holiday promo, spring promo, back-to-school promo, "my dog's birthday" promo — the offer is identical; the wrapper creates novelty. Hormozi compares this to fraternity party planning — invent any excuse, the party (offer) is what matters. Customers don't care about the reason; they care about the deal. But the reason creates permission to feel urgency about a deal they've seen before.

Example 1 · Obvious

Mint Mobile's "buy 3 get 3 free" — symmetric BOGO with the $45 upfront anchor. The $45 number shows up in every ad, every email, every landing page variant. They've found their number.

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

Asymmetric BOGO ("buy 1, get 2") — often converts better because the perceived free portion exceeds the paid portion. Hormozi explicitly says he'd test "buy 1 get 2" vs "buy 3 get 3" because the asymmetric version might win while costing the business less in free months.

Example 3 · Edge-case

A SaaS company running "ends soon" for 18 consecutive months — the urgency phrasing is intentionally subjective to stay evergreen. Legally grey, but mechanically effective. The prospect's imagination supplies the deadline.

▾

Once you find a winner, your job is to find new wrappers, not new offers. Inventing new offers resets your conversion data and forces you to relearn what works. Same offer, holiday wrapper. Same offer, spring wrapper. Same offer, "my dog's birthday" wrapper. The wrapper creates novelty; the offer creates revenue.

Module 06

The Conversion Stack

Ads acquire attention. The stack turns attention into revenue — through congruence, objection-handling, and follow-up.

A · Core Four Layers From Click to Cash

Ads acquire attention; the conversion stack turns attention into revenue. Mint Mobile's stack has four layers. Layer one: landing page congruence. Every ad pushes to mintmobile.com — the same URL, the same anchor price ($15/month), the same offer. Congruence means the click delivers what the ad promised. A mismatch between ad and landing page is the #1 silent killer of conversion. If the ad says $15 and the page opens at $30, the prospect bounces — not because $30 is too expensive, but because the bait didn't match the hook.

Layer two: urgency architecture. The page opens with a ticking timer and a "new customer offer" banner. Multiple CTAs appear above and below the fold. Hormozi notes that adding a second or third CTA button usually increases conversion — but only up to a point. Beyond three CTAs you create "referee confusion" — too many options, no clear action.

Layer three: objection handling via FAQ. The bottom of the page stacks FAQs. Each FAQ resolves a specific buying question: "Will it work in my area?", "Can I bring my family?", "What about SIM cards?" Hormozi notes that every FAQ represents a few percentage points of prospects who needed just one more answer. The zip-code coverage checker is a particularly clever micro-commitment — it resolves the #1 objection (coverage) before the prospect ever sees pricing.

Layer four: email follow-up. Non-buyers enter a sequence that restates the offer, then addresses one objection per email: "It's easy to switch," "Here's the SIM card situation," "Bring your family." Hormozi rates Mint Mobile's email sequence a 5/10 because it restates the offer without enough personalization or micro-commitments. The principle, though, is sound: each email handles one obstacle, in order of prevalence. The most common objection goes in email #1; the second most common in email #2; and so on.

Example 1 · Obvious

The Mint Mobile coverage-checker zip code tool — a micro-commitment that resolves the #1 objection before the prospect ever sees pricing. The prospect types their zip, the tool says "excellent coverage in your area," and the prospect proceeds with one less reason to bounce.

Example 2 · Counter-intuitive

Adding a step (intermediary landing page → sales page) sometimes increases conversion because it filters tire-kickers and warms the rest. More friction can mean more commitment. Mint Mobile uses this — a soft quiz-style landing page feeds into the harder sell.

Example 3 · Edge-case

Mint Mobile's three-tier plan page — the decoy middle tier nudges buyers toward the 12-month commitment, but the page also offers "renew later" risk-reversal for those who hesitate. The decoy pulls; the risk reversal catches. Both work in tandem.

07 · Retention

Memory techniques

Five mnemonics to make the playbook stick. Read each aloud once. Revisit before your next ad campaign.

H · M · C

Hook, Meat, CTA

The three-slice skeleton. Hook grabs, meat delivers value, CTA tells them what to do. Every ad. Every time. In that order.

U-TURN

Underdog = Them; Ruler = No-name

Use Us-vs-Them only when you're the underdog. If you're #1, attack the alternative (couch, DIY, doing nothing). Never legitimize a smaller rival.

SER

Speed · Ease · Risk

Three vectors for value. More good stuff (faster, easier, risk-free) and less bad stuff (not slow, not hard, not risky). Twelve ad angles from one product.

UPS-OM

Unaware → Problem → Solution → Product → Most-aware

The five awareness layers. Match the hook to the layer. Cold traffic needs curiosity; warm traffic needs an offer. Retargeting = just make the offer.

BOG-A

Asymmetric Beats Symmetric

"Buy 1 get 2" usually beats "buy 3 get 3." The perceived free portion must exceed the paid portion for maximum conversion pull.

SOW-DW

Same Offer, Different Wrapper

Once an offer wins, don't reinvent it. Rewrap it. Holiday promo, spring promo, dog-birthday promo. The excuse creates novelty; the offer creates revenue.

Cheat sheet

09 · Still confused?

FAQ

Paragraph-length answers to the questions most likely to be lingering.

If "Us vs Them" works for underdogs, what if I'm a mid-tier brand — neither #1 nor the smallest? ▾

Mid-tier brands have a strategic choice. You can attack the leader (risky — they have more resources to retaliate) or attack the category's inertia ("your current setup is fine but slow"). Hormozi's framework suggests attacking the alternative is usually safer for mid-tier players because it doesn't provoke a price war with the leader. The decision comes down to whether your differentiation is genuinely better than the leader on one axis (then attack) or simply different (then frame against the alternative).

"Asymmetric BOGO beats symmetric" — does this apply to services too, or just products? ▾

It applies to services, with a caveat. For services, "buy 1 get 2" usually means "pay for 1 month, get 2 months free" or "buy 1 hour, get 2 hours free." The asymmetry works the same way — the prospect perceives they're getting more value than they paid for. The caveat is that services with high marginal cost per unit (like 1-on-1 coaching) can't always afford the asymmetry. Software, subscriptions, and digital products can. Run the math on your unit economics first.

How do I find my audience's awareness layer without running expensive tests? ▾

Three free signals: (1) Search volume — if people search "[your brand] review," they're Product Aware. If they search "[your problem] solutions," they're Problem Aware. (2) YouTube comments on competitor ads — the questions they ask reveal their layer. (3) Your sales calls — the first three questions a prospect asks tell you what they already know. Most-aware prospects ask about pricing and timelines. Problem-aware prospects ask "how does this even work?" Pattern-match your hooks accordingly.

Mint Mobile uses Ryan Reynolds. I'm not famous. Does this playbook still work? ▾

Yes — with one adjustment. Reynolds can skip the curiosity hook because his face IS the curiosity hook for a warm audience. You can't do that. Your version: use UGC creators, micro-influencers, or customer testimonials as your "face." The awareness layer you target shifts from Most-Aware to Problem-Aware or Product-Aware, which means your hooks need more curiosity and education. But the skeleton (Hook → Meat → CTA), the value equation, the offer engineering, and the conversion stack all work identically. Hormozi explicitly notes that any influencer-business with 50k+ followers can run the same play; Reynolds' version just scales to everyone.

"Same offer, different wrapper" — isn't that deceptive if the offer never actually ends? ▾

It's a grey area legally and ethically. Mint Mobile uses "ends soon" rather than a hard date specifically because the offer doesn't actually end — it just gets rewrapped. From a compliance standpoint, subjective language ("soon," "limited time") is more defensible than hard deadlines you don't enforce. From an ethical standpoint, the offer IS real — customers do get the deal. The wrapper creates urgency; the deal delivers value. The line crossed is when the "urgency" is fabricated to pressure a decision the customer wouldn't otherwise make. Use with care, and consult a compliance professional if you're scaling.

How do I know if my decoy tier is actually steering purchases where I want? ▾

Run the test. Track what percentage of buyers choose each tier. If you want 70% on the large tier and you're getting 40%, your decoy isn't strong enough — move the middle price closer to the large. If you're getting 90% on large, your decoy is too strong (you're leaving money on the table from people who would've paid more for medium). The right split depends on your unit economics. Most businesses aim for 60–70% on the target tier, 20–25% on the decoy, and 10–15% on the entry tier.
