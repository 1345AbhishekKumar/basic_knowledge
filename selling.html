Module 1

The Flipped Funnel — AIDA vs. NEPQ

The model most salespeople inherit is older than they are. AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action — has been repackaged over the decades as “consultative selling,” but the shape never changed: 10% of the call on fake rapport (“how’s your day going?”), another 10% on surface-level questions, then 50% on presentation — features, benefits, why you’re the best — and the final 30% on closing and objection handling. Buy or die.

That shape produces the numbers-game mentality: call more, knock more doors, collect no’s to find a yes. It works just well enough to keep you in it — and caps you just hard enough to burn you out, because people weren’t built to be rejected by most of the humans they talk to. Selling has the highest attrition of any profession, and the model is the cause, not the people.

NEPQ flips the ratio. Roughly 85% of the conversation goes to engagement — questions that build trust and widen the gap between where they are and where they want to be. About 10% presents a solution they’ve already described wanting. And 2–5% is commitment — because the decision was really made during the engagement, not at the close.

Because pain and the fear of future pain are the only two things that make a human change. A presentation informs; a gap compels. If they feel no need to change, no closing technique on earth matters.

Step by step

  1. Audit where your minutes actually go on a typical call — most people are surprised.
  2. Move questioning from 20% of the conversation toward 85%.
  3. Let the gap — not your pitch — create the need to change.
  4. Present only what they told you they want, in their words.
  5. Treat the close as a scheduled next step, never a battle.

In the field

Example · 18 years in the trenches

Jeremy Miner sold for nearly 18 years across four industries — two B2B, two B2C — earning multi-seven-figure commissions. He rarely heard “can you do it cheaper?” or “your competitor is 5% less.” Not because his prices were low, but because his questions kept prospects in results-based thinking. People pay more when they trust you can get them the best result.

Decision point: a prospect mentions a cheaper competitor. Old instinct — defend your price. NEPQ — you never entered the price frame in the first place, so there is nothing to defend.

Example · Two ways to work a pipeline

The numbers-game rep closes 1 in 10 and grinds harder for the same result. The skills-game rep refines the questions, the tonality, the problem-finding — and closes 6 or 7 in 10. Less time on the phone, more commissions, and more people actually helped. Same leads; different conversations.

Decision point: when results plateau, the numbers game says “make more calls.” The skills game says “make better conversations.”

One more · The attrition statistic

Selling has the highest attrition of any profession in the world. The transcript’s claim: it isn’t the people who fail — it’s the model, which manufactures the very sales resistance and objections that eventually grind people down.

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Sales is a numbers game — just call more.” More calls through a broken model only caps you faster and burns you out. The conversion rate is the skill; the dial count is just fuel.

“You shouldn’t push — just let them decide.” The opposite. You must be seen as the trusted authority who moves the sale forward — you just do it in 2–5% of the conversation, because the decision was already made during the gap.

Why this mattersThe sale is decided in the engagement — so spend your minutes where the decision actually happens.

Module 2

Status & the First Twelve Seconds

Every society ranks people. A thousand years ago in Polynesia it was tribal tattoos; in medieval Europe, titles; today, followers and wealth. Salespeople default to the bottom of that ranking — you can out-earn a doctor two to one and still lose the status contest to the white coat and the degree on the wall. And yet nothing exists without an economy, and nothing moves without a sale. The person doing the selling starts every call at a disadvantage — unless they change the frame.

The verdict lands fast. Within 5 to 12 seconds of any conversation, the prospect’s subconscious has already read your tonality, pace, and body language. Come in too excited, too needy, too pushy — and you trigger fight-or-flight: the guard goes up, and every answer you get from then on stays vague and surface-level. Come in calm, neutral, almost unsure you can help yet — and the guard stays down while curiosity takes over.

The tools are small and deliberate: downplaying (“the first part of this call is pretty basic”), neutral language (“possible next steps” — nobody refuses to discuss possible next steps), and verbal pacing — slowing down and pausing so a question has time to land. Your tone is how the prospect interprets the intention behind every question you ask.

Because the subconscious verdict happens before your words land. You cannot argue someone out of a judgment they made in eight seconds — you can only avoid triggering it.

Step by step

  1. Arrive neutral, not excited — like an expert who already has plenty of business.
  2. Sound like you’re not yet sure you can even help — you don’t have enough detail.
  3. Downplay the structure: “the first part of this call is pretty basic.”
  4. Use neutral terms — “possible next steps,” never “I’ll show you how to get started.”
  5. Slow down and pause; fast questions get shallow answers.

In the field

Example · The white coat outranks the commission check

A salesperson earning $600,000 a year still ranks below a doctor earning half that — the degree on the wall and the white coat confer status without a word being claimed. NEPQ borrows the doctor’s signals: calm detachment, unhurried pace, and the posture of someone who diagnoses before they prescribe.

Decision point: the prospect is curt. Old instinct — turn up the warmth. NEPQ — turn down the neediness; status is quiet.

Example · “Can you see me?”

On a Zoom call with the prospect’s video off: “I’m a little behind schedule — can you hear me okay? … And can you see me?” They turn the camera on themselves, almost embarrassed, without ever being told to. On an outbound call, shuffling paper near the mic works as a pattern interrupt — curiosity about the sound replaces the reflex to hang up on “another salesperson.”

Decision point: you need their attention. Demand it and the guard rises; earn it with a small curiosity and it arrives on its own.

One more · The downplay, word for word

“The first part of this call is pretty basic — it’s really more for us to understand what you’re doing now and the results you’ve been getting, compared to where you’re wanting them to be, to see what that gap looks like. And then towards the end, if you feel like this might be what you’re looking for, we can go over some options and talk about possible next steps. Would that help you if we did that for you?”

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Enthusiasm sells.” Over-excitement reads as neediness, and neediness triggers fight-or-flight. You don’t have to be boring — you have to be unbiased, like someone who isn’t sure they can help yet.

“Status comes from income.” The doctor with the smaller paycheck still outranks you. Status in the call comes from situational signals — tone, pace, and how little you need the deal.

Why this mattersYour tone is how the prospect interprets the intention behind every question you ask.

Module 3

Connection & Situation Questions

Connection questions do three jobs in the first moments of a call: they take the focus off you and put it on the prospect; they move the conversation from price-and-cost thinking into results-based thinking; and they disarm — letting the guard down so answers stop being vague and generalized.

The formula is almost mechanical. Observe, neutrally, why they’re on the call — “it looks like you booked in about possibly getting help with your retirement planning” — attach the end result, “so you can get a higher rate of return” — then hand the microphone back: “what was it that caused you to want to look into this further?” They don’t just answer you; they tell themselves why they’re shopping. Whoever says it first believes it most.

Situation questions come next — factual questions about what they’re doing now, how long, what it costs. But watch the tonality. “What are they making you pay every month?” plants doubt without a single negative word. “That’s a fairly decent policy — we just don’t typically see a family your size on it” does the same. You never criticize the current provider; you let your tone imply you know something they don’t.

Because whoever talks first, sells first. When they say their own reasons out loud, you stop being a pitch and start being an answer.

Step by step

  1. Repeat back the problem they came with — neutrally, with “possibly.”
  2. Name the end result of what you do — this sets the results frame.
  3. Ask what caused them to look into it further; let them say their reasons.
  4. Gather the facts of their real situation: what, how long, what it costs.
  5. Seed doubt with tonality — never with words.

In the field

Example · Auto dealership, outbound lead

“It looks like you just responded to an ad this morning about possibly looking at that 2022 red A6 — we just got it in as a trade-in. What aspects of the car caused you to maybe want to test drive it?” Then, quietly building urgency that sounds believable: “And do you know what type of vehicle you might be looking for besides just the red Audi — just in case it’s already gone by the time you get down here?” No “lots of people are looking at it” — most prospects don’t believe that anyway.

Decision point: you want urgency. Fake urgency triggers skepticism; a plausible “just in case it’s gone” triggers imagination.

Example · HVAC, in-home appointment

“So it looks like you and Ryan scheduled about possibly upgrading your heating and cooling system — he mentioned getting the air circulating upstairs, so your kids are able to sleep better at night.” You are not selling heating and cooling systems. You are selling cooled upstairs air and sleeping children — the result of what the system does.

Decision point: the technician’s notes say “upstairs unit.” The homeowner’s reason says “my kids can’t sleep.” Sell the second one.

One more · SaaS for associations

“So it looks like you booked in with us to look at possible outside help with your awards program — and maybe being able to automate that process.” You’re not selling SaaS; you’re selling the end of a manual data grind. The prospect corrects or confirms — either way, you now know the real situation.

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Small talk builds rapport.” “How’s your day going?” is a predictable question the prospect has scripted in their brain. They read it as I’m trying to get you to like me so I can sell you something — and they treat you like every other salesperson. Lower status, guard up.

“Qualify with budget early.” Asking about budget in the first minutes locks the prospect into cost-based thinking — the exact frame you’re trying to escape.

Why this mattersIf you sound like every other salesperson, you’ll be treated like every other salesperson — lower status, no trust.

🔗 This connects to Module 4 because the facts you gather in situation questions are the raw material of the gap.

Module 4

Problem Awareness & the Gap

This is the heart of the model. Problem awareness questions build the gap between the prospect’s current state — where they actually are — and their objective state, where they want to be. Most prospects don’t truly understand their own situation: they know the surface problems, not the root causes, and certainly not what it costs them to leave those problems alone. You can’t build a gap to somewhere if nobody knows where “here” is.

The move that makes it work is a reversal. Instead of telling them why they should change — which goes in one ear and out the other — you ask questions that make them defend why they want to. “I mean, these are fairly decent cabinets… what’s causing you to feel like you might want to replace them though?” Now they’re arguing their own case. “It sounds like things are 100% perfect for you — what would you change if you could?” works because nobody is 100% happy with anything, and the framing makes it almost impossible not to confess the crack.

The gap has to be felt, not announced. Tension you state is pressure; tension they feel is motivation. And the problems must get personal — even with a Fortune 100 department head, the question eventually lands on how this affects them, not just the department.

Because tension you announce is pressure; tension they feel is motivation. The gap has to be theirs, or it isn’t a gap — it’s a lecture.

Step by step

  1. Anchor the current state in their own facts, not your assumptions.
  2. Probe gently: “what else would you change if you could?”
  3. Ask what’s causing them to feel they might want something different.
  4. Dig past the surface problem to the root cause — and its personal impact.
  5. Let them say the reasons for change out loud. Stop talking.

In the field

Example · Marketing agency, scaling pains

The prospect wants to scale to $10M a month and sits at $7M. “I mean, the vendor you’re using — they’re fairly decent — what’s making you feel like the leads they’re getting you aren’t going to be enough to really scale the business to ten million a month?” You plugged in their number, and now they’re explaining — to themselves — why the status quo can’t hold.

Decision point: they give a surface answer. Old instinct — move on to your deck. NEPQ — probe once more: “what else would you change though, if you could?”

Example · The “100% perfect” probe

“So it sounds like things are 100% perfect for you guys over there — what would you change if you could?” Psychologically, people resist claiming 100% of anything — the car they loved, the partner they adored, all developed a flaw within months. “Well, I wouldn’t say 100%…” — and the real pain walks out on its own.

Decision point: they insist everything is fine. Argue and you lose; add “100%” and they argue with themselves instead.

One more · Health insurance network

“When you get ready to go to the doctor, do you pick whoever you want — or are you forced to only use the doctors on the company’s list? Oh — so they’re making you go just to the doctors on their list, though…” The word forced does quiet work: nobody likes being made to do anything, and the prospect now feels the constraint they’d been living with.

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Tell them why they should change.” Told reasons go in one ear and out the other. Reasons they say out loud are ten times more persuasive — because the most credible voice in the room, to them, is their own.

“Every prospect in my industry has the same situation.” Guessing — “throwing mud against the wall, hoping something sticks” — is how you present to a problem they don’t actually have. Hope is a drug, and an unpredictable way to make a living.

Why this mattersPeople buy on emotion and justify with logic. The gap is where the emotion is generated — by them, not you.

🔗 This connects to Module 5 because once the gap exists, they’re ready to face what the past cost them and what the future demands.

Module 5

Solution Awareness & Consequence

Solution awareness questions run in two directions. Backward first: what have they already done about these newly discovered problems — and what held them back? This gets them questioning their own thinking and hands you the blueprint of every future objection. Then forward: what does the future look like once the problems are solved?

Future pacing starts logical and drills to emotional. “What would you be able to do that you feel you can’t do right now?” gets a list — play basketball with my son, go jogging again. Then go deeper: “But on your end — what would that do for you personally?” And deeper still: “How would your life be different than it is now?” That last question is where the emotion lives, and emotion is where decisions are made.

Then, while they’re standing in that good future, you turn the light on the alternative. Consequence questions get the prospect to defend, out loud, why they have to change — now. “What happens if you don’t do anything about this… and it gets worse?” Start the sentence in a challenging tone to trigger the emotion, and land it in a concerned tone so they know you’re for them. Pain and the fear of future pain are the only two drivers of human change; these questions make both impossible to ignore.

Because a problem that costs nothing to ignore will be ignored — forever. Consequence questions put a price tag on doing nothing.

Step by step

  1. Ask what they’ve already tried — and what held them back from it working.
  2. Future-pace: “if we were able to… what would you be able to do that you can’t now?”
  3. Drill down: “what would that do for you personally?” then “how would life be different?”
  4. Pivot: “You certainly sound motivated — but what if you don’t do anything about this…?”
  5. Land the consequence in a concerned tone. Verbal-pace long questions with pauses.

In the field

Example · Weight loss, 105 pounds

“Let’s say we were able to help you lose the 105 pounds — and more importantly keep it off so it doesn’t come back. What would you be able to do that you feel you can’t do right now?” — “Play basketball with my son. Go jogging like I used to.” That’s the logical list. Then, tone lowered into concern: “But how would it be different though — you being able to spend that time with your family… how would your life be different than it is now?” That’s where the emotion comes out.

Decision point: they give you logistics. Old instinct — move on. NEPQ — ask one layer deeper, and the real reason surfaces.

Example · Dental implants, challenging → concerned

“What happens, John — and you certainly sound motivated — but what happens if you don’t do anything about this… and you keep losing the bone density in your jaw… and now you can’t even get the implants?” The opening edge challenges them to defend; the landing tone says I’m concerned for you. If they soften — “I just hope that won’t happen” — you lean in: “Do you want to keep living with that pain in your mouth, if you didn’t have to?”

Decision point: they shrug. Old instinct — push harder. NEPQ — lower your tone into concern and let the silence carry the weight.

One more · Solar, and why you must verbal-pace

“What happens if you don’t do anything about this — and Edison keeps raising your rates every year like they always have — but now you’re 75, on a fixed income — and the bill is three or four times higher — how would you guys pay for it at that point?” A question that long must be paced with pauses. Said fast, it’s noise; paced out, the prospect hangs on every word and their brain literally thinks deeper. Fast questions are why so many answers come back vague.

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Stay positive — don’t dwell on the negative.” If the prospect can’t relive their pain or fear its future, they feel no need to change — and no need to change is the factory that produces every objection you hate.

“Consequence questions work any time.” Asked in the first two minutes, before trust or gap, they sound like a scare tactic. They only land three-quarters of the way in, after the prospect has emotionally opened up.

Why this mattersPain and the fear of future pain are the only two drivers of change — consequence questions make both impossible to ignore.

Module 6

Commitment — Letting Them Close Themselves

If the first 85% did its job, commitment is 2–5% of the process — and it’s not a battle, it’s a question. In a one-call close you transition to the presentation and then commit; in longer cycles you commit to the micro-commitment — the demo, the proposal, the meeting with legal. Either way, something goes on the calendar. “Let me know if you’re interested” is not a model; it’s a surrender.

The key question is deliberately emotional: “Do you feel like this could be the answer for you?” — never “do you think,” which hands them back to logic and invites think-it-over objections. Then pause. They’ll either say “I really do” — in which case you push back: “Hold on — why do you feel like it is?” — or “I do, but…,” in which case the real concern finally surfaces where you can work with it.

Every push is an invitation to self-sell. “What specific aspects of what we covered do you feel will help you the most?” “Why look at doing this now — why not just push it down the road like a lot of companies do?” Each time, they argue their own case out loud — and a prospect selling themselves is ten times more persuasive than you ever will be. Then you simply state the next step, plainly, and ask: “Would that be appropriate?”

Because your prospect trusts their own voice ten times more than yours. Your job was never to convince them — it’s to hand them the microphone.

Step by step

  1. Transition only after the gap is built — never before trust exists.
  2. “Do you feel like this could be the answer?” — then stop talking.
  3. Push back on their yes: “hold on — why do you feel like it is?”
  4. Ask which specific aspects will help them most; let them list the value.
  5. State the next step plainly and ask, “Would that be appropriate?”

In the field

Example · Final expense insurance

Three options on the table: $10K, $20K, $30K funeral coverage. “Which one of those would you possibly lean more towards?” — “I’d say option two.” — “I thought you’d say option one.” Now they defend option two — to themselves. Then lean in: “Do you feel like this is what you’re looking for, to really take the burden off your kids so they’re not responsible for those expenses when you pass away?” And simply: “The next step would be to make sure you’re eligible — I’ll get that pulled up.”

Decision point: they pick the middle option. Old instinct — upsell. NEPQ — pretend to expect less, and let them sell themselves up.

Example · B2B culture consulting, the push-away

They’ve listed why they like the program. Now push away: “Well, why look at doing this now? I mean, you certainly sound motivated — but why not just push it down the road, like a lot of companies do who end up losing their best people?” — “Well, the reason we have to do it now is…” They are now defending urgency. You never manufactured it; you only asked them to argue against it, and they refused.

Decision point: momentum is high. Old instinct — strike while the iron’s hot and rush the paperwork. NEPQ — make them defend the timing, then state the next step calmly.

One more · Real estate listing

“Do you feel like this marketing plan is going to be the answer for you guys?” — “Oh, for sure.” — “Hold on — why do you feel like it is?” After they list their reasons: “I don’t really have anything else to go over — it looks like we covered the basis of what you’re looking for to sell the property quickly. The next step would be to fill out the listing agreement, arrange for the photographer, and start listing. Would that be appropriate?”

❌ What People Get Wrong

“Closing is a battle you win at the end.” The decision was already made during the engagement. The commitment question doesn’t create the decision — it surfaces it, along with any real concern still hiding behind “I need to think about it.”

“‘Do you think…’ means the same as ‘do you feel…’.” “Think” moves the brain from the emotional side to the logical side — and logic manufactures think-it-over and do-more-research objections. “Feel” keeps them where the decision lives.

Why this mattersYour prospect is ten times more persuasive than you will ever be — the whole craft is handing them the microphone.

FAQ

Isn’t this manipulative?

NEPQ surfaces problems that genuinely exist and asks permission-based questions at every step — nothing is invented. The alternative — pitching features nobody asked for and pressuring at the end — is the approach that actually treats people as numbers.

Does it work on cold calls and door-to-door?

Yes. The structure is identical; only the connection question changes. On outbound contact it references why you’re calling (“you responded to an ad…”) instead of a booking, and a small pattern interrupt earns the first ten seconds.

What if prospects give one-word answers?

That’s usually a tonality problem, not a question problem. Something in your pace or tone triggered the guard. Slow down, drop into concern, and ask one layer deeper: “what makes you say that?” Surface answers are a symptom, not a personality trait.

How long until it feels natural?

Script three questions per type for your industry, then drill them on real calls for two to four weeks. The words feel awkward for about a week; the tonality takes longer — record yourself, because you can’t hear your own neediness in the moment.

Do objections disappear completely?

No — and the transcript doesn’t claim that. But if you prevent even half of the objections you currently get, your numbers change by default. Most objections are simply the sound of a prospect who felt no need to change.

Do I still need to close?

Yes. You can’t end timidly — you must be seen as the expert who moves the sale forward. You just do it in 2–5% of the conversation, with a commitment question instead of a closing battle, and always with a next step on the calendar.

Next steps

  • Write three connection questions and three situation questions for your exact industry — in your words, not generic ones.
  • Record your next three calls. Grade one thing only: did you sound needy, or neutral?
  • For one week, replace every “how are you today?” with a connection question. Notice how the answers change.
  • Practice the consequence tone arc out loud — challenging to concerned — until it stops feeling strange.
  • Print or pin the cheat sheet where you take calls. Glance at it before every conversation for the first month.
