If I started today

Ten things a 15-year software builder would do differently now — rebuilt here as ten short chapters you can open one at a time, plus a set of recall cards at the end so the ideas actually stick.

01

Disruption is fast. Adapting is slow.

+

Being "on track" early in your career doesn't stay true on its own — if you're not tracking new capabilities and new ways of working, you quietly drift from the path that's actually current. This is why a senior engineer with 15 years of experience can lose out to a junior candidate: the junior spent their (much shorter) time on exactly what's in demand right now, while the senior accumulated depth in things the market has moved past.

Total years of experience isn't the variable that matters — the relevance of that experience is. Picture two skill bars: a veteran's bar is almost entirely made of years of general experience, with only a sliver devoted to whatever's currently disrupting the field (say, AI). A total beginner's bar is small overall, but nearly all of it is spent on exactly that disrupting trend. If a job posting is specifically looking for AI capability, the beginner's smaller total skill can still beat the veteran's larger total skill, because it's aimed at the right target.

This plays out as a real, recurring email exchange: someone brand new asks how to learn as fast as possible, gets told "pair fundamentals with AI-augmented learning," and reacts with something close to alarm — "using AI is going to slow down my learning" — then goes back to studying entirely solo, the old-school way. That reaction is exactly the mindset this whole talk is arguing against.

The framing for veterans is different: you don't need to abandon your 15 years, you just need to spend a bit of extra deliberate time closing the gap on whatever's new. For beginners, the same asymmetry is an advantage — you can point 100% of a smaller effort straight at what's currently in demand, something a 15-year veteran can't cleanly do without unwinding old habits first.

Rule: if you can influence how fast you personally adapt, you're already ahead of most of the field.
02

Fundamentals and AI aren't opposites

+

A common trap for beginners: refusing to touch AI tools until they're "senior enough," believing it will weaken their foundation. That's a false choice. The actual answer is to learn fundamentals and pair that learning with AI from day one — not have AI write everything, and not avoid it either.

There are times to write code by hand, times to let an agent write it, and times to use an agent purely to deepen your own understanding — architecture decisions, bug hunts, security review. Used this way, AI doesn't make your software worse; it makes it better, and it compresses years of self-study into months. The concrete claim from the talk: roughly six months of daily agent use produced learning comparable to a couple of years of solo study.

The warning attached to this: none of it works if you become "just a vibe coder" who has agents write everything with no understanding of why. The goal is AI-enhanced software engineering — you still learn to code and understand fundamentals, you just don't quarantine AI use until some arbitrary "senior" milestone before you're allowed to touch it.

Rule: don't split "learn the fundamentals" and "use AI" into a sequence — run them in parallel.
03

Borrow someone else's 20 years

+

There's a category of judgment — knowing which architectural decision will hurt you in production, which pattern only looks fine on a whiteboard — that neither solo practice nor AI can hand you. It only comes from someone who has already lived through the mistake. The fastest way to acquire it is to find that person and learn directly from them, even when the process is uncomfortable (being told to rewrite something from scratch, repeatedly, is annoying in the moment and formative in hindsight).

The concrete example given: early in a first job, pair programming with a much more experienced developer who kept rejecting finished work — "no, rewrite it from scratch, do it this way" — which felt irritating and slow in the moment ("dude, I just want to get this app built"). Looking back, that friction was the single most valuable stretch of the whole early career, because it was the first time the why behind certain patterns actually landed, not just the how.

If you can't find a mentor yet, that's usually a sign you haven't tried hard enough — network on LinkedIn, message people, show up. One concrete low-effort habit: whenever a book genuinely helps, message the author directly on LinkedIn to say so. Several authors have responded warmly to exactly that — even ones writing in completely unrelated genres (romance novels, in one example) — because a specific, genuine message stands out. Person-to-person contact is still the single highest-value channel for growing skill, getting opportunities, and understanding "why," not just "how."

Rule: find someone ahead of you in whatever you're trying to do, from day one — don't wait until you feel "ready" to talk to them.
04

Chase applied experience, not more tutorials

+

Basic how-to tutorials are becoming commoditized — an LLM can already explain most of what a tutorial used to teach. What an LLM can't hand you is someone else's lived experience: an engineering blog explaining exactly why a company chose a specific database architecture, or a niche podcast where practitioners talk through their actual discoveries and failures. That's the material that teaches judgment, not just syntax.

Two concrete examples from the talk: a "Zero Knowledge" podcast, where every guest was actively working in cryptography — hearing how they reasoned through problems, what they discovered, and what tripped them up made it possible to separate what actually mattered from what didn't, far faster than solo study could. Separately, a Discord engineering blog post explaining their move to a NoSQL database structure was more valuable than any generic "SQL vs NoSQL" tutorial, because it explained the specific why behind the choice — which is exactly the part beginners struggle with when they're staring at a wall of database options with no sense of which tradeoffs matter.

Coins vs. the treasure chestTutorial-hopping and asking AI one-off "how do I" questions collects small coins — real, but slow, and they take forever to add up to real skill. The treasure chest — deep applied experience from people who've actually built the thing — sits right in front of most people, who walk past it chasing more coins instead.

05

Build a foundation, then a top hat, not a plateau

+

Two failure modes: spreading effort evenly across everything (you end up average at all of it, exceptional at none of it), or topic-hopping without ever specializing. The fix is a shape, not a rule — a broad general foundation across non-overlapping fundamentals (networking, databases, back end, low-level programming), topped by real depth in a small number of things you actually specialize in.

Courses are underrated for the foundation layer specifically because they force an end-to-end path instead of scattered self-directed research — you get 80% of the benefit of a topic just from following one good course through to the end. Concrete example: learning React for the first time by watching a single ~4-hour crash course start to finish in one sitting. That didn't mean knowing how to build everything in React afterward — but it gave a working mental model and a video to anchor back to, which was enough of a foundation to build depth from later.

The named list of foundational areas worth scratching the surface of: networking, databases, back-end development, desktop applications, and low-level programming. None of these need to be mastered up front — the point is broad exposure, not depth, at this layer.

Rule: scratch the surface of many fundamentals, but pick a small number of things to actually go deep on.
06

Learn the diff, not the same thing twice

+

Learning "loops" in Python and then re-learning "loops" in JavaScript feels productive but mostly teaches syntax translation — a low-value skill you can pick up on demand with documentation or AI. The higher-value move is going deep in one language until you hit genuinely new territory — generators, object-oriented design, async, concurrency — and only then learning the differences between that language and a new one, rather than re-learning everything from zero.

The counter-example is the early self-taught period this talk warns against: studying jQuery one day, jumping to Angular the next, then to something unrelated like Google Apps Script automation — chasing whatever felt interesting that day, never staying anywhere long enough to hit genuinely new territory. It felt like learning; the actual efficiency was poor, because every jump mostly re-covered ground already known in a different syntax.

The positive version of the same instinct: in a university Java class, having already learned C, the two languages were treated as close enough that code was written in C first and then minimally "Java-ified" — translating only the real differences rather than re-learning the same concepts from scratch.

This applies directly to AI tools too: sampling five different AI coding tools at a shallow level (being an "AI power user" who's never actually written a custom skill or sub-agent) is weaker than getting genuinely good at one and transferring that depth elsewhere later.

Rule: go deep in one thing until it's uncomfortable, then translate — don't reset to zero in a new tool or language just to feel busy.
07

Turn AI into a learning instrument, not an autocomplete

+

Concrete practices worth adopting: keep a knowledge vault (a folder of notes AI helps you structure and correct as you study); write your own reusable "skills" — saved prompts you invoke like functions — for things like researching how a concept translates across languages, or generating alternative design approaches to the same problem so you see it from more than one angle.

The single highest-value use: asking AI to surface alternatives you wouldn't have known to look for, instead of accepting the first isolated solution as "the way it's done." Concrete example given — object-oriented programming: many learners never realize some languages are functional-first instead, some are trait/interface-based with no formal classes at all, some support multiple inheritance and some deliberately don't, and even within a language that supports OOP (Java forces it; Python doesn't), it's worth asking whether OOP is even the right architecture for the problem at hand. An AI "design alternatives" skill can be built specifically to surface that kind of cross-paradigm comparison on demand.

Second concrete example — concurrency: given three tasks, there are meaningfully different ways to structure them: run them serially one-by-one then report "done"; run them in parallel but block until all finish before reporting "done"; or fire them off asynchronously, continue immediately, and let "done" arrive whenever the last task actually finishes. Rather than learning one isolated pattern, asking AI for real-world examples of when each shape is the right call — and the further-down choice between OS-managed threads, lightweight runtime-managed threads, or async/await — turns a single technique into an understood decision space.

It can also generate runnable examples for things documentation just asserts (like what actually happens on integer overflow), turning "trust and move on" into "see it happen and understand it." The same "fan out multiple variations to see outside your current view" approach applies to real project work too — generating several different front-end implementations in parallel rather than committing to the first one an agent produces.

Rule: use AI to multiply the number of angles you see a problem from, not just to get one answer faster.
08

Build something real, and keep it running

+

Toy projects abandoned after a day teach very little. A project worked on continuously, with a domain, real branding, and ideally real users, forces you to face the problems companies actually care about: production data, GDPR-style constraints, latency, and live schema migrations that can't corrupt existing data or break users mid-flight. Those skills are close to impossible to fake, and far more valuable than another local prototype.

Real, sustained projects also build product thinking — what features are worth building, what people will actually pay for — which is exactly the layer AI is still weak at: gluing pieces together into something a real user wants.

The concrete story: building a cryptocurrency from scratch in Rust — nicknamed, half-jokingly, "Currycoin" — specifically to learn a new language while forcing engagement with real hard problems: how a wallet actually works, how the encryption works, how a transaction is sent, building a CLI to interact with it. It never got far — within about a week of starting, a company doing (unrelated, non-crypto) Rust work reached out with a job offer, likely influenced by having something more substantial than an afternoon project to point to. Depth on one meaningfully hard project outweighed a portfolio of shallow finished ones.

Rule: treat your side project like a real company would — domain, branding, real users — not a subdomain that never leaves localhost.
09

Resourcefulness beats the degree

+

Self-imposed limits ("I'm not able to do X") become the actual ceiling on what you achieve. The most valuable single trait to cultivate is resourcefulness — the ability to figure something out, whether alone, with AI, or through other people — because a university pipeline can quietly train the opposite: everything handed to you, no need to push through discomfort yourself.

The claim that "AI is destroying the entry-level market" is only half the story — the deeper issue is that many graduates arrive without real-world project experience regardless of AI, because the degree pipeline itself hasn't adapted (see chapter 1: systems lag behind disruption too). A blunt hiring-side comment on this: someone who leads with "I have a bachelor's degree" and nothing else invites the assumption their strongest skill is socializing, not coding — because so many degree-holders genuinely can't code well on graduation.

The classroom example given: sitting next to a classmate who spent lecture time watching Twitch streams — paying to be there while checking out entirely. That same low-effort pattern, transplanted into today's environment, would still fail; the difference is it's now easier to blame "the market" or "AI" instead of the actual lack of discipline behind it.

Rule: weigh a degree on its actual return, and build real-world skill in parallel with it either way — that self-driven work is what a diploma alone doesn't give you.
10

Three career moves worth making early

+

Detach identity from title. A few years back, during a hiring surge at the big-name "FAANG"-type companies, not landing one of those specific roles felt close to failure. In practice there are thousands of companies and far more roles than "software engineer" alone — developer relations (helping companies integrate technical products), leading technical workshops, technical marketing, and eventually a full-time role built around making technical videos (an offer around $180k base, roughly $240k total) all counted as equally legitimate paths, some more fitting than straight engineering ever was.

Break out of your network. You don't know what you don't know — proximity to people already doing what you want resets your sense of what's normal (pay, opportunity, standards) faster than years of solo study. Concrete story: co-founding a company and working alongside engineers who'd come from top-paying roles, hearing salary numbers for the first time that seemed almost unbelievable — coming from a background (rural Ohio, corn fields, the factory as the "good job" locally) where those numbers simply weren't part of the visible ceiling. That exposure alone was enough to start pushing for higher pay elsewhere, because the ceiling had visibly moved.

Practice discomfort deliberately. A specific pricing story: charging $500 for a service, working up the nerve to try 3x'ing it to $1,500 — feeling real anxiety submitting that number — only for the client to accept instantly without blinking. That happened repeatedly over roughly a month or two, adding up to about $28,000, a huge sum right out of high school. The real lesson wasn't "push through discomfort once" — it's that repeated exposure expands what counts as comfortable, to the point that the old $1,500 rate now looks irrationally low for the same effort.

And as AI automates more of the impersonal layer — resumes, cover letters, first-pass filtering — genuine human-to-human interaction becomes scarcer and therefore more valuable, not less. Automate what should be automated; go more human, not less, wherever a real relationship is the actual point.

Rule: optimize for resourcefulness, proximity to people ahead of you, and comfort with discomfort — title and pedigree matter less than all three.
