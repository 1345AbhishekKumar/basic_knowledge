01
⚡ The meta-skill · ~6 min

Adapt or Diverge

A · Core Explanation

Here is the frame for everything else in this lesson: disruption moves very fast, and adaptation is typically slow. When you're early in your career you might be genuinely on track — but if you're not keeping up with trends, new capabilities, and new approaches to work, you slowly diverge from the proper path. The danger is that the divergence is invisible day to day. It only becomes visible at layoff time.

This is why someone with 10, 15, 30 years of experience can be laid off and lose interviews to entry-level engineers: those years don't automatically contain the experience that matters today. Calendar time and relevant skill are different currencies. The newcomer in the transcript competes with less total skill — but the right slice of it.

The lever you control: the speed at which you adapt. Disruption you can't slow down; your adaptation rate you can raise. Treat adaptation as a skill you train, not weather you endure.

Because a role pays for the slice of your skill set it can use. If a company is hiring for AI capabilities — using LLMs, shipping AI features — your 15 years of unrelated depth simply don't get counted. The market prices relevant surface area, not total volume.
B · Step-by-Step
  1. Treat "on track" as a decaying state. Being current is a snapshot, not a status. It expires on a schedule you don't control.
  2. Schedule trend scans. Weekly time boxed to: what's new in tools, capabilities, and workflows — even (especially) things that threaten your current stack.
  3. Allocate deliberate hours to what's in demand now. Not everything — but a fixed slice of every week goes to the disrupting force.
  4. Measure adaptation speed, not years. Ask monthly: "What can I do now that I couldn't do 90 days ago?"
C · Examples
✅ The obvious one

The laid-off senior

A senior engineer with decades on the resume gets laid off and struggles against entry-level candidates — because those juniors spent their (shorter) careers focused on the stuff that's in demand right now.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one

The newcomer wins with less total skill

A brand-new developer with a smaller overall skill set — but the majority of it aimed at the disrupting technology — beats the veteran for an AI-capability role. Smaller pie, bigger relevant slice.

D · Visual Analogy

🧭 The concept: silent divergence

Two people start at the same point. One keeps correcting course; the other holds the original heading. Nothing dramatic happens for years — then the gap is a canyon.

🏃 The analogy: the Red Queen's race

In Through the Looking-Glass, the Red Queen tells Alice: "It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Your effort feels like standing still — because the landscape moves under your feet.

🧠 Why it works: both pictures share the same hidden variable — a moving reference frame. On a treadmill, "running hard" and "going nowhere" are the same event. In a career, "working hard on last decade's stack" and "falling behind" are the same event. The analogy makes the invisible drift feel physical.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: years of experience = current expertise.

Truth: it can be one year of experience, repeated fifteen times.

Reasoning: experience only compounds if you keep updating the inputs. Otherwise it fossilizes — and the market quietly reprices you while your resume stays the same length.

Myth: being on track is a durable status.

Truth: it's a snapshot — and the track itself is moving.

Reasoning: "on track" measures you against today's map. Disruption redraws the map on its own schedule, so the only stable position is one that includes regular correction.

F · Why This Matters
💚 This is the frame for the entire lesson. Every skill below — AI, mentors, depth, shipping — only pays off if your adaptation engine is running. Adaptation speed is the one meta-skill that keeps all the others current.

🤖 The refusal trap · ~8 min

AI Is Your Tutor, Not Your Crutch

A · Core Explanation

The author gets near-daily emails: "What should I do to learn as fast as humanly possible?" — and when he answers "use AI agents to enhance your progress," the reply is often: "Whoa, I don't want to use AI, it'll slow down my learning." His reaction: you asked what I'd do in your position, then refused the answer. The belief that AI "destroys your foundation" isn't a property of the tool — it's a flaw in the approach to the tool.

The correct stance is both/and. Should you know how to code? Yes. Should you understand the fundamentals? Absolutely — that's where you start. But pair it with AI-enhanced learning. There are times you write everything by hand; times you let agents write code; and times you use agents purely to deepen understanding without letting them touch the code. Splitting these into "first learn, then use AI" forces you into the structure of 20-year veterans who are failing to adapt.

The evidence he offers: after using agents daily for six months, his learning was comparable to a couple of years of solo study. Used correctly, agents don't make software worse — they improve system design, architecture, bug-hunting, and security. The goal is AI-enhanced software engineering, not vibe coding (letting AI write everything while understanding nothing).

B · Step-by-Step — The AI Learning Workflow
  1. Build a knowledge vault. A folder of markdown notes about your project or study. AI helps structure your thoughts and flags incorrect claims. It becomes shared reference for you and the agents.
  2. Write reusable AI "skills." A skill is a prompt you define once and invoke like a function: research, code-translation, design-alternatives. The alternatives skill is the most valuable — more on that below.
  3. Demand runnable examples. Don't accept stated facts — have AI generate a quick example of overflow, rounding, or whatever the docs assert, then run it and watch it happen.
  4. Fan out agents. Spawn multiple agents for multiple approaches in parallel — it injects randomness and shows you the space of solutions, not one siloed answer.
  5. Turn AI into your examiner. Have it quiz you, review your notes for flaws, and write failing tests that you must implement to pass.
Because the biggest failure of modern learning is siloed concepts. Studying OOP, you may never learn that functional patterns can replace many OOP design patterns, that trait-based languages skip formal classes, or that some languages allow multiple inheritance and others don't. An alternatives skill attacks the same topic from every angle — it can even challenge the category: "do we need OOP here at all?" That breadth used to require years of exposure. Now it's one prompt.
C · Examples
✅ The obvious one — concurrency

Three tasks, three architectures

Learning concurrency? Don't memorize one pattern. Have AI generate runnable code for: serial (1→2→3→"done"), parallel-wait (all finish → "done"), and fire-and-forget ("done" now, tasks finish later) — plus real-world cases for choosing each, and the trade-offs of runtime light-threads vs OS threads vs async/await.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one — attack the category

"Do we even need OOP?"

While studying object-oriented programming, his alternatives skill surfaces functional replacements, trait-based designs without classes, and multiple-inheritance differences — then questions whether OOP is needed at all in a language like Python. It breaks the "this is how it's always done" illusion.

D · Visual Analogy

🏋️ The concept: fundamentals + AI together

You still write code, still learn fundamentals — but an always-available reviewer corrects your form in real time and shows you angles you'd never find alone.

🥊 The analogy: the sparring partner

A coach never fights for you. They throw controlled challenges at exactly your level, point out the telegraphed jab you can't see yourself, and make you repeat it right. Refusing the coach to "protect your fundamentals" is… not how anyone gets good at boxing.

🧠 Why it works: in both cases the effort stays yours — what multiplies is feedback density. Six months of daily corrected reps beats two years of uncorrected ones. That's the whole arithmetic of AI-enhanced learning.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: AI and strong fundamentals are enemies — pick one.

Truth: a bad approach to AI slows learning. Used as examiner, example-generator, and reviewer, it accelerates understanding — the author's own six months ≈ years of solo study.

Reasoning: he admits he held this exact belief first ("I'm good with what I have"). The shift wasn't the tools — it was forcing himself to use them in every way possible, at high quality.

Myth: AI is a reward for the experienced.

Truth: waiting "makes zero sense" — you're voluntarily adopting the structure of 20-year veterans who are failing to adapt to current workflows.

Reasoning: the people succeeding right now mix hand-written code, agent-written code, and agent-assisted understanding — situationally. Copying the winners' mix beats copying the losers' purity.

F · Why This Matters
💚 This is the wedge that separates you from every beginner saying "I'm not using AI." While they protect an imaginary purity, you compound — same hours, radically more learning per hour.

03
🧭 Collapse time · ~7 min

Borrow Decades: Learn From People Ahead of You

A · Core Explanation

If he restarted today, he'd find a mentor — someone with far more experience — on day one. When he started, he was alone: wasting hours searching the internet and YouTube ("back in my day I wrote my code by hand — I feel like a boomer"). His verdict: there is something AI and solo practice cannot replace — the experience that takes years and years to develop. When you're the little guy trying to operate beyond your skill set (which is exactly where learning happens), the best move is to withdraw from the skills of others.

No mentor yet? "You probably haven't tried very hard." LinkedIn, Reddit, communities — peer-to-peer communication is still the biggest value for skill, network, jobs, and opportunities. The fallback tier is applied-experience content: engineering blogs and practitioner podcasts — content about why decisions were made in production, not tutorials. His claim: the basic tutorial era is ending, because anything you'd once look up, an LLM can now explain with examples. What an LLM has never lived — "we chose this database because…" — is where the remaining value sits.

His image for it: tutorial-hopping collects coins — tiny findings that take forever to add up — while the treasure chest of a few sentences from an experienced person sits ignored right in front of you. And his pair-programming story proves it: the senior who forced him to rewrite everything was infuriating in the moment — and in hindsight, the most helpful period of his journey, because he finally learned why.

B · Step-by-Step
  1. Find someone 2–20 years ahead of you — day one, not someday. The goal is to collapse time.
  2. Check important architectural decisions against experienced people. AI can sanity-check; humans can say "I've watched that burn down."
  3. Swap tutorial hours for applied-experience content. Engineering blogs, practitioner podcasts — the "here's what happened when…" genre.
  4. When someone makes you rewrite it, ask for the why. The rewrite is the fee; the reasoning is the product.
  5. Message creators after consuming their work. "Hey, I read your book. Good stuff." — it works shockingly often (he's gotten replies from total strangers… including romance novelists).

📡 Where applied experience lives (chunked — expand for more)

  • 🎙 Practitioner podcasts (e.g., the Zero Knowledge Podcast — every guest was doing cryptography in the field)
  • 📝 Company engineering blogs (e.g., Discord's NoSQL article — the why behind a database choice)
  • 💬 Communities & forums where builders share production scars
  • 🤝 Pair programming / code review from seniors
  • 📚 Books — then message the author about them
  • 🏗 Open-source maintainers' postmortems and RFCs
  • 🎤 Conference talks focused on war stories, not syntax
C · Examples
✅ The obvious ones

Zero Knowledge & Discord's NoSQL article

The podcast gave him practitioners' thinking, discoveries, and blind spots — he learned what was valuable and what wasn't, fast. The Discord article showed why they needed that database — and "why choose one approach over another" is the true skill, nearly impossible to learn alone as a beginner.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one

He could teach database design at 16 — and couldn't warn you

He could articulate structures for a store or a social network. What he couldn't say: "you never want to do this in production, because I did that once and this happened." Concepts can be learned young; scar tissue cannot. That gap is exactly what borrowing experience fills.

D · Visual Analogy

🪙 The concept: coins vs. the treasure chest

Each tutorial yields a coin — real, but tiny. Collecting them forever never sums to wealth, while the chest of veteran insight sits unopened because it doesn't feel like collecting.

🏦 The analogy: a 30-year savings account

A mentor has been depositing hard-won lessons for decades. One honest conversation lets you withdraw more than you could earn coin-by-coin in years. Compound interest, but for judgment.

🧠 Why it works: both frames expose the same error — optimizing for volume of input instead of value per minute. Coins feel like progress because counting is easy. Chests feel intimidating because they require a conversation. The math says otherwise.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: information access equals experience access.

Truth: AI can explain anything it has never lived. Production judgment — "never do X, because it corrupts data at 3 a.m." — only exists in people.

Reasoning: his own 16-year-old database lectures prove it: flawless concepts, zero scars. You can either earn the scars over a decade or borrow them over coffee.

Myth: you need to formally "ask someone to be your mentor."

Truth: low-friction touches compound: a message about their book, a specific question in a forum, showing up consistently where practitioners talk.

Reasoning: he got replies from authors of romance novels with a two-line note. The barrier is effort and specificity, not status.

F · Why This Matters
💚 Judgment — knowing why one design wins in production — is the last thing AI can't hand you, and the first thing that gets you trusted with real systems. Borrowing it is the single biggest time-collapse available.

🎩 Learn the diff · ~8 min

The Top Hat: Broad Brim, Deep Crown

A · Core Explanation

Start with general learning: cover a large base of fundamentals for a broad understanding — then build a skyscraper on top. (In the video he draws a wide rectangle and deadpans: "and I lied. This is actually a top hat.") The brim is your foundation; the tall crown is becoming very good at a very few things. Spread thin across many topics and you become average at all of them — fine for a foundation, fatal for providing value nobody else can.

The two failure modes: not knowing what to specialize in, and topic-hopping. His own early years were trash-efficiency: jQuery on Monday, Angular on Tuesday, Google Apps Script on Wednesday — always stopping at the exact point where the new material met stuff he already knew. It felt like learning. It wasn't, because real learning is painful — it demands focus, cross-referencing, note-taking. If it's easy, you're just exercising what you already know.

The efficiency weapon is the diff principle: don't learn loops in Python and loops in TypeScript — learn loops once, then learn how the two languages differ. Translation is a low-value skill you pick up on demand via docs or AI. Go deep in one language until you hit generators, OOP, async, architecture — new territory — and everything else becomes a diff. Same rule for AI tools: being a surface user of five tools who's never written a skill or sub-agent isn't power. He picked Claude Code, went deep, and let the patterns transfer.

B · Step-by-Step
  1. Build the brim with non-overlapping fundamentals. Networking, databases, backend, desktop apps, low-level programming — a little time in each buys ~80% of the benefit.
  2. Pick ONE language and go end-to-end. A crash course in one sitting gives you an anchor and "the way of thinking" — his 4-hour React course example. You won't master it; you'll own a map.
  3. Push past the "I already know this" layer. Loops → functions → generators → OOP → web dev → async. Depth is where undiscovered things live.
  4. For the next language, learn only the diff. OOP in Python → OOP in JavaScript plus the delta. Never pay full price twice.
  5. Same for AI tools. Master one deeply; the rest are translations of patterns you already own.
Because difficulty is the signal that you're at the edge of your current ability. His test: if you walk away without concrete things you learned and it felt easy, you "exercised your brain on stuff you already know." Depth is only reachable through the painful band — that's why topic-hoppers plateau forever: they always turn back right before it starts to hurt.
C · Examples
✅ The obvious one

Loops twice, value zero

Learning loops in Python, then "learning" loops again in TypeScript with slightly different syntax — feels productive, teaches nothing new. His Java class version: he wrote the code in C and just "Java-ified" it. Translation should cost minutes, not weeks.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one

The "AI power user" who's never gone deep

Some experience with ChatGPT, some with Codex, some with Claude, Cursor, Anti-gravity… and never once written a Claude Code skill or a sub-agent — "basic stuff." Five shallow holes, no water. Breadth across tools is not power; depth in one tool is, because the patterns transfer.

D · Visual Analogy

🎩 The concept: the top hat

Wide base of fundamentals, tall mastery of a few things. The shape itself is the strategy — the brim makes the crown possible, and the crown makes you valuable.

⛏️ The analogy: one deep well vs. twenty holes

Dig twenty shallow holes and you get twenty dry pits at the exact depth where you always quit. Dig one well and you hit water — discovery — the layer below what everyone already knows. And once you own one deep well, piping water to a new site (a new language) is cheap.

🧠 Why it works: both images make the same hidden cost visible — the opportunity cost of the shallows. Every hour re-learning loops in a new syntax is an hour not spent below the waterline, where the genuinely new (and the genuinely valuable) concepts live.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: language count = value.

Truth: depth in one language + cheap diffs beats shallow multi-lingualism. His note to recruiters: a genuinely deep developer picks up your specific stack fast — that's the skill that matters.

Reasoning: deep experience teaches how to think in code; the syntax of your internal framework is a diff, learnable in days.

Myth: smooth = effective.

Truth: "Learning is actually painful… if it's super easy and you walk away with nothing concrete, you exercised your brain on stuff you already know."

Reasoning: the pain of focus, cross-referencing, and note-taking is the feeling of new neural territory. Topic-hoppers flee that feeling and call it "finding their passion."

F · Why This Matters
💚 Depth is where you discover things that aren't in any tutorial — which is precisely the zone where you become hard to replace, and where adaptation gets cheap: new tools are just diffs against your crown.

05
🚀 From localhost to life · ~8 min

Ship Something Real, Embarrassingly Early

A · Core Explanation

Start building your own software as soon as possible — and don't let the projects be throwaways. A calculator or to-do list is fine to learn a language's syntax, but then make something that takes real time, effort, and thought. His biggest early downfall: tinkering with something for a day, getting bored or stuck, moving on. The turn came when he committed to one ongoing project — a cryptocurrency from scratch, in Rust — figuring out wallets, encryption, transactions, and a CLI, one piece at a time. Within a week of starting, a company reached out wanting a Rust developer. Not even a crypto company. The project was provable evidence of complex work — and it likely made the difference between getting that senior role and not.

What building your own software actually buys you: real provable experience; a product mindset (what features do people want, need, and pay for — and what does each cost to build?); work with real data — location & latency, GDPR and privacy law, and live migrations that reshape data without corrupting it or alarming users; plus customers, marketing, sales, support, and security learned firsthand. His verdict: that bundle is 100× more valuable than prototypes running on your local computer.

Here's the AI-era twist: AI can generate basic CRUD apps — and increasingly complex ones. What it struggles with is piecing everything together into a valuable product with real users, maybe income, maybe an exit. That higher-level thinking is your job, and it only grows as a share of software engineering. So if he restarted: real domain, real branding, real customers — do everything a real company does, not a subdomain on a random host. Even directing agents to build strengthens the product muscle — as long as you are the one thinking.

B · Step-by-Step
  1. Pick a medium-difficulty project. Challenging every day, not crushing. (He'd now choose something easier than crypto-in-Rust.)
  2. Stay on ONE project over time. The tinker-abandon-repeat loop is the killer; continuity is where depth compounds.
  3. Ship it live. Domain, branding, deployment — treat it like a company from day one.
  4. Get real users and real feedback. Reviews, support, monetization, marketing, sales — these skills are becoming a larger part of software engineering.
  5. Face real-data problems head-on. Privacy/GDPR, latency and data location, live migrations that protect production data while the schema evolves.
Because production raises stakes that localhost can't simulate. With real data you answer: where is it legally allowed to live? How fast can it reach users? How do you reshape a live structure without corrupting a single row — while users notice nothing? A beginner presenting only local demos signals risk to a company, "not because you don't know how to code, but because you don't know how to code in the context of what that company is trying to do — build production software."
C · Examples
✅ The obvious one

The mobile-dev gauntlet

Want mobile development? Build an app, push it to the store, get reviews, acquire users, figure out monetization, advertising, marketing, and sales. Each of those is a "super, super valuable" skill — and together they're the shape of modern software engineering.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one

CurryCoin: the product flopped, the proof worked

His crypto project "sucked — nobody was going to buy CurryCoin," and he abandoned it within a week… for a senior engineering job it probably helped him land. The lesson: the artifact's market success matters far less than the provable complexity you demonstrated building it.

D · Visual Analogy

🏖️ The concept: localhost vs. production

Locally, everything obeys you: no traffic, no weather, no laws, no one to disappoint. Production has all four, simultaneously, forever.

🚗 The analogy: parking lot vs. the highway

The parking lot teaches pedals and cones. The highway teaches merging, judgment, and split-second risk — the skills that actually define driving. Nobody hires you to parallel-park between cones.

🧠 Why it works: both cases share the missing ingredient — other agents with their own intentions (users, data, attackers, regulators, weather). You can only learn to negotiate with them by being on the road. That's why a scrappy deployed app teaches more than a flawless local one.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: learning and building are sequential phases.

Truth: building is the learning — the hard curriculum (migrations, security, support, sales) only exists on the live side of the boundary.

Reasoning: he got a job offer within a week of starting his project — before it was anywhere near "finished." The market rewards trajectory and proof, not completion.

Myth: a working demo = working product.

Truth: to a hiring manager, all-local demos introduce high risk — you've never touched production data, real users, or real stakes.

Reasoning: "You might not be getting opportunities not because you don't know how to code, but because you don't know how to code in the context of what that company is trying to do."

F · Why This Matters
💚 A shipped, imperfect product teaches you more in a month than a year of polished prototypes — and it's the one artifact nobody can fake for you. In a world where AI generates apps, having actually shipped is the new proof of work.

🤝 Mindset · network · staying human · ~8 min

The Long Game: Expand Your Normal, Stay Human

A · Core Explanation

Stop being tied up about a role, title, or company. Six years ago the FAANG hysteria said: land at Google or you're a failure. Reality: thousands — if not millions — of companies can use your skills lucratively, and the roles go far beyond "software engineer." His own resume includes developer relations, technical workshops, marketing for technical products — and a final offer to make technical videos from his basement: $240k ($180k base). Those opportunities are "equally as good, if not better" — and pure SWE roles are becoming less common as AI-era variants emerge.

Break out of your network fast — because you don't know what you don't know. As a co-founder, he sat with engineers from high-paying backgrounds and discovered salary numbers that were "unheard of in rural Ohio, where my neighbor is a corn field." Seeing it made it claimable. Same with pricing: he wanted to charge $500, forced himself to ask $1,500 — and the client accepted without blinking. He nearly undersold himself; the work ended up totaling $28,000 over a couple of months, right out of high school. Today he wouldn't consider that $1,500 job — his comfort zone expanded until $1,500 looks "irrationally low." The breakthrough isn't leaving your comfort zone once; it's expanding it permanently. And discomfort is trainable: sales, negotiating, pushing back — he avoided them early and now calls them "one of the greatest unlocks." His secret: "I'm always uncomfortable."

The mindset layer: the single most valuable skill is resourcefulness — figuring anything out, alone, with AI, or through people. His university rant, condensed: the degree pipeline leaves people unprepared, so "AI is destroying the entry-level market" misses the deeper rot — graduates arrive without real-world value. If starting today he'd weigh the bachelor's honestly, but build real things through those four years regardless. And his final advice: as AI makes everything less human, go the opposite direction on the human parts. Automate with AI where you can — but in high-value moments (hiring, key clients, one-on-one support), bring your actual human self. In a world saturated with AI, human-to-human is the differentiator.

B · The Five Career Moves
  1. Loosen your grip on any single title or company. Adjacent roles (dev rel, technical content, solutions) often pay better with better fit.
  2. Get around people above you. Their normal becomes your normal — for salaries, rates, and ambition.
  3. Practice discomfort deliberately. Ask for the higher number. Collect rejections. The tolerance compounds.
  4. Build resourcefulness. The go-getter stance: strong goals, and figure it out — solo, with AI, or through people.
  5. Automate with AI, differentiate with humanity. The more synthetic everything gets, the more your real human presence is worth.
Because your bids are anchored to what you've seen. In rural Ohio, his anchor was the factory. Around ex-FAANG co-founders, the anchor moved — and his behavior followed within months. You can't negotiate what you can't imagine; imagination is a network effect.
C · Examples
✅ The obvious one

The corn-field salary shock

Co-founding with ex-big-tech engineers exposed him to compensation he literally didn't know was possible. Result: he pushed for higher pay in later roles — "I've seen it in other people. What's preventing me?" The information was free; the network was the gateway.

🔀 The counter-intuitive one

The client who didn't blink

He tripled his price through pure anxiety… and the client said "yeah, that's fine" without a moment's thought. Tripling wasn't brave — it was still too cheap. The market was more generous than his comfort zone. (Also: making technical videos from a basement outbid "real" engineering offers at $240k.)

D · Visual Analogy

🎯 The concept: expanding the comfort zone

A single brave act uses willpower. A shifted normal uses identity — the scary number becomes the boring number, and the boring number becomes "irrationally low."

🌡️ The analogy: the thermostat

You don't fight a cold room forever by lighting matches (willpower). You turn the dial, and the system automatically works to hold the new temperature. Exposure to higher-level people is dial-turning: after enough time at the new setpoint, maintaining it costs nothing.

🧠 Why it works: both pictures separate effort from setpoint. Matches and one-off brave asks burn out; a reset normal self-maintains. That's why "networking up" isn't schmoozing — it's infrastructure work on your own expectations.
E · ❌ What People Get Wrong

Myth: AI is the root problem for new grads.

Truth: there's some truth to it, but the deeper problem is the degree pipeline graduating people without real-world value — "the problem really isn't AI necessarily."

Reasoning: disruption is fast, institutions lag. What universities teach now isn't what gets you hired now — and the "AI will hurt your learning" line drilled into students may protect the institution, not the student.

Myth: full automation is the winning posture.

Truth: the winning posture is the opposite where it counts: automate the automatable, then go heavy on human-to-human where value is highest.

Reasoning: when AI floods resumes, cover letters, and outreach, the inhuman volume makes genuine human contact scarce — and scarcity is value. He personally handles his one-on-one support "above and beyond," deliberately refusing to hand it to AI.

F · Why This Matters
💚 Your network sets your ceiling; your humanity sets your floor. Both compound for decades — long after any specific framework, tool, or model is obsolete. Work as if the result 100% depends on your input… then strategize so the same input yields more.
