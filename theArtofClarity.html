What You'll Learn

This lesson unpacks six ideas Howard Marks uses to think clearly and write simply — ideas that apply just as much to a scientist, a founder, or a student as to an investor. You'll meet each idea through explanations, diagrams, real examples, analogies, and short interactive checks.

  • Why the only thing truly inside your control is the quality of your output — and why that should calm you down, not stress you out.
  • Why writing "the way you speak" beats jargon, even when jargon feels more impressive.

MODULE 01

Control What You Can

Howard Marks opens this conversation with a life philosophy disguised as an offhand remark: randomness governs an enormous share of what happens to you, and almost none of that is within your control. Markets move on forces no individual can command. Readers may or may not appear. A viral tweet, a chance meeting, a magazine left in a hotel room — these arrive uninvited and reorder outcomes regardless of merit. Yet buried inside that unsettling truth is a liberating one: the quality of what you produce is entirely yours to command. You decide how carefully you think, how many times you revise a paragraph, how honestly you test your own logic.

Marks's advice to his son distills this into a workable creed: set a simple goal — produce the highest-quality output you're capable of — and once you've genuinely met that bar, permit yourself to relax, because everything from that point forward belongs to chance, not to you. This reframes ambition itself. Instead of chasing results you can't guarantee, you chase process integrity, something you actually can guarantee. Notice this isn't fatalism — effort still matters enormously. Marks is simply insisting that effort be aimed exclusively at the one dial you can turn. This idea quietly organizes everything else in his approach: he wrote for two decades to an audience of roughly a hundred people with zero feedback, holding the same editorial standard he uses today for 300,000 subscribers, because his bar was never set by audience size — only by his own best thinking.

Obvious

Marks writes memos on no fixed schedule, but always at the same standard of rigor — regardless of whether he expects 100 readers or 300,000.

Counter-intuitive

Jack Dorsey claimed "success is never accidental," but Marks flatly disagrees: hardworking, able people are frequently unlucky, and unqualified people sometimes get lucky. Effort does not guarantee outcome.

Edge case

For years Marks mailed physical memos to ~100 clients and "never heard back" — an edge case where output quality had to be its own reward, since there was no feedback loop at all to validate it.

Controlling quality while ignoring outcomes is like a chef who can't control whether the restaurant is full tonight — but plates every dish exactly as well whether the room is empty or packed.
Common Misconception: "Hard work and skill guarantee success"
Reality: Marks argues the opposite. Randomness (or "luck") is unevenly distributed, and preparation doesn't always meet opportunity. Plenty of able, hardworking people never get lucky; plenty of unremarkable people do. This doesn't mean effort is pointless — it means effort should target the one variable luck can't touch: how good your work actually is.
Why it matters: This single reframing removes anxiety about things you cannot govern — outcomes, reception, timing — and channels all your energy into the one lever guaranteed to be yours: the rigor of your own process.

MODULE 02

Write Like You Speak

One of Marks's most quoted lines is his ambition to "write the way I speak" — no elevated diction, no showing off, just one person talking plainly to another. He traces the opposite instinct to a line attributed to Mark Twain: a profession is "a conspiracy against the laity." Jargon, in this view, isn't a neutral technical necessity; it's frequently a wall insecure professionals build to protect a monopoly on authority. If only initiates can decode the vocabulary, only initiates can be trusted — and that arrangement conveniently keeps the professionals in charge. Marks refuses to play that game. He uses contractions. He reaches for the common word over the impressive one whenever the common word carries the same meaning. He wants no "level difference" between himself and his reader: "I'm a person, you're a person, we're both people. I know about some stuff you don't, and I'd like to share it."

Crucially, this isn't a call to dumb down ideas — it's closer to the opposite. Oliver Wendell Holmes distinguished between "simplicity on this side of complexity," which is simply thin thinking, and "simplicity on the far side of complexity," the hard-won clarity that comes only after wrestling an idea to the ground. Marks's plain sentences carry decades of sophisticated reasoning; the simplicity is the finish line, not the starting point. This is why financial writing dense with jargon so often fails: readers scroll past in under two seconds, not because the ideas are too advanced, but because the words create friction nobody is paid enough attention to push through.

Obvious

Marks's memos are full of contractions ("I'm," "don't") and everyday words, deliberately avoiding financial jargon wherever plain language says the same thing.

Counter-intuitive

Being simple is actually harder and more prestigious than sounding sophisticated — echoing the idea that "simple" sits above "genius" on some scales of mastery.

Edge case

Marks notes he spends about 1.5 seconds looking at a typical jargon-dense white paper before scrolling past — an extreme case where technical correctness completely fails to communicate.

Jargon is like a locked clubhouse door — it makes insiders feel special, but it also locks out the very people whose minds you were trying to change.
Common Misconception: "Sophisticated ideas require sophisticated language"
Reality: Simplifying language is not the same as simplifying the idea. Marks argues that the truly hard, valuable skill is expressing a sophisticated idea in accessible words — "simplicity on the far side of complexity." Writers who hide behind jargon are often protecting insecurity, not preserving nuance.
Why it matters: Clarity determines whether ideas spread at all. A memo written for ~100 clients in 1990 reached 300,000+ subscribers largely because it read like a person talking, not a professional guarding a vocabulary.

Scenario: You're drafting a report for both experts and non-experts in your field.

MODULE 03

Glean, Pile, Polish

Marks never forces himself onto a writing schedule, yet a memo reliably appears every September and every January — proof that structure can emerge from unstructured freedom rather than external deadlines. His process begins long before any sentence is written: he calls it "gleaning." An article clipped here, a video watched there, an offhand thought jotted on a scrap of paper — each gets tossed onto a literal pile. He waits, sometimes for months, until "the pile is substantial in terms of content, not height." Only then does he write a rapid first draft.

The real transformation happens afterward, in what he lovingly calls "polishing" — his term for revision. He estimates doing this about five times, and each pass obeys the law of diminishing returns: the first revision might make the piece 20% better, the second another 10%, then 5%, then 2%, then 1%, until further changes stop paying off. Before publishing, he routes the draft past a small trusted circle he calls, borrowing from columnist William Safire, his "irregulars" — readers who catch everything from logical gaps to the wrong style of hyphen. Only after their fixes does he consider the piece finished. The lesson generalizes far beyond memo-writing: a strong first pass doesn't require genius, but disciplined, structured revision paired with outside eyes is what separates a mediocre draft from work people describe as life-changing.

Obvious

The "Getting Lucky" memo began with an airline-magazine article about luck that connected to ideas Marks had already been "piling up" for a while.

Counter-intuitive

More revision passes yield less improvement each time (20% → 10% → 5% → 2% → 1%) — meaning endless polishing eventually stops being worth the effort.

Edge case

Marks has zero fixed writing schedule, yet a memo appears every September and January without fail — structure emerging from freedom, not deadlines.

Writing a memo is like brewing tea: you don't rush it. You let scattered ideas steep together over weeks or months until the flavor is ready to pour.
Common Misconception: "Good writers produce brilliant first drafts"
Reality: Even a master distiller like Marks relies on roughly five structured revision passes plus outside editors ("irregulars") before a memo is ready. The first draft is just raw material — the polishing is where quality actually gets built.
Why it matters: This removes the myth that great writing requires innate genius. It's a repeatable process: gather over time, wait for enough material, draft quickly, then revise with diminishing (but real) returns, and get outside eyes before publishing.

Scenario: You've just finished a rapid first draft of an important piece.

MODULE 04

Think in Pictures

Marks thinks in pictures. Ask him to explain why a stimulus-driven market rally isn't permanent, and he doesn't reach for a regression equation — he describes a ball hovering above the ocean, suspended only by a jet of water shooting up from below. The waterspout is the Federal Reserve's stimulative policy; the ball is asset prices. The image makes an abstract claim — "this rally is conditional on continued intervention" — instantly graspable: of course the ball falls when the spout stops. He extends the same habit to markets generally, writing recurring memos connecting investing to poker, backgammon, and sports, because a bare discussion of "investing" rarely holds attention the way an unexpected analogy does.

Warren Buffett supplies Marks's favorite example of this compression: "it's only when the tide goes out that we find out who's been swimming naked" — fifteen words that replace an entire paragraph about how bull markets disguise the difference between skill and luck. But Marks is careful to flag the danger built into this same tool: he frequently prefaces his own analogies with warnings about reasoning by analogy, because an image that clarifies one relationship can quietly smuggle in false ones if pushed too far or trusted too completely. The discipline, then, isn't to avoid analogies — it's to use them deliberately, test whether they illuminate the specific mechanism you intend, and discard the parts of the picture that don't actually apply.

Obvious

The waterspout-and-ball image explains why a stimulus-driven rally isn't permanent: stop the spout, and the ball falls.

Counter-intuitive

Marks constantly warns against reasoning by analogy even while relying on it heavily — the same tool that clarifies can mislead if trusted too literally.

Edge case

Buffett's "swimming naked" line compresses an entire paragraph of survivorship-bias logic into fifteen words — an extreme case of analogy as compression.

Analogies are like flashlights in a dark room — they illuminate one path clearly, but if you assume the whole room looks like what's lit, you'll trip over furniture you never saw.
Common Misconception: "Analogies are just decorative flourishes"
Reality: For Marks, visual and analogical thinking is a core cognitive process for generating and organizing insight — not something added after the thinking is done. He says he "thinks in pictures and graphical relationships," and drew charts on whiteboards years before they became chapters in his books.
Why it matters: Analogies compress complex, multi-step logic into instantly graspable images, dramatically increasing how well an idea is remembered, understood, and shared.

MODULE 05

Reconcile Contradictions

Marks attributes much of his intellectual originality to an accident of timing: he studied practical, qualitative investing at Wharton from 1963–67, then moved directly into a University of Chicago MBA program built almost entirely on the brand-new, rigorously quantitative "efficient market" theory — the idea that markets already price in everything knowable, so true bargains can't exist. Two respected frameworks, learned back-to-back, flatly contradicted each other — and then a career in actual markets contradicted pieces of both. Marks calls on Yogi Berra to frame the puzzle: "In theory, there's no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is."

Rather than picking a side, Marks treated the contradiction itself as the interesting material. He argues explicitly that writing about ideas that already agree with each other accomplishes little — "reconciling two things that seem to be at odds that exist in the same marketplace, that's really challenging" — and that challenge is exactly where valuable insight lives. This reconciliation isn't a one-time event; it's the ongoing work of building what Marks calls a "creed," assembled from many sources over a long period and distilled into durable, principle-based beliefs about what actually works, what doesn't, and what you refuse to do. Notably, this kind of philosophy cannot be produced by a new graduate — a recent MBA has plenty of theory but no lived friction between theory and reality yet to reconcile. The creed only exists on the far side of years spent testing ideas against outcomes.

Obvious

Marks reconciles "you can find bargains through analysis" (Wharton) with "markets are efficient" (Chicago) into a nuanced view of where inefficiencies actually exist.

Counter-intuitive

Writing about ideas that agree with each other is boring; the valuable writing comes from wrestling with ideas that contradict — the harder the tension, the more valuable the resolution.

Edge case

A brand-new MBA has only theory (ABC), with no lived friction against reality (D, E) yet — an edge case showing philosophy requires time, not just knowledge, to form.

Reconciling two clashing frameworks is like a chemist mixing two reactive compounds — the useful reaction only happens at the point of tension, not by pouring one beaker into an empty one.
Common Misconception: "You should pick one school of thought and stay loyal to it"
Reality: Marks's most valuable, durable insights emerged from holding two contradictory frameworks simultaneously and forcing them into dialogue over decades — not from declaring allegiance to either Wharton's practical style or Chicago's efficient-market theory alone.
Why it matters: This tension is literally the origin of Marks's most famous risk framework and much of his best writing — an intellectual engine that keeps generating new insight even after 55 years in the business.

Scenario: You've learned two frameworks in school that seem to contradict each other in practice.

MODULE 06

Stand on Giants

Marks is emphatic that originality doesn't require ignoring everyone who came before you: "it's an unacceptable conceit to think that nobody who went before you knew it better." Rather than skimming widely and shallowly, he returns obsessively to a small set of thinkers — Nassim Taleb on randomness, John Kenneth Galbraith on the futility of forecasting, Charles Ellis on avoiding losers rather than chasing winners, Michael Milken on surviving rather than picking winners in bonds, and above all Peter Bernstein, author of Against the Gods, whom Marks calls "perhaps my greatest hero" in investment philosophy.

The relationship with Bernstein's work spans decades and offers a striking case study: Marks wrote "Risk" in 2006, believing he'd captured everything he knew; "Risk Revisited" in 2014, believing that was now complete; and then discovered, buried at the bottom of a paper pile, a Bernstein memo asking "Can We Reduce Risk to a Number?" that had already reached many of the same conclusions, brilliantly. Rather than treat this as redundant or embarrassing, Marks published "Risk Revisited Again," transparently marking newly incorporated Bernstein material in italics so readers could see exactly what was added and from whom — an act of intellectual honesty modeled on horse-racing's rule against "past-posting," or placing a bet after the race is already over. Later that year he spent an anxious Thanksgiving hunting through home archives for another half-remembered Bernstein quote. That sustained, humble, cumulative engagement with a few great minds — rather than a scroll through many mediocre ones — is precisely what let a 1990 idea still feel true, undiluted, in 2024.

Obvious

Marks repeatedly cites Buffett's "swimming naked" line because Buffett said in fifteen words what would take Marks a full paragraph.

Counter-intuitive

Discovering that Peter Bernstein had already reached similar conclusions decades earlier didn't embarrass Marks — he published "Risk Revisited Again," openly crediting Bernstein in italics.

Edge case

Marks spent an anxious Thanksgiving searching physical boxes of old papers for a half-remembered Bernstein quote — a case showing how much manual, unglamorous effort underlies "instant" wisdom.

Studying a few great thinkers deeply is like tending a single ancient tree instead of scattering a thousand seeds — sustained cultivation produces sturdier, longer-lasting fruit than a shallow forest.
Common Misconception: "Truly original thinkers avoid quoting others"
Reality: Marks explicitly says it's "an unacceptable conceit to think nobody who went before you knew it better." The best writers stand openly on the shoulders of giants — citing Buffett, Bernstein, Taleb, and others — rather than pretending to have invented every idea from scratch.
Why it matters: This explains why Marks's ideas about risk, cycles, and luck feel both original and timeless — they've been tested against decades of the best available thinking and his own repeated re-derivation, which is why a 1990 idea can still hold up today.

Still Confused? FAQ

Do I need to be a naturally gifted writer to write like Howard Marks?

Not according to Marks himself. He describes his process as mechanical and repeatable: collect scattered ideas over time (gleaning), wait until you have enough material, write a fast first draft, and then revise it about five times, with each pass yielding a smaller but real improvement. The one part that isn't purely mechanical is having a genuine point of view — a "creed" — which only comes from years of testing ideas against real experience. So the craft of writing clearly is learnable; the substance to write about takes time to earn.

Isn't Howard Marks risking his competitive edge by publishing his investment thinking for free?

Marks addresses this directly: he believes that knowing a concept and being able to implement it are two very different things. Most readers who agree with his ideas still won't be disciplined enough to apply them consistently, so sharing the "what" doesn't hand away the "how." He also argues the benefits of writing — clarifying his own thinking, building trust with clients, and simply enjoying the process — outweigh the modest competitive risk of transparency.

How long should I wait before I start writing my own memos or newsletters?

Marks is candid that early-career writing has limited value beyond practice: he didn't publish his first investment memo until 1990, after 21 years of work, because before that he simply didn't have enough hard-won perspective to share. His advice to a newer professional is blunt — write for practice or to communicate routine updates, but don't expect readers to treat a 28-year-old's opinions as seasoned wisdom. Substance takes time to accumulate; clarity of expression can be practiced immediately.

What if my industry genuinely requires technical jargon?

Marks isn't arguing that precise terms should never be used — he's arguing against jargon used as a barrier rather than a tool. His method is to introduce a technical idea once, explain it plainly, and then use consistent, accessible language around it, rather than assuming familiarity or using complexity to signal authority. The goal is always communication, not display.

Analogies feel like they oversimplify complex topics — aren't they dangerous?

Yes, and Marks says so explicitly: he frequently cautions against reasoning by analogy even as he uses it constantly. The danger isn't the analogy itself — it's forgetting where the analogy stops applying. His discipline is to use an image only as far as it accurately maps onto the real mechanism, and to state the limits of the comparison rather than letting the audience over-extend it.

How do I find my own "creed" or philosophy if I'm just starting out?

Marks suggests that a philosophy isn't handed down from school — it's built through years of testing what you learned against what actually happens, then keeping what holds up and rejecting what doesn't ("figure out what's real and what's just theory"). Start by noticing contradictions between what you were taught and what you observe in practice; those contradictions, wrestled with over time, are the raw material of a genuine point of view.

Next Steps

  • Read Howard Marks's book The Most Important Thing, structured around the same idea explored in Module 5 — that there is no single "most important thing," only a set of principles held in productive tension.
  • Read Peter Bernstein's Against the Gods for the deeper history of probability and risk that shaped Marks's own thinking (Module 6).
  • Start your own "pile" — a running file of clippings, quotes, and half-formed thoughts you revisit periodically, rather than trying to write from a blank page.
  • Practice the "read it aloud" test on your own writing: if it doesn't sound like something you'd say to a friend, revise it (Module 2).
  • Find your own small group of "irregulars" — two or three people willing to give you honest, detailed feedback before you publish anything.
  • Study a handful of shareholder letters (Buffett's Berkshire letters, Bezos's Amazon letters) specifically looking for compressed analogies and recurring themes, the way this lesson analyzed Marks's memos.
